MSIS · AME 5003 · Unit III · Session 32

BERT and masked language modelling

Session 31 left a sentence on the table: an encoder sees the whole sequence, so it is for understanding. This session is that model — and the training objective it is forced to use. Along the way it pays a debt from sessions 21 and 22.

An encoder block and a decoder block run the same three equations. They differ only in whether the future may be seen.

That single difference is why encoder-only models are for understanding, and decoder-only models are for generation. This session is the encoder-only model: BERT, Devlin et al., NAACL 2019.

§10.1

"The causal models of Chapter 8 are generative models, designed to easily generate the next token in a sequence. But the focus of bidirectional encoders is instead on computing contextualized representations of the input tokens."

"There are tasks for which it would be useful, when processing a token, to be able to peek at future tokens. This is especially true for sequence labeling tasks… such as the named entity tagging task…"

That is session 7. Whether Washington is a PERSON or a GPE may depend on words that come after it — Washington was born in 1732 against Washington raised the federal funds rate. The course set that problem up twenty-five sessions ago.

Sessions 11–14 trained language models by predicting the next word. That works precisely because the model cannot see ahead: the answer is hidden.

Remove the mask and the objective collapses. A bidirectional model asked to predict the word at position i can simply read position i. The loss goes to zero without the weights learning anything. The task has become copying, and copying teaches nothing.

Bidirectionality costs the training objective that Unit II ran on.

A replacement is needed. Masked language modelling is an answer to this problem, not a definition that happens to have a name.

Delete a word, ask the model to name it, keep the weights. CBOW predicted the centre word from an average of nearby context vectors, and out came one static vector per word type.

The same fill-in-the-blank shape, pointed at a bidirectional transformer, produces a different vector for every occurrence. Hold that; it returns when we reach contextual embeddings.

15% of tokens are chosen — and treated three ways

treatmentexample
80%replace with [MASK] lunch was [MASK]
10%replace with a random token, by unigram probabilitylunch was gasp
10%leave unchanged lunch was delicious

The loss is computed only at the chosen positions, and the target is always the original token — even where the input was left alone. The model is asked: what actually belonged here?

"Adding the [MASK] token creates a mismatch between pretraining and downstream finetuning or inference… the model might only predict tokens when it sees a [MASK], but we want the model to try to always predict the input token."

At fine-tuning time there are no [MASK] tokens at all. A model that has only ever produced a prediction where it sees one has learned a trick that does not fire when the model is used. The 10% random and 10% unchanged force it to hold an opinion about every position.

Teacher forcing trained the decoder on gold prefixes it would never have at inference; scheduled sampling was the patch. Here pretraining puts [MASK] in the input and fine-tuning never does; the 80/10/10 split is the patch.

Two sessions apart, same diagnosis: a model trained in a condition it never meets at deployment will underperform.
Question 1 of 2

Why does MLM replace only 80% of the chosen tokens with [MASK], rather than all of them?

Correct: the pretrain / fine-tune mismatch. Downstream, the input contains no [MASK]. If the model has only ever been asked to fill one, that is the only place it will bother to represent the token well.

The second is the answer nearly everyone gives, and it is not the reason — difficulty is a side-effect, not the design. The fourth confuses the 15% with the 80%: the softmax is already only at the chosen positions, and changing how those are corrupted does not change how many there are.

A second objective, trained at the same time

[CLS] lunch was delicious [SEP] I ordered seconds  [SEP]     ->  IsNext
[CLS] lunch was delicious [SEP] the stock market fell [SEP]  ->  NotNext

Given two sentences packed into one input, did the second actually follow the first? Half the time it did; half the time it is sampled at random from the corpus.

The final-layer vector at [CLS] carries the prediction — a classifier sits on that single position and outputs IsNext or NotNext.

That is what [CLS] is for: a dedicated place for a sequence-level decision, trained to summarise the pair. It earns a second job in session 33, when a different classifier sits on the same position for a downstream task.

The paper argued NSP helps on paraphrase, entailment and discourse coherence. SLP3 records what happened: "Some models, like the RoBERTa model, drop the next sentence prediction objective."

NSP belongs in the original model — it is why [CLS] and [SEP] are in the input. RoBERTa kept masked language modelling, dropped NSP, trained on more data for longer, and did better. The second objective was not load-bearing.

Session 18 checked idf against the Shakespeare features. Session 21 checked king−man+woman. Session 22 checked GloVe's pitch. Session 26 checked the GRU, in a paper two of its authors signed.

This is the fifth: an original claim, checked against what followed, coming back weaker than advertised. The habit is more durable than any of the five results.

Session 21's concrete failure

bank  ->  banks 0.744, banking 0.690, Bank 0.670, lender 0.634,
          banker 0.609, depositors 0.603

Every one is the financial sense. The river sense is not ranked low — it is absent, because there is one vector and the more frequent sense has taken all of it.

§10.3.1 opens on the same word

a bank can hold the investments
agriculture burgeons on the east bank

"The word 'bank' can mean: (1) a financial institution or (2) a sloping mound."

A bidirectional encoder maps (x1…xn) to (h1…hn), where each hi has been contextualised by the entire sequence, left and right.

bank in the first sentence is mixed with investments; in the second with east and agriculture. Word2Vec could not do this even in principle: its table has one row per word type, and a row cannot be two places at once.

One point where the contextual model has two

The course promised this fix twice and named the unit it would arrive in. A bidirectional encoder does not add a second row to Word2Vec's table — it stops having a table of word types at all, at the output.

The input still begins with a static embedding, looked up from 30,000 WordPiece rows. The stack of unmasked encoder blocks is what contextualises it.

SLP3's Figure 10.6 takes BERT embeddings of the word die across many contexts, projects them to two dimensions, and the points separate into three clusters: the English noun, the English verb, and the German article.

Three clusters, one spelling, no sense labels supplied. The model was never told die has three readings. The surrounding tokens were enough.
Question 2 of 2

What does a contextual embedding give that Word2Vec cannot?

Correct: a different vector for the same word in different contexts — the failure session 21 measured. Word2Vec has one row per word type; a bidirectional encoder produces one vector per occurrence.

The second is true and irrelevant, which makes it a good distractor: a 768-dimensional Word2Vec would still be one vector per word. The third describes WordPiece, which BERT does use, but subword tokenisation still gives one embedding per subword type. The fourth tells the model where a token sits, not which sense it is.

The embedding table alone is 21% of the model — 23 million parameters sitting in a lookup, before any block has run. The vocabulary is not a rounding error.

And session 31's finding holds here: in each block the feedforward layer is twice the attention, 4.7M against 2.4M, in a model whose fame is entirely about attention.

Session 3 taught WordPiece as "a close cousin of BPE", showed doomscrolling → ["doom", "##scroll", "##ing"], and promised BERT uses it.

Here is that vocabulary: 30,000 WordPiece tokens. The input still begins as a lookup in a static table. What has changed is what happens after the lookup.

Exercise

A bidirectional transformer trained to predict token i+1

First: why would training fail to teach it anything useful about language? Second: what does masked language modelling change, so that the same model now has something to learn?

Why it fails. The model can see the token it is asked to predict — position i's query may attend to position i+1's key and value, which is the answer. The loss goes to zero by copying, and copying requires no knowledge of English. The causal mask was what made next-word prediction a question with a hidden answer.

What MLM changes. The answer is hidden again, but not by masking the future — by corrupting the present. A subset of positions is replaced, and the model must recover the originals from the surviving context on both sides. The targets are no longer in the input in plain sight.

The 80/10/10 split is a refinement of the same idea: even after the answer is hidden, the cue that it was hidden must not become the only place the model bothers to think, because that cue is absent when the model is used.

That is a pretrained bidirectional encoder: 12 unmasked blocks, a 30,000-token vocabulary, trained to fill in blanks from both sides, producing a different vector for bank in each of two sentences.

That is what BERT is. Session 33 is what it is for: the expensive part is already done, and what remains is to sit a small head on top and train it on a labelled task — and then ask whether a smaller copy can be made to behave like this one.

Summary

Bidirectionality makes next-word prediction trivial, so masked language modelling replaces it: 15% chosen, 80/10/10, predicted from both sides — and the split exists because [MASK] is absent downstream, the same mismatch as teacher forcing. NSP puts a sentence-pair decision on [CLS]; RoBERTa dropped it and did better.

The output is a contextual embedding: two vectors for bank where sessions 21 and 22 could only have one. BERT-base is about 108M parameters — 21% of them the embedding table, and twice as much feedforward as attention in every block.
