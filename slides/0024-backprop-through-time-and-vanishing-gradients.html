MSIS · AME 5003 · Unit III · Session 24

Training an RNN: backprop through time, and the vanishing gradient

Session 23 described what the architecture allows but did not test whether training delivers it. It does not, at least not by default, and that failure is where the next architecture comes from.

The claim session 23 left open

"The architecture allows a decision to depend on information from hundreds of words in the past."

The answer is no, not by default — and the reason is not a defect in any implementation. It follows from the architecture session 23 described.

U, W and V are trained as session 20's neuron was: choose a loss, compute each weight's contribution, adjust against the gradient, repeat.

None of that is new. What is new is the distance the error signal must travel.

In sequence classification there is a single loss, computed after the last word. To improve, the network must work out how much the weights used at step 1 contributed to an error made at step n.

Session 23's unrolled figure supplies the answer: the unrolled network is an ordinary feedforward network, so we differentiate backward along every arrow in it.

Two passes

  • Forward — run the sequence, computing ht and yt, accumulating the loss and saving every hidden state
  • Backward — walk the sequence in reverse, computing the gradient at each step and passing an error term back to the step before

That is all backpropagation through time means: ordinary backpropagation, applied to the unrolled picture. We do not derive it — the mechanism matters for one consequence.

To reach step 1, the gradient must pass through every step in between.

Reason 1 — the hidden state has two roles

At every step ht must supply what the current decision needs and carry forward what a future decision will need. One vector, no machinery separating them.

Whatever the current step finds useful will overwrite what a later step depends on — not because of any numerical problem, but because there is only one place to store both.

Reason 2 — the gradient is multiplied once per step

ht feeds ht+1, which feeds ht+2. The backward pass therefore applies repeated multiplications, as many as the sequence is long.

Multiplying any quantity by the same factor a hundred times sends it to one of two limits: zero or infinity. SLP3's name for the first is the vanishing gradients problem.

SLP3's example, chosen carefully

The flights the airline was canceling were full.

Predicting was after airline is easy — the singular noun is one word away. Predicting were is hard.

Why were is hard, in two ways

  • The word that determines it, the plural flights, sits many steps back, and everything between has been writing into the same hidden state
  • The singular airline sits much closer and favours the wrong answer — the nearby incorrect cue is stronger than the distant correct one

That distance is what reason 2's gradient must survive and what reason 1's overloaded hidden state must preserve. A single sentence exhibits both failures.

Session 23's toy recurrence, unchanged

ht=Uht−1+Wxtwith U=0.5, W=1, h0=0

Differentiating one step gives ∂ht/∂ht−1=U

∂hn∂h1=Un−1

U=0.5 — already below 1.

The example session 23 computed forward by hand, with values chosen only for ease of arithmetic, was a vanishing case throughout. This is session 23's arithmetic, read backwards.

Twenty words back, 0.0000019 is not a small learning signal but effectively none. A fifty-word sentence is not a long document; it is a single paragraph.

Change one value — U=1.5 instead of 0.5 — and the same recurrence runs the opposite way: 38 after ten steps, four hundred million after fifty.

The same architecture and the same formula produce the opposite failure. Below 1 it vanishes; above 1 it grows without bound. Only a factor of exactly 1 is stable, and nothing in training keeps it there.

Exercise

Same toy, U=0.5

What is ∂h5/∂h1 — the gradient reaching back four steps? And what would U have to be for this to grow as you reach further back?

∂h5/∂h1=U4=0.54=0.0625. Four steps back, the signal is already down to six per cent.

For it to grow, |U|>1 — the exploding case in the table's right-hand column. Note that nothing in training keeps U near 1; it is a learned weight like any other. Vanishing is the more common outcome, not the only one.

The example has no activation function, which is why the arithmetic stayed inspectable. A real RNN squashes every step with tanh⁡, adding one more factor to every backward step: tanh′⁡(z)=1−tanh2⁡(z), which is at most 1 and usually well below it.

That factor makes vanishing worse and never better. It cannot recover a shrinking gradient, and it is not the cause of the exploding case.

Session 23's chain, watched backwards

The shared weights that made the architecture work are the same shared weights that make one number get multiplied by itself n times.

Question 1 of 2

Why does the gradient reaching an early timestep shrink toward zero in a long sequence?

Correct: repeated multiplication by the same U, once per step.

The first is a real contributing factor and a good instinct — tanh′⁡ is at most 1, so it makes vanishing worse — but it is not the mechanism, and it cannot explain the exploding case. The second is false instructively: shared weights mean the parameter count does not grow with sequence length, which was session 23's selling point. The advantage and the defect are the same design decision.

SLP3's framing is not about gradients at all. It is about context management: architectures that "learn to forget information that is no longer needed and to remember information required for decisions still to come".

That description corresponds exactly to reason 1.

If the problem is one vector serving two purposes, the solution is to give the two purposes separate machinery: a place to hold context that is not where the current decision is computed, plus learned control over what enters and leaves it.

That is the LSTM, and it is the subject of session 25.

The same design also addresses reason 2, as a consequence rather than a goal: the cell state gives the gradient a path where information is added from step to step rather than multiplied through a weight matrix, and a sum does not decay as a repeated product does.

This is the standard explanation but not SLP3's framing, and should not be attributed to the book. Two reasons, two solutions, one architecture.
Question 2 of 2

In SLP3's own framing, what does an LSTM change about a plain RNN?

Correct: it gives the two jobs separate machinery, with learned control over what is remembered and forgotten.

That is reason 1's fix, and it is why the LSTM looks the way it does. The gradient benefit is real but secondary — remember the LSTM as "a trick to stop gradients vanishing" and its actual design will look arbitrary next session. The third describes a bidirectional RNN, a different idea that does not solve this; the fourth is a real workaround that works by giving up the long-range dependency.

Three sessions, one argument

SessionWhat it established
23The architecture — hidden state, shared weights, one network for any sequence length. It allows long-range memory.
24The limit — one vector doing two jobs, and a gradient multiplied by the same matrix once per step. It does not get long-range memory, not by default.
25The response — separate the two jobs, learn what to keep. The LSTM exists because of this session, not because of session 23.

These two sessions were not parts one and two of a treatment of RNNs. The first described the architecture accurately; the second measured it accurately; and the gap between them is where the next architecture comes from.

The same pattern has appeared throughout: session 18's idf, session 19's cosine, session 21's analogy, session 22's comparison. Each time the claim is checked against the arithmetic and what survives is kept.

Summary

Backpropagation through time is ordinary backpropagation on the unrolled network, and it fails in two ways at once: one hidden state serving both the current decision and later ones, and a gradient multiplied by U once per step, so ∂hn/∂h1=Un−1 collapses below 1 and explodes above it.

Session 23's own U=0.5 was already a vanishing case. The fix is to stop requiring one vector to serve both purposes — an explicit context layer with learned control. That is the LSTM.
