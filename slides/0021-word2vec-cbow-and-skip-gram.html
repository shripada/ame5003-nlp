Word2Vec: CBOW and Skip-gram

Last session ended with a sentence that would have been unreadable an hour earlier. This session builds the model it describes, and shows that the prediction task is not what the model is for.

The sentence from session 20

"CBOW averages the context word vectors, multiplies by an output matrix, applies softmax, and does gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss."

We build the model it describes, and then establish that the prediction it makes is discarded.

J. R. Firth, 1957

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Words occurring in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. That is the entire premise, and every embedding method in this course depends on it.

Session 19's table, again

piedatacomputer
cherry 4428 2
digital 5 16831670
information5 39823325

cos(cherry, information) = 0.018 against cos(digital, information) = 0.996. No information about meaning was supplied.

Those vectors have three dimensions. With three axes, two words that both load on data and computer have very little room to differ — hence 0.996.

The 300-dimensional vectors built today spread similarity over far more room and do not produce values of that kind. A cosine of 0.996 in one representation and in another are not equivalent claims. We measure the difference later in this session.

Every method in Unit II so far has counted the company a word keeps. Word2Vec predicts it instead.

Rather than counting the context, we predict it — and keep whatever the model had to learn in order to predict well.

Delete a word, ask for it back

a  great  _____  about  hope

Nobody needs a system that fills in blanks whose answers are already known. The task is not the reason for building the model.

The reason is what a model must become to do it well. To predict film from that context, it has to arrange its numbers so words in similar company have similar numbers — the only structure in the data that helps.

The prediction is a pretext; the weights are the product.

And no labelling is required

Session 16 needed 2,000 reviews somebody had read and tagged. Word2Vec needs none — the label is the word that was already there.

Delete a word, ask for it back, and check the answer. Every sentence ever written is training data, and that supply, rather than any sophistication in the method, is why the approach became dominant.

CBOW — Continuous Bag of Words, window ±2

a  great  [film]  about  hope
|______|          |__________|
 context            context        target = film

"Bag of words" for the usual reason: the four context words enter as an unordered set, because they are about to be averaged.

Session 20's shapes, unchanged

what it isshapenote
Eembedding matrix10000 × 300one row per word in the vocabulary
xaveraged context300mean of a few rows of E
W′output matrix10000 × 300one row per word again, on the way out
zscores10000z=W′x, one score per vocabulary word
pprobabilities10000p=softmax(z)

Four steps, all of them taught last session

  • Look up. Pull the four rows of E for a, great, about, hope
  • Average. Add and divide by four — one vector x of 300 numbers
  • One layer, then softmax. z=W′x scores every vocabulary word; softmax makes 10,000 probabilities
  • Loss, then descend. −log⁡ of the probability given to film, then w←w−η⋅gradient

All of them — including the four rows of E we just looked up. Those rows are weights, so gradient descent adjusts them like anything else.

That is the whole training loop. The window slides one word along and repeats. After a few billion repetitions, words used in similar company point in similar directions. No definition of any word was ever supplied.

Skip-gram reverses the arrows

Same matrices, same softmax, same loss — only the direction of the arrows differs.

CBOW makes one training example out of that position. Skip-gram makes four, one per context word.

Which to use

  • Skip-gram is the usual choice. Four examples per window instead of one, so the same corpus gives more signal — and it matters most for rare words, which CBOW barely sees
  • The cost is time: four predictions per position rather than one
  • CBOW is faster and slightly better on frequent words, because averaging four context vectors reduces noise

Both are in the same 2013 paper and both ship in every implementation. Absent a specific reason, use Skip-gram.

Each word has two vectors, and both are trained

vectorwrittenused when
word w's input vector vw=row w of Ew appears as context
word w's output vector uw=row w of W′w is the thing being predicted

The score the model gives word w is uw⋅x — its output vector dotted with the averaged context.

Session 20's quiz said: when training stops, "you throw away the rest of the network and keep that matrix". This is the rest of the network. W′ is discarded.

The word vectors in general use, including every downloadable Word2Vec file, are the rows of E. Some implementations offer to average the two sets; this is rarely done.

Everything so far is correct and none of it is practical. The difficulty is the softmax denominator.

Turning scores into probabilities means dividing by a sum over the whole vocabulary — so one training example needs a score for every word you know.

Realistic figures

vocabulary1,000,000 words
corpus1,000,000,000 tokens (small, by 2013 standards)
exponentials per example106
training examples109 (CBOW; Skip-gram makes about ten times more)
per pass over the corpus1015 exponentials

At a billion exponentials per second — a generous rate — about 11.6 days for one pass. Mikolov's paper trained on 100 billion words.

The cost is not in producing the answer but in normalising it.

We want information about one word, and softmax makes us score a million to get it.

Not a faster softmax — a different question

real pair:        (film, about)    <- actually appeared together  -> say YES
invented pairs:   (film, kubernetes)                              -> say NO
                  (film, wheat)                                   -> say NO
                  (film, thirteen)                                -> say NO
                  ...  k of them, drawn at random

One very large decision becomes a small number of binary ones.

A single dot product, mapped into a probability

P(this pair is real)=σ(u⋅v)where σ(z)=11+e−z

Push σ(u⋅v) towards 1 for the real pair, and σ(−u⋅v) towards 1 for each invented one.

Which is to say: pull the true pair's vectors together and push the fake pairs' apart. Typical k is 5 to 20 for a small corpus, 2 to 5 for a large one.

Session 20 showed the sigmoid and said it would appear here — and that it would not be a hidden activation. Here it is: one dot product turned into one probability.

Word2Vec still has no hidden non-linearity anywhere. It is a shallow model, and that is what makes it fast enough to be useful.

The same units as before

arithmetic per exampleper pass over 10⁹ tokens at 10⁹ ops/sec
full softmax10⁶ exponentials10¹⁵ 11.6 days
negative sampling, k=56 sigmoids6 × 10⁹ 6 seconds

Roughly 167,000 times less arithmetic, and the resulting vectors are as good or better. This is why Word2Vec had the influence it did.

Exercise

Window ±2, target on

the cat sat on the mat. What exactly is the input to CBOW, and what is the target? How many training examples does Skip-gram get from the same position?

Two words each side gives the context cat, sat, the, mat.

CBOW: input is the average of those four rows of E — one 300-number vector. Target is on. One training example.

Skip-gram: input is on's row, and there are four separate examples — (on → cat), (on → sat), (on → the), (on → mat). Same position, four times the signal.

The context is a set: CBOW would give an identical input for sat cat on mat the, because averaging destroys order. Same trade as session 17.

Question 1 of 2

Word2Vec is trained to predict a missing word from its context. What do we actually keep when training finishes?

Correct: the embedding matrix.

The prediction task is a pretext. We set it because a model can only do it well by arranging its weights so that words used in similar company end up with similar numbers — and those weights are what we came for.

W' is thrown away with the rest of the network. And Word2Vec is not a language model in session 11's sense: it has no notion of word order inside the window, so it cannot give you the probability of a sentence.

Question 2 of 2

What problem does negative sampling solve, and how?

Correct: it replaces the vocabulary-wide softmax with a handful of binary decisions.

The cost was never in producing the answer — it was in normalising. Six sigmoids instead of a million exponentials, about 167,000 times less arithmetic.

The third option describes something real but different: the 3/4-power sampling distribution is how negatives are drawn, not what the technique is for.

"The film was good" and "the film was bad" occur in the same positions in the same kinds of sentence. Antonyms appear in nearly identical company — which is what makes them antonyms rather than unrelated words.

These vectors encode distribution, not meaning. Usually close enough to be useful, occasionally exactly wrong. It is why averaged word vectors turn out to be a mediocre sentiment feature, which lab 8 measures.

Both halves are true

The result is genuine. Second place out of 300,000 is queen, then monarch, princess, crown_prince. Nobody arranged that neighbourhood.

The usual description overstates it. The vector does not equal queen. It lands near king, inside a royal neighbourhood, and queen is the closest non-input. A weaker claim than arithmetic, and a more informative one.

Session 19 said embedding cosines "really do go down to −1"

4,000 random words, ~16 million pairs:

mean cosine            +0.066
median                 +0.058
1st percentile         -0.079
99th percentile        +0.291
minimum found          -0.270
fraction negative       17.4%

The first half of the claim was right — negative cosines do occur, so count vectors' lower bound of 0 is genuinely gone. The second half was not.

Trained embeddings do not spread over the whole sphere. They sit in a fairly narrow cone, and almost every pair of words is slightly positively aligned.

The formula's range is −1 to 1; the data's range is roughly −0.3 to 1. With count vectors a cosine of 0.3 was weak; here it is above the 99th percentile. A cosine is interpretable only against the distribution the representation actually produces.

And each word gets exactly one vector, whatever it means

bank  ->  banks 0.744, banking 0.690, Bank 0.670, lender 0.634,
          banker 0.609, depositors 0.603

Every one is the financial sense. The river sense is not ranked lower but absent — the more frequent sense has taken the whole vector. Same for bat: batsman, batting, wicket_keeper, nothing about the animal.

One word, one vector, no matter how many things the word means.

Fixing that is what contextual embeddings do, and that is Unit III.

Session 20 asked whether these beat lab 7's 0.823

Not yet established, and the answer is not obvious. Lab 8 measures it.

There is a specific reason for doubt, already visible: good and bad have a cosine of 0.719, so averaging a review's word vectors makes positive and negative words partly cancel — which TF-IDF does not do, because it keeps them as separate features. A better representation of words is not automatically a better representation of documents.

Summary

Word2Vec sets up a task nobody wants solved and keeps the weights the model needed to do it, so it trains on raw text with no labelling. CBOW averages the context and predicts the centre; Skip-gram reverses the arrows for more examples and better rare words. Negative sampling replaces the vocabulary-wide softmax with a few yes/no decisions.

What comes out captures which words behave alike, which is also why bad is the second-nearest word to good.
