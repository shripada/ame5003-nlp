Tokenization

How a computer splits text into words — and why that turns out to be a hard problem.

What tokenization is

  • Break text into pieces, called tokens
  • Sounds easy: split on spaces
  • "the cat sat" → ["the", "cat", "sat"]

Works for this sentence. Stops working almost immediately.

Why splitting on spaces fails

"Dr. O'Neill isn't in — he's at St. Mary's,
 re: the U.S.A. deal."

Splitting on spaces produces problems

  • Mary's, — the comma is not part of the name
  • isn't is really two words: is and not
  • Dr. and U.S.A. contain full stops — split on those and you break these

Every rule you add to fix one case breaks another.

Careful rules do win in the end — but they only settle the punctuation.

Rule-based tokenization

  • The Penn Treebank tokenizer — a large set of regular expressions, written by hand
  • Years of difficult examples, already handled
  • We do not write it; we call it.

NLTK, on the same sentence

word_tokenize("Dr. O'Neill isn't in — he's at "
              "St. Mary's, re: the U.S.A. deal.")

['Dr.', "O'Neill", 'is', "n't", 'in', '—', 'he',
 "'s", 'at', 'St.', 'Mary', "'s", ',', 're', ':',
 'the', 'U.S.A.', 'deal', '.']

Checking the three problems

  • Dr. and U.S.A. kept their full stops — it knows abbreviations
  • The final . came off as its own token — that one is punctuation
  • isn't → is + n't — negation stays visible
  • Mary's → Mary + 's, comma separate

Use the library

For English text, a good rule-based tokenizer is a solved problem. There is no reason to write one by hand.

But it stops being enough when we reach Unit III — and the reason has nothing to do with punctuation.

What we mean by a “model”

  • Sessions 1–2: rules written by hand
  • A model is the opposite — it learns patterns from examples (training)
  • It keeps a handful of numbers per word it knows

That's all “a model” needs to mean today. We build one properly from session 20.

Every model needs a vocabulary

  • A fixed list of every token the model knows
  • Built once, from training text — then fixed forever
  • Fixed because the model keeps a row of numbers per token

Ten thousand words means ten thousand rows.

Language keeps inventing new words

training:  vocabulary = every word seen in training

later:     model sees "doomscrolling"
           -> not in the list -> <UNK>

Every unseen word becomes the same token. They all look identical to the model.

doomscrolling contains scroll — a word the model knows well.

The meaning is sitting right there in the letters. Splitting on spaces throws that away.

Exercise

The constraint

The word list is fixed, language keeps producing new words, and <UNK> is not available. What can be changed?

Make the pieces smaller than words.

Word parts can spell any new word out of parts already in the list. But single letters carry no meaning on their own.

Big pieces carry meaning but miss new words. Small pieces cover everything but carry little meaning. Sit in the middle.

Byte-Pair Encoding

  • Start with a list of single characters
  • Count every pair of neighbouring symbols
  • Join the most frequent pair. Add it to the list.
  • Repeat k times

k is a parameter we choose, and it determines the size of the final list.

A small set of words

5  l o w _
2  l o w e s t _
6  n e w e r _
3  w i d e r _
2  n e w _

list:  _, d, e, i, l, n, o, r, s, t, w

Count on the left. _ marks the end of a word.

Merge 1: count the pairs

e r  in newer (6) + wider (3) = 9   <- most frequent

merge 1:  e + r  ->  er

5  l o w _
2  l o w e s t _
6  n e w er _
3  w i d er _
2  n e w _

Repeat

merge 2:  er + _  ->  er_
merge 3:  n + e   ->  ne
merge 4:  ne + w  ->  new
merge 5:  l + o   ->  lo
merge 6:  lo + w  ->  low

5  low _
2  low e s t _
6  new er_
3  w i d er_
2  new _

In three of these six steps, two pairs had the same count. A real implementation follows a fixed rule for choosing, so that every run gives the same result. Try it yourself, merge by merge →

Look at what it learned

The symbol er_ — in English, -er means “more of something.” A real piece of grammar.

Nobody told the algorithm any of this. It only counted pairs.

Take the word lower — not in the training list

BPE splits it into low + er_. Both pieces are already known.

No unknown words — any word can be spelled from pieces you already have.

Unknown characters are a different matter

  • A script or emoji that never appeared in training is not in the list either — so <UNK> comes back
  • GPT models run BPE over bytes, not characters
  • Only 256 possible bytes — all in the list from the start
Question 1 of 2

What happens if you increase k, the number of merges?

Correct: bigger list, fewer tokens.

Each merge adds one symbol to the list, and replaces two symbols with one. Real systems land around 30,000–50,000 tokens — BERT 30,522, GPT-2 50,257.

Question 2 of 2

Why does BPE mark the end of each word with _?

Correct: so merges cannot run past the end of one word.

Without the marker, the last letter of one word and the first letter of the next word would count as a pair, like any other pair. You would get useless symbols that cross from one word into the next.

BERT’s tokenizer

"doomscrolling"  ->  ["doom", "##scroll", "##ing"]

## means “this joins to the piece before it” — same idea as our _, opposite end.

Summary

Tokenization is not simply a matter of splitting strings. It determines the units a model is able to represent.

Choose whole words, and new words become unknown. Choose word parts, and the model can handle anything.
