What NLP is, and your first tool

Why language is hard for computers — and how to pull facts out of text with a pattern.

What natural language processing is

  • Your phone predicted the next word as you typed
  • Your email put a message in the spam folder
  • A search engine found a page without your exact words
  • Something translated a sentence for you
  • A chatbot gave you a sensible answer

By the end of this course, you will have built versions of most of these.

What is NLP?

  • NLP stands for Natural Language Processing
  • It means teaching computers to work with human language — the words people speak and write
  • Not just English. Any language.

Every example on the last slide was NLP.

Terms you will encounter

  • AI — the big goal: make a computer do something that used to need a person
  • Machine learning — one way to do that: show the computer examples, instead of writing rules yourself
  • Deep learning — one kind of machine learning: networks with many layers

Each one is inside the one before it — like nested circles.

Where NLP fits

NLP is not one more item on that list. NLP is a problem: language.

People have solved it three ways — rules, then machine learning, then deep learning. This course follows that order, one unit at a time.

Where NLP sits

The part of NLP outside AI is where this course starts. A regular expression is NLP and learns nothing.

Computers are good at deterministic tasks — apps, algorithms, calculations.

Language is not deterministic.

Read this sentence

“The old man the boat.”

The sentence is grammatical

  • Here man is a verb. It means “to work on” or “to operate”
  • So: the old people operate the boat
  • We read “old man” as one noun phrase, then revise when we reached “the boat”

A computer has the same problem — and no instinct to help it.

Who has the telescope?

“I saw a man on a hill with a telescope.”

Language is ambiguous.

Almost every technique in this course is a way of dealing with it.

We start at the bottom

Your first tool: regular expressions

Before any of the more sophisticated methods, we need a way to extract facts from raw text.

A regex is a small language for describing shape

  • A regular expression is a pattern, and the computer finds every match
  • The oldest tool in NLP — still one of the most used
  • Pulling structured facts out of unstructured text is called information extraction

For dates, phone numbers and ID numbers, this is often the complete answer. No machine learning needed.

In this course we use regex, not re

pip install regex

Same functions and same patterns as re. Python's built-in re does not handle Indian scripts correctly. We see why in a minute.

The most common regex bug

+ means “one or more”. To match a literal plus sign, write \+.

This is called escaping. Everybody forgets it at least once.

re.findall(r"\w+", …) on Kannada

text = "ಕನ್ನಡ ಭಾಷೆ"   # "Kannada language" — two words

re — wrong

['ಕನ', 'ನಡ', 'ಭ', 'ಷ']
# four "words"

regex — right

['ಕನ್ನಡ', 'ಭಾಷೆ']
# two words

Inside “ಕನ್ನಡ” — five characters, one special

CharCodepointRole
ಕU+0C95letter
ನU+0CA8letter
್U+0CCDcombining mark — the virama
ನU+0CA8letter
ಡU+0CA1letter

re's \w accepts letters and digits but rejects combining marks — so it ends the word at the virama.

Why this matters for the rest of the course

In Unit II we count words to build bag of words and TF-IDF.

If the word counts are doubled before we start, every number after that is wrong — and nothing crashes to tell you.

Exercise

Write a pattern

A PIN code: exactly six digits.

A roll number like MSIS2026001: four letters, then seven digits.

PIN code: \d{6}

Roll number: [A-Z]{4}\d{7}

\w{4}\d{7} is not tight enough — \w also accepts digits and underscore.

Question 1 of 2

What does [^aeiou] match?

Correct: any single character that is not a vowel.

^ inside brackets means not. Outside brackets it means start of line. Same symbol, two jobs.

Question 2 of 2

Which pattern matches both colour and color?

Correct: colou?r.

? applies to the character right before it — u — making it optional. A quantifier attaches to the one thing just before it.

Summary

Language is ambiguous, and a computer has no intuition for resolving it.

But when the thing we want has a fixed shape — a date, a phone number, an identifier — a regular expression will find it, and a trained model is unlikely to do better.
