Question answering and text summarisation

Two syllabus clauses in one hour. The first half points Unit I's retriever at the generator Unit III built. The second is a taxonomy, and one architecture the course already has.

Question answering and summarisation are listed separately in the syllabus; the calendar gives them one sitting. Neither half gets the treatment an application deserves.

We say what each one is, what it cannot do, and which pieces of the course already built it. QA has SLP3 chapter 11 behind it; summarisation has no SLP3 chapter in this release, so it gets a taxonomy and one honest note about the tooling.

§11.3 names the flaw in the terms Unit I left it

"They work only if there is exact overlap of words between the query and document… an issue called the vocabulary mismatch problem."

Session 8's matrix matched words exactly. Session 10 weighted the 1s and still needed the term to appear. The asker and the writer have to choose the same words — and lab 4's inverted index fails the same way, because there is no postings list to merge.

"Instead of (sparse) word-count vectors, using (dense) embeddings… implemented in modern times via encoders like BERT."

Session 21 already placed synonyms near each other in a static space; session 32 built contextual embeddings. §11.3 joins those facts to Unit I: the retriever a student built in lab 4 fails when asker and writer choose different words.

Sequence-pair classification, pointed at retrieval

z=BERT(q;[SEP];d)[CLS]
score(q,d)=softmax(U(z))(11.17)

That is (10.11) with a different letter for the matrix. In session 33 the pair task asked whether B follows from A. Here the pair is a query and a document, and the label is relevance.

SLP3 notes this is too expensive to run over a whole collection per query, so it is used as a reranker; a cheaper bi-encoder encodes documents once and scores by a dot product. We take the first, because it is a head this course has already trained.

Exercise

The only document that answers the query shares no content words

Query: How can I treat a headache? · Document: Ibuprofen reduces pain in the temples.

Why does session 10's TF-IDF ranking return nothing? What changes if the retriever encodes both with BERT?

After session 4's stop words, the query is treat headache and the document is Ibuprofen reduces pain temples. No term occurs in both, so every tf-idf weight in the overlap is zero and the cosine is zero. Session 8's matrix is the same failure in binary; lab 4's postings for treat and headache do not include this document.

A dense retriever does not need the words to match. treat sits near reduces, headache near pain, and the document comes back.

The instructive wrong answer. Expanding the query by hand with synonyms would also retrieve it, and that is how sparse IR used to paper over the problem. It does not scale, and it retrieves documents matching a synonym in the wrong sense. Dense retrieval moves the synonymy into the representation rather than the query string.

A pretrained model that has seen the location of the Louvre can generate Paris from the question as a prefix. Three things go wrong, and they are why the rest of the chapter exists.

Models hallucinate — generate a response not faithful to the facts of the world. Dahl et al. (2024) found models hallucinating from 69% to 88% of the time on legal-domain questions.

They do this even when the fact is stored in the parameters and the feedforward layers fail to recall it. And they are not well calibrated, so a wrong answer often arrives with complete certainty.

Medical records, internal documents, personal email, legal discovery — none of it was in the web crawl the model was pretrained on.

A larger model does not help. Scale does not insert a document the training set never contained.

The weights are frozen at the training date. Session 21 showed the static version: Word2Vec's 2013 vectors have no entry for COVID, selfie, blockchain or emoji. Session 22 repeated it for GloVe.

Retrieval is the answer that does not require retraining. The model's weights stay where they were; the documents are current.

§11.4

"We use IR techniques to retrieve documents from some specified store of documents that are likely to have useful information. Then we use a large language model to generate an answer conditioned on these documents in addition to the original query."

A retriever and a generator. Sparse retrieval from Unit I, dense retrieval from sessions 32–33, generation from sessions 27–33.

Nothing in the picture is a new model. Retrieval-augmented generation is those two things connected — and the connection is the new fact, not either box.

The retriever is Unit I's pipeline; the generator is session 27's

Retrieve top-k, build a prompt, call the model

retrieved passage 1
retrieved passage 2
...
retrieved passage k
Based on these texts, answer this question:
What year was the premiere of The Magic Flute?

RAG "can help mitigate hallucination, by giving the model a set of trusted documents", lets a model work over proprietary data, and addresses knowledge that moved.

Grounding the answer in a passage also lets the system show where it came from — not a guarantee that the answer is true, but a citation the user can check.

Retrieved passages can be irrelevant or wrong, and the generator can still hallucinate from them.

A larger model does not fix a document it never saw; retrieval does not make generation faithful. Both halves remain necessary.
Question 1 of 2

What problem does RAG solve that a larger model does not?

Correct: proprietary or post-cut-off knowledge. A larger model still has a training date and a training set.

The second is backwards — RAG adds a step. The third confuses the components: the expensive retriever is an encoder. The fourth is the claim the previous slide declined to make.

Two metrics, chosen by the form of the answer. Exact match for multiple-choice — the percentage matching the gold exactly. Token F1 for free text — bags of tokens, F1 per question, averaged.

Lab 6 defined precision, recall and F1 by hand from a confusion matrix. Token F1 is the same three quantities pointed at token overlap; session 34's chrF was the same move pointed at character n-grams. Lab 6's F1 is β=1, chrF usually β=2, token F1 back at β=1.

Gold: the louvre is in paris. Prediction: the louvre museum is in paris

gold tokens:  the, louvre, is, in, paris              (5)
pred tokens:  the, louvre, museum, is, in, paris      (6)
overlap:      5

precision = 5/6     recall = 5/5     F1 = 0.91
exact match = 0

Exact match scores the extra word as a complete miss; token F1 as a partial hit.

Neither is the thing we actually care about, which is whether the answer is right.

Session 14's trade, restated: intrinsic is inexpensive and approximate, extrinsic expensive and definitive, and a gain on the proxy does not guarantee a gain on the task. Perplexity, chrF, token F1 — every application session ends up needing a metric.

Closed-book QA answers from the model's parameters alone. Open-book is given one or more documents — via RAG, or because a dataset attached a passage.

Natural Questions can be run either way; MMLU is closed-book. There is a third thing, and it is not either of those.

SLP3 teaches QA as prompting improved with retrieval. Lab 12 asks for fine-tuning for question answering, which is extractive span prediction on a fine-tuned encoder.

Given a question and a passage, the model marks the start and end of the answer span inside it — closer to session 7's span-finding than to generating a sentence. A student who meets span prediction in the lab has not missed a lecture; they have met the other half of the clause.

The teachable core of the second half

Extractive summarisation selects existing sentences from the source and arranges them. Abstractive summarisation generates new text.

Extractive cannot produce a sentence the document does not contain — so it cannot paraphrase, compress two facts into one clause, or repair a badly written source.

It also cannot be wrong the way abstractive can: it cannot hallucinate, because every sentence it emits was in the input. The same shape as extractive-versus-generative throughout the course — span prediction against RAG.

A long document in, a short document out, different lengths, no word-to-word alignment. That is the encoder-decoder, unchanged.

The notebook's abstractive example uses T5 — Text-To-Text Transfer Transformer — which is an encoder-decoder transformer. Session 31's architecture is doing the work.
Question 2 of 2

Which summarisation approach can hallucinate?

Correct: abstractive. Hallucination is a response not faithful to the facts, and generating new text is what makes it possible. Extractive can select a misleading sentence or miss the point; it cannot emit a sentence that was not there.

The second uses the word for a different failure. The third flattens a structural guarantee. The fourth would be true of extractive, and is the reason abstractive is the one that can invent.

The notebook demonstrates Luhn, latent semantic analysis, LexRank and TextRank through sumy, plus a BERT-based extractive summariser. Each ranks the document's sentences and takes the top few.

We derive none of them. One is worth naming because its similarity is already in the course: TextRank is PageRank over a sentence-similarity graph, and the similarity is cosine, from session 19.

from gensim.summarization import summarize — that module was removed in gensim 4.0.

Lab 8 and session 22 need gensim 4.4, which is what classroom/pyproject.toml pins, so the two requirements cannot both hold. The sumy, summa and T5 examples are unaffected.

T5 takes the document and generates a shorter one: session 27's encoder-decoder with session 31's blocks, trained text-to-text — the same weights pointed at whatever prefix names the task.

The notebook's 2020 claim that abstractive summarisation is "more of a research topic than a practical application" is a statement about the tooling of that year, not about the architecture. The architecture was finished in this course four sessions ago.

Summary

Sparse retrieval needs exact word overlap — the vocabulary mismatch problem — and a BERT encoder is the modern repair. RAG is a retriever plus a generator: it mitigates hallucination, reaches proprietary data, and tracks knowledge that post-dates the weights, without guaranteeing a faithful answer.

Exact match and token F1 evaluate QA, and lab 12's task is extractive span prediction rather than RAG. Extractive summarisation cannot hallucinate; abstractive can, and is session 27's encoder-decoder. Every application session needed a metric, and every metric is a proxy.

Session 36 is chatbots, and the last hour of the course.

RAG is the bridge it will use: a modern assistant answering from documents is a retriever plus a generator, which is what this session assembled.
