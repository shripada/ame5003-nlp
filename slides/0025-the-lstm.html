The LSTM: two vectors, and three gates that manage them

Session 24 found two problems. The first one — one vector asked to do two jobs — is what this architecture is built to fix. The gates are how it does the managing, not what the design is about.

Session 24's two reasons

  • The hidden layer does two jobs at once — serve the current decision, and carry information forward. One vector, no machinery separating them
  • The gradient is multiplied once per step on the backward pass, driving it toward zero over any real distance

SLP3 §13.5, the sentence session 24 stopped before

"…architectures designed to explicitly manage the task of maintaining relevant context over time, by enabling the network to learn to forget information that is no longer needed and to remember information required for decisions still to come."

It is about managing context. It says nothing about gradients.

The architecture that follows is a response to reason 1. It helps with reason 2 as well, and we get to that — but taking the gradient as the motivation makes the rest of the hour look like an arbitrary list of parts.

The clause that matters most

"The key to solving both problems is to learn how to manage this context rather than hard-coding a strategy into the architecture."

Session 4 hard-coded a strategy with a stop-word list. Session 5 hard-coded one with a stemmer's rules. Session 20 replaced counted weights with fitted ones.

This is the same trade applied to memory: rather than deciding in advance what to forget, learn it. It is the fourth time the course has made that trade.

Before any gate: a second vector

vectorjob
ctcarry information forward — the long-term memory of the unit
htserve the current step — what the unit outputs now, and what the next step's gates read

That is the architecture. Everything else is machinery that manages the two, and the gates exist because ct exists — not the other way round.

The essential change in the LSTM is that two lines run through the unit rather than one.

All three gates are the same construction

A feedforward layer, then a sigmoid, then a pointwise multiplication with the layer being gated.

The sigmoid pushes its outputs toward 0 or 1, so multiplying by it acts as a soft binary mask: near 1 lets a value through, near 0 erases it. Because the gate is computed from weights, the mask is learned rather than fixed.

Session 20 used the sigmoid to make a score readable as a probability. Session 21 used it to score a word pair in negative sampling. Here it is a mask.

Same function, three different jobs — the function does not carry a meaning, the surrounding architecture assigns it one.

⊙ is the element-wise product: multiply two vectors of the same length position by position, producing a third of that length.

Also called the Hadamard product. It is not a dot product and not a matrix multiply — nothing is summed, and no dimension changes.

The forget gate — what to delete

ft=σ(Ufht−1+Wfxt)(13.20)
kt=ct−1⊙ft(13.21)

The forget gate is computed, then multiplied into the previous context. Where ft is near 0, that part of the context is erased; near 1, it survives.

kt is the part of the old context being kept.

The add gate — what to write

gt=tanh⁡(Ught−1+Wgxt)(13.22)
it=σ(Uiht−1+Wixt)(13.23)
jt=gt⊙it(13.24)

gt is the candidate — the new information extracted from this step's input. it is the gate that decides how much of it actually is written.

Their product jt is what gets written.

gt is content, computed exactly as a plain RNN computes its hidden state: the same two weight matrices on the same two inputs, squashed by tanh⁡.

The old RNN is still in there, as one line of a larger unit. It is also the only tanh⁡ of the four — the three gates use σ, because a gate needs to be a mask between 0 and 1, while content needs to be able to be negative.

SLP3 calls this the add gate. Olah — the second reading — and essentially everyone else call it the input gate, which is why its symbol is it.

Same gate, same equation, two names. There is no fourth gate. The same kind of collision as session 23's, where one book's W and W′ were another's U, W and V.

What was kept, plus what was written

ct=jt+kt(13.25)

That is the whole update, and it is an addition.

That it is an addition rather than a product matters for the gradient, and the point returns later in the session.

The output gate — what this step needs now

ot=σ(Uoht−1+Woxt)(13.26)
ht=ot⊙tanh⁡(ct)(13.27)

The context is up to date; the last question is how much of it this step should expose, and the output gate ot answers it.

This is the separation of the two jobs in one line: the unit can hold something in ct and still emit almost nothing in ht.

Three gates, three questions

gatedecidesequations
forgetwhat to delete from context13.20–21

Everything one-dimensional, so ⊙ is ordinary multiplication. The gate values are given, not derived from weights — the question is what the context line does, not where a sigmoid comes from.

If a gate's pre-activation were −4, then σ(−4)=0.0180 — the sort of number in the f column. We are simply starting after the computation. Three steps from c0=0: write, hold, wipe.

Row 2, in detail

Context arrives at 0.7600 and leaves at 0.7298 — a loss of 4% — while the unit emits h=0.0623, which is nearly nothing.

Whatever step 2's input was, it was not written into memory (i=0.03) and not reported (o=0.10). The unit is remembering without acting.

In an RNN, h is the memory — the vector it emits is the vector it carries. Emitting nearly zero therefore means remembering nearly zero.

The two jobs are one vector, which is session 24's reason 1 stated as arithmetic. Here they are two vectors, and row 2 is what the separation buys.

Row 1 writes: i=0.95 lets the candidate through almost intact. Row 3 wipes: f=0.01 deletes what was held, and context drops to 0.0123.

Note o=0.95 in row 3 — the output gate is wide open and the unit still emits almost nothing, because there is nothing left to emit. The gates control different things and are genuinely independent.

The context line crosses each step with a multiply and an add

Session 24's figure had a ×U on every hop of a single chain. The context line here crosses each timestep with an element-wise multiply by a gate and an addition.

No weight matrix sits on it at all. The lower line is the hidden state, which does still get transformed at every step.

Exercise

Carry the context forward untouched while emitting almost nothing

What would f, i and o have to be? And what would a plain RNN have to do to achieve the same thing?

f≈1 so nothing is deleted, i≈0 so nothing new is written, o≈0 so nearly nothing is emitted. That is row 2, which used 0.98, 0.03 and 0.10 and lost 4%. g does not matter — with i≈0 the candidate is masked out whatever it is.

A plain RNN cannot do it. Its memory and its output are the same vector, so "emit almost nothing" and "carry everything forward" are contradictory instructions. No setting of U and W satisfies both, because the problem is not the weights — it is that there is only one vector.

Question 1 of 2

Why does the LSTM add a second vector ct alongside ht?

Correct: the hidden state was doing two jobs at once — SLP3's framing and session 24's reason 1.

The first is the tempting one and it is true as a consequence, but it is not the motivation. Remember the LSTM as a gradient trick and its three gates become an arbitrary list. The third describes something closer to attention, session 28: nothing is stored per word here. The fourth is a size explanation for a structural problem.

Look again at 13.25

ct=jt+kt

Travelling backward along the context line, the gradient passes through an addition and an element-wise multiply by a gate — not through a weight matrix, and not through the same weight matrix once per step.

A path built from additions does not decay exponentially the way a repeated product does. This is why the LSTM also helps with reason 2.

This is the standard explanation, not SLP3's framing, and should not be attributed to the book.

Chapter 13 motivates the architecture as context management and does not present the additive path as the reason for the design. Take it as a consequence rather than a purpose — session 24 said the same, and the two sessions agree deliberately.

Three limits that matter for what comes next

  • Still sequential. Computing ct needs ct−1, so steps cannot run in parallel across time however much hardware there is. That is the limitation the transformer removes
  • Still one vector per position. Everything to the left is compressed into ct, whatever the length. Better, not solved
  • Four times the parameters. Eight matrices against two — at d=300, 720,000 against 180,000

Three gates and eight weight matrices, and the unit does one job: manage a context vector. Is all of it necessary?

Could the same behaviour be obtained with fewer gates? The GRU is one answer to that question, and it is session 26.
Question 2 of 2

What does the output gate ot control?

Correct: how much of the context is emitted now, which is a separate question from how much is kept. Row 2 is the evidence: 0.7298 held, 0.0623 emitted.

The third is the forget gate and the fourth the add gate — both real, just not this one, and mixing them up is the ordinary way to get 13.20–13.27 wrong. The three gates answer three different questions: what to delete, what to write, what to say.

Summary

The LSTM exists because one hidden state cannot both serve the current decision and carry information forward. Its one structural change is a second vector. Three gates manage it, all the same pattern — layer, sigmoid, element-wise multiply — so each is a learned soft mask, and gt is not a gate but the candidate content.

Because ct=jt+kt is an addition, the gradient also survives better along it — a consequence of the design rather than its purpose. Still sequential, still one vector, and four times the parameters. That last cost is session 26's opening question.
