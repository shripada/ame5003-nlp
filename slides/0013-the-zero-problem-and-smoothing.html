The zero problem and smoothing

Counting calls an unseen sentence impossible, which it is not. Fixing that means taking probability away from what we saw and giving it to what we did not.

Session 12 ended on a model that fails

P(I want to eat Indian pizza)=0

This is not an artefact of a small corpus that disappears with more data; at realistic vocabulary sizes it becomes more severe.

A 50,000-word vocabulary admits 2.5 billion possible bigrams, and no corpus holds more than a small fraction. Most test sentences contain at least one unseen pair.

The remedy is smoothing

Remove a small amount of probability from the events we observed, and distribute it among those we did not.

Where the name comes from

  • Picture the probabilities as a bar chart over possible next words: a few tall bars, then a flat row of zeros
  • Smoothing lowers the tall bars slightly and raises the zeros off the floor — the distribution becomes less jagged
  • The total must still sum to 1, so every unit given to an unseen word is taken from a seen one. No probability is created

Add one to every count

MLEP(w2|w1)=count(w1w2)count(w1)add-oneP(w2|w1)=count(w1w2)+1count(w1)+V

The +1 in the numerator ensures that no count is zero any more.

The denominator must grow too, or the probabilities stop summing to 1. It grows by V, the vocabulary size, because 1 was added to each of V possible next words.

Our corpus has 10 tokens a model might predict — the nine words plus </s>. So V=10.

For the context eat, which occurs 3 times, the denominator becomes 3+10=13.

Two-thirds of the evidence actually collected has been handed to words that never followed eat at all, on no evidence whatsoever.

A pair observed on every opportunity it had is left with eight thousandths of one per cent.

The defect is that add-one treats every unseen word as equally likely.

It has no way to know that curry is a plausible continuation of Indian and the is not. There are so many implausible words that they overwhelm the evidence.

Add-one is used in practice, but on problems with small vocabularies — the Naive Bayes classifier in session 16 is one.

For language models it mainly establishes what an adequate method has to do better.

Add-one falls back on no information, spreading a flat estimate evenly. But we are never entirely without information.

If we have not seen she wants Indian, we may still have seen wants Indian; failing that, we know how frequent Indian is on its own. Ask the same question with less context.

Backoff: shorten the context until a count exists

trycountsresult
trigram — she wants Indian0 / 1zero, back off

The phrase was never seen, but Indian is reasonably frequent, so the result is sensible. Add-one would have given it the same value as a word seen once.

The shorter n-gram's probabilities already sum to 1 on their own, so borrowing them directly would push the total above 1.

Backoff as implemented scales the borrowed values down by a discount factor. The principle is what matters here; the details are in the reading.

Interpolation: combine all of them, every time

P(w3|w1w2)=λ1P(w3|w1w2)trigram+λ2P(w3|w2)bigram+λ3P(w3)unigram

The weights sum to 1: λ1+λ2+λ3=1.

The combination

0.5×0.333+0.3×0.333+0.2×0.115=0.290

The trigram and bigram give the same 1/3 here, which is an accident of a four-sentence corpus where almost every context occurs once or twice.

What the arithmetic shows is the unigram floor: whatever the longer contexts contribute, including nothing at all, the combination cannot fall to zero.

The λ values are not guessed, and not fitted on the training corpus — doing that would just reproduce the training data.

A separate held-out set is set aside, and the values that score it best are chosen. Measuring what "best" means is the next session.

Backoff and interpolation compared

  • Backoff uses the shorter n-gram only when the longer one has a zero count
  • Interpolation uses all of them on every estimate, weighted
  • Interpolation generally performs better and is simpler to implement, which is why it is the more common choice

Smoothing handles an unseen pair of known words

Session 12's failure was Indian pizza, and pizza is not in our vocabulary at all. It has no count to smooth and is not among the V words the probability was spread over.

Reserve a slot: <UNK>

  • Before training, replace rare words — say every word occurring once — with the single token <UNK>
  • It becomes an ordinary vocabulary item with real counts
  • At test time, any unseen word is replaced by <UNK> and gets that probability

The model no longer says the sentence is impossible. It says the sentence contains a word it does not know — accurate, and useful.

Exercise

Think it through

Suppose that instead of adding 1 to every count we add 0.01. Does that solve add-one's problem with a 50,000-word vocabulary?

It helps considerably, and it is a real method — add-k smoothing. The denominator becomes count(w1)+0.01V, so the added term is 500 rather than 50,000 and observed bigrams keep far more of their probability.

It does not fix the underlying defect. Every unseen word still gets exactly the same share, so the model still cannot tell that curry beats the after Indian. The wrong estimate has only been made smaller.

Backoff and interpolation differ in kind, not degree: they use evidence about the words themselves. Tuning k reduces the error; backing off avoids it.

Question 1 of 2

Why does add-one smoothing add V to the denominator?

Correct: 1 was added to each of V possible next words, so V was added.

The probabilities over all next words must still sum to 1. V extra counts were distributed in total, one per possible next word, so the denominator grows by exactly V.

Question 2 of 2

What is the main advantage of backoff over add-one smoothing?

Correct: it uses evidence about the word instead of one flat share for all.

Both avoid a zero for an unseen pair, so that is not the distinction, and neither recovers a word missing from the vocabulary — that is what <UNK> is for. The difference is that backoff falls back to a shorter n-gram, so a common word receives more than a rare one.

Summary

Counting gives zero to everything it has not observed, which is most of language. Smoothing moves probability from the observed to the unobserved, always out of the observed counts. Add-one spreads a flat share and is far too crude at realistic V; backoff and interpolation fall back on a shorter context, so the fallback still reflects evidence.

That completes the n-gram model: count, assume a short history, smooth.

One question remains, and every other choice depends on it: how do we tell whether a model is any good?

Bigram or trigram? Which λ values? None of it can be decided without a way to measure model quality. Next session: perplexity.
