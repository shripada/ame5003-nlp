Language models and n-grams

A model that guesses the next word. It is the same idea ChatGPT runs on — and the simplest version of it is just looking at the last word or two.

Unit I was about finding text

We cleaned it, tagged it, indexed it, ranked it. Every technique assumed the text already existed.

Unit II asks a different question: given the words so far, what word comes next?

You already do this

I want to eat Indian ____

Most readers supply food, perhaps curry, and almost certainly not helicopter. No lookup is involved — the prediction comes from knowing how English sentences proceed.

Definition

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence of words. Equivalently, it predicts the probability of the next word given the words that came before.

Scoring a sentence and predicting a word

sentenceprobability
I want to eat Indian foodhigh
I want to eat Indian helicoptervery low
eat Indian I food want tonear zero

Score each next word and you can score a sentence; score sentences and you can compare them.

The model makes no judgement about whether a sentence is true, polite, or sensible.

It reports only how likely those words are to appear in that order, in the kind of text it was trained on.

Where prediction is useful

  • Autocomplete — the three words above a phone keyboard are the model's three highest-probability predictions
  • Speech recognition — "recognise speech" and "wreck a nice beach" sound nearly identical; the model picks the more probable one
  • Spelling and grammar — "their are two" scores low, "there are two" high
  • Machine translation — among several grammatical translations, pick the one that reads most like natural language
  • Text generation — predict a word, append it, predict again

A large language model is a language model, under exactly the definition above: words so far in, a probability for every possible next word out, pick one, append, repeat.

That loop is how a page of text is produced one word at a time. The question being answered is identical.

We begin with n-grams because they pose the same problem in a form whose every component can be inspected, and not because they are a historical preliminary to the real thing.

Context, prediction, the difficulty of rare words, the measurement of quality — every idea in the rest of the course appears here first, small enough to verify by hand.

To predict properly, use everything before it

P(food|I want to eat Indian)

Manageable for five words. But a model must handle any history at all, and most sequences of eight or more words have never been written down anywhere.

A model that learns from examples cannot learn from examples it does not have. The longer the history required, the more certain it is that the history was never observed.

Use only the last few words instead

P(food|Indian)

The first form conditions on everything and cannot be estimated. The second conditions on the last word alone and is easy — Indian food appears constantly in any corpus.

Discard the distant history, keep a small window. That is the idea underlying n-grams.

Definition

An n-gram is a run of n words next to each other in the text.

Obtained by sliding a window of width n along the sentence, one step at a time.

I want to eat Indian food

nnamethe n-gramshow many
1unigram I · want · to · eat · Indian · food6
2bigram I want · want to · to eat · eat Indian · Indian food5
3trigram I want to · want to eat · to eat Indian · eat Indian food4

A sentence of T words yields T − n + 1 n-grams. The windows overlap, which is how the model sees each word in more than one context.

An n-gram model uses the previous n − 1 words

modelcontext it looks atquestion it asks
unigramnothing at allhow common is this word?
bigramthe previous 1 wordwhat usually follows Indian?
trigramthe previous 2 wordswhat usually follows eat Indian?

A unigram model predicts common words — "the of and to a". A bigram model keeps adjacent pairs; a trigram model comes noticeably closer to natural language.

Possible sequences, with a 50,000-word vocabulary

modelpossible sequences
unigram50,000
bigram2,500,000,000
trigram125,000,000,000,000
4-gram6,250,000,000,000,000,000

Each increase in n multiplies by the whole vocabulary. These are the cells the model must be able to fill, not the sequences the corpus contains — the corpus supplies at most one n-gram per word position, so almost every cell is one it never filled.

More context gives better predictions, but more context leaves less data from which to estimate it.

In practice, trigram to 5-gram models are typical. Neural models later in this unit avoid the tension entirely, which is the main reason they displaced n-grams.

Exercise

A bigram model predicts from one word

It is asked to continue the students opened their, predicting from their alone. What has been discarded, and what error does that make likely?

It discarded the students opened — the part indicating that the answer is probably books, laptops or notes. It sees only their, which almost any noun can follow: hands, car, parents.

The output will be grammatical but off-topic. This is the characteristic n-gram failure: locally well-formed, globally incoherent. The deciding word, opened, lay just outside the window.

Question 1 of 2

What does a language model do?

Correct: it gives a probability to a word sequence, to predict the next word.

Scoring a sequence and predicting the next word are one capability seen from two directions. Truth is not involved: a confidently stated false sentence gets a high probability, because "likely" means "resembles real language".

Question 2 of 2

Why do n-gram models not simply use a large n, such as 10?

Correct: longer sequences almost never repeat, so there is too little data.

The number of possible n-grams is the vocabulary size raised to the power n. Most 10-word sequences have never been written down once, and a model that estimates from counts cannot estimate from a sequence it has never seen.

Summary

A language model assigns a probability to a word sequence, which is the same as predicting the next word. An n-gram model does it in the simplest way: keep the previous n − 1 words, discard the rest.
