MSIS · AME 5003 · Unit I · Session 05

Stemming and lemmatization

Two ways of cutting a word down to a root — and how to decide which one a task needs.

One difference is left, and it is the largest

study    studies    studying    studied

Four forms of one word. For counting and for search we want them to count as the same item, so a search for studying finds a page saying studies.

Stemming: remove the ending by rule

  • The standard tool is the Porter stemmer
  • An ordered list of rules of the form “if a word ends in -ing, remove it”
  • No dictionary, and no idea what any word means

The whole family collapses to one string

study      ->  studi
studies    ->  studi
studying   ->  studi
studied    ->  studi

And argue argued argues arguing all become argu.

studi is not a word of English.

That is not a defect. The stemmer has one job — map related words to the same string — and it did it.

A stem is never displayed. It is an internal key, and for search that is enough.

Rules with no meaning behind them make mistakes

organization   ->  organ     the meaning is gone
was            ->  wa        not a word
mice           ->  mice      unchanged
better         ->  better    no link to "good"

Three kinds of failure

  • organization → organ — the rule removed too much. This is over-stemming
  • was → wa — a rule fired that should not have, leaving a non-word
  • mice — a stemmer can only remove endings, and an irregular plural has no ending to remove, so mouse is out of reach

Lemmatization: look the word up

  • Also reduces a word to a root, but the root is a dictionary form
  • That form is called the lemma
  • Because it looks words up rather than chopping them, it reaches the cases stemming cannot

The same four words

mice     ->  mouse     irregular plural
was      ->  be        irregular verb
better   ->  good      knows they are related
studies  ->  study     a real word

Every output appears in a dictionary.

A stem is a shared key.

A lemma is the base form of the word.

That is the whole difference, and it is the one the exam asks for.

The root can depend on the sentence

"we had a meeting"       NOUN  ->  meeting
"they are meeting now"   VERB  ->  meet

Same spelling, different roots. The only thing telling them apart is the part of speech.

Given no part of speech, it guesses “noun”

lemmatize("was")           ->  wa      wrong
lemmatize("was", verb)     ->  be      correct

lemmatize("better")        ->  better  wrong
lemmatize("better", adj)   ->  good    correct

Guessed wrong, was becomes wa — no better than the stemmer managed.

Lemmatization is only as good as the part-of-speech information it is given — and labelling every word by hand is not practical.

spaCy works out the part of speech and lemmatizes in one step. Working out the part of speech is a substantial task of its own, and it is session 6.

Exercise

Think first

A search engine over several million documents. It must be fast, and the roots are never shown to a user — they only match a query against a document.

Stem, or lemmatize?

Stem. Speed matters, the roots are never displayed, and non-words like studi are fine as internal match keys. A dictionary lookup per word would be slower and buy nothing.

Change the task so the roots are shown to a user, or fed to a model, and the answer becomes lemmatize. As in session 4, the task decides.

Question 1 of 2

The Porter stemmer turns organization into organ. Why is this not necessarily a problem?

Correct: for search, all that matters is that related words share a key.

It becomes a real problem when organ — the instrument, or the body part — stems to the same key and is matched wrongly. That over-collapsing is the cost of the rule-based approach.

Question 2 of 2

Why can a lemmatizer turn was into be, when the Porter stemmer only manages wa?

Correct: the lemmatizer looks the word up, so it knows irregular forms.

Stemming only removes endings, so was → be and mice → mouse are beyond it — there is no ending to remove. The costs are the part of speech, and the speed.

Summary

Stemming chops by rule: fast, no dictionary, need not give a real word, right for search. Lemmatization looks up: accurate, needs the part of speech, slower, right when the output must be real words.

That completes the cleaning sequence. Next: part-of-speech tagging — the information lemmatization just asked for.
