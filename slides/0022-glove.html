GloVe

One loss function and one co-occurrence matrix. The session's real subject is the question GloVe raises about Word2Vec, and the answer, which differs from the one GloVe's own paper gives.

Session 21 set counting aside and replaced it with predicting: delete a word, predict it, keep the weights.

This session asks the question that move raises and does not answer — was prediction necessary, or would counting have worked all along, given the right quantity to count?

Word2Vec slides a window across the corpus, asks one question at each position, and discards the window. Over a billion tokens it meets (ice, steam) thousands of times and never holds the total.

The corpus already contains that total. Counting once how often every word appears near every other produces a table Word2Vec spends a whole training run approximating one window at a time.

Global Vectors

Build the global co-occurrence matrix, then fit vectors to it directly.

Xij is how many times word j appears within some window of word i, across the whole corpus — session 19's pie/data/computer table, scaled to every word against every word.

Co-occurrence matrices long predate the paper. Its contribution is the claim that a single probability is not the informative quantity, and a ratio of two probabilities is.

Exercise

Both drinks have 10,000 co-occurrences in total, so the counts compare directly
X(espresso,sugar)=5X(juice,sugar)=45X(espresso,caffeine)=80X(juice,caffeine)=4

Work out P(k|espresso)/P(k|juice) for each probe. Which word is each ratio "about"? And what would a ratio near 1 tell you?

sugar: 5/45 ≈ 0.11 — small, so sugar belongs to juice.

caffeine: 80/4 = 20 — large, so caffeine belongs to espresso.

A ratio near 1 means the probe is equally associated with both, or with neither, and carries no information for distinguishing them. A plain probability says only that two words co-occur often or rarely; the ratio says which of two candidates a third word belongs with.

The equal totals are what let the 10,000s cancel. That is a convenience of this exercise — in the real table below the two words have very different totals.

Pennington et al. trained on 6 billion tokens, 350 times this corpus, where two arbitrary words almost always co-occur by chance. Sparse counts fail where they always fail — this is session 13's smoothing problem in a different matrix.

Fit the vectors to the log of the raw count

wi𝖳w~j+bi+b~j=log⁡Xij

wi is word i's vector, w~j is word j's vector in the context role, and the b terms are per-word biases.

The biases absorb how frequent each word is on its own, leaving the dot product to encode the relationship rather than raw frequency.

Over every nonzero cell of X

J=∑ijf(Xij)(wi𝖳w~j+bi+b~j−log⁡Xij)2

The algebraic identity

log⁡(Xik)−log⁡(Xjk)=log⁡(Xik/Xjk). So if the model fits wi𝖳w~k≈log⁡Xik for every word, then a difference of dot products is automatically fitting the log of the ratio.

The log is what turns "fit a count" into "fit the ratio that carries the meaning". The paper's three-page derivation is not needed to use the result.

Squared error on log⁡Xij lets a few extremely frequent pairs — (the, of), (a, the) — dominate the loss and suppress everything else.

Exactly how one very large raw count previously overwhelmed idf. The correction has the same form as session 18's log damping.

Cap how much any single pair can contribute

f(x)={(x/xmax⁡)αif x<xmax⁡1otherwise

The paper's values are xmax⁡=100 and α=3/4.

Frequent pairs are capped at full weight rather than growing without bound, and very rare pairs — co-occurring once or twice — are down-weighted too, because a single accidental co-occurrence is largely noise. Same problem as session 18, same form of correction, different matrix.

There are two matrices, w and w~, corresponding to Word2Vec's E and W′. Session 21 said some implementations offer to average the two, and that this is rarely done.

GloVe is the implementation that does it. Summing w+w~ gives marginally better vectors than either alone, and every released GloVe file has it already applied.
Question 1 of 2

GloVe's loss pulls wi𝖳w~j+bi+b~j toward a target value. What is that target?

Correct: the log of the raw co-occurrence count, log⁡Xij.

Nothing here is a probability of anything — no softmax, no prediction step, no sampling. GloVe is closer to matrix factorisation than to a classifier. The log matters because a difference of two such fits reconstructs the co-occurrence ratio, which is what the method is built to recover.

It would be natural to conclude that GloVe has solved the antonym problem, but it has not. 0.645 is still a high cosine, close behind the tenth-ranked word.

And this file used different preprocessing (lower-cased), a different corpus (Wikipedia and Gigaword), and different hyperparameters. The defensible reading: two representations trained on different data with different settings disagree at the margins.

About the same — and the theoretical distinction is smaller than either paper's framing suggests

Levy & Goldberg (2014) proved Skip-gram with negative sampling is implicitly factorising a matrix of shifted pointwise mutual information: it was using global corpus statistics all along, just never writing them down.

Levy, Goldberg & Dagan (2015) then showed that matching hyperparameters mostly closes the gap. The real lesson of GloVe is not "counting beats predicting" — it is that counting and predicting were never as different as either abstract made them sound.
Question 2 of 2

What does later research (Levy & Goldberg) say about GloVe vs. Word2Vec?

Correct: Skip-gram was already using global statistics, and matched hyperparameters close most of the gap.

GloVe's own paper argues the third option is false, and its pitch is the first option. The 2014 result is what makes that pitch hard to defend: SGNS is provably factorising a PMI-based matrix, so "counts vs. prediction" was never a clean split. The 2015 follow-up measured it and found hyperparameters mattered more than the choice of method.

Every representation from session 8 onward answers the same question: how to turn a word or a document into numbers a machine can compare.

Each answer corrected something the previous one got wrong, at the cost of some of its interpretability. Sessions 21 and 22 are where that trade is most pronounced — a 300-dimensional vector contains no single number whose meaning can be stated.

Both share every limitation session 21 identified

  • One vector per word, regardless of how many senses it has — bank gets a single point wherever the river and financial senses average out
  • Fixed at training time — a word absent from the corpus has no vector at all. Session 3's vocabulary problem, unchanged
  • Blind to order — both are fit from window-based co-occurrence, so neither represents a sequence

All three are Unit III topics. The third is why Unit III opens with a model built to process a sequence as a sequence, one step at a time.

Summary

GloVe counts once, then fits vectors so their dot product recovers the log of each count — because a difference of two such fits reconstructs the ratio that actually distinguishes one word from another. A weighting function stops frequent pairs dominating, the same complaint session 18 made about raw counts.

In practice it produces vectors about as good as Word2Vec's, not better, and the "global counts beat local prediction" story does not survive Levy & Goldberg. That closes Unit II.
