Part-of-speech tagging

Labelling each word with its grammatical class, and why the same word takes different labels in different sentences.

Part-of-speech tagging labels each word with its grammatical category.

The word classes

ClassJobExamples
Nouna thing, person, place, ideaphone, Manipal
Verban action or staterun, book, is
Adjectivedescribes a nounold, best
Adverbdescribes a verb or adjectivedaily, very
Pronounstands in for a nounI, they, it
Determinerintroduces a nounthe, a, this
Prepositionrelates a noun to the reston, in, to

Open and closed classes

  • Open — nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs. New members arrive all the time: to google, selfie
  • Closed — determiners, pronouns, prepositions. Nobody coins a new word for the

The closed classes are, not coincidentally, the same words a stop-word list removes.

If every word belonged to one class, tagging would be a dictionary lookup. It is not, because a word's class depends on how it is used.

book as a noun and as a verb

"Give me the book."         book  ->  NOUN
"I can book a flight."      book  ->  VERB
"They book flights daily."  book  ->  VERB

Words do not have a fixed word class

  • the book — a determiner is followed by a noun, so it is a noun
  • can book — a modal is followed by a verb, so it is a verb
  • A dictionary lists both classes and has no way of choosing between them

The tagger reads the whole sentence and uses each word's neighbours.

This makes tagging a sequence-labelling task: a word is tagged within the sequence it sits in, never on its own.

Nothing in the word itself resolves the ambiguity; only the context around it does.

Exercise

From session 1 — try it again now

"The old man the boat." is grammatical English.

What job does each word do?

The old means “the old people”, so old stands in as a noun. man is the verb — to man a boat is to staff it. The old people staff the boat.

Most readers take man for a noun first and hit a dead end. That is a garden-path sentence.

The same sentence through spaCy

spaCy tags man as a noun, which is the error a human reader makes on first reading.

Taggers are about 97% accurate overall, but genuinely ambiguous sentences remain beyond them.

Tagsets: two levels of granularity

  • Universal — coarse, about 17 tags: NOUN, VERB, ADJ, ADV, DET. Readable, and consistent across languages
  • Penn Treebank — fine, about 45 tags. A noun splits into NN and NNS; a verb into VB, VBZ, VBP and more

A tagset is the list of tags a tagger may choose from. Most tools produce both.

One sentence, both tagsets

WordUniversalPenn Treebank
TheyPRONPRP (personal pronoun)
bookVERBVBP (present, not 3rd-sing)
flightsNOUNNNS (plural noun)
dailyADVRB (adverb)

Neither tagset is more correct. They describe the same thing at different granularity.

The coarse tagset is enough to feed a lemmatizer; the fine one is for when singular versus plural matters. A larger tagset also gives a tagger more ways to be wrong.

Where tagging is used

  • Lemmatization — session 5 needed the part of speech, and this is where it comes from
  • Named entity recognition — names are nouns, so the tags narrow down where in a sentence to look for them
  • Everything downstream — classical pipelines tag first, because later stages depend on each word's job
Question 1 of 2

Why can POS tagging not be done as a simple dictionary lookup?

Correct: a word's class depends on the words around it.

book is a noun in “the book” and a verb in “can book”. A dictionary lists both and cannot choose. That is what makes this a sequence-labelling task.

Question 2 of 2

You need to know only whether each word is a noun, verb, or adjective, to feed a lemmatizer. Which tagset fits best?

Correct: the Universal tagset.

The lemmatizer wants only the broad class, so 17 tags is enough. Singular versus plural, or which present tense, is detail it never uses. Match the tagset to what the task needs.

Summary

Tagging labels each word with its grammatical class, and it needs the whole sentence — the same word is a noun in one position and a verb in another.
