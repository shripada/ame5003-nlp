MSIS · AME 5003 · Unit III · Session 33

Fine-tuning, DistilBERT and knowledge distillation

Session 32 built BERT and stopped at what it is. This session is what it is for: a small head trained on labelled data, the same head pointed at session 7's tagging scheme, and then a smaller copy trained to behave like the large one.

SLP3 §10.4

"We add application-specific circuitry (often called a special head) on top of pretrained models… The finetuning process consists of using labeled data about the application to train these additional application-specific parameters. Typically, this training will either freeze or make only minimal adjustments to the pretrained language model parameters."

SLP3 places this under transfer learning: knowledge acquired on one task — masked language modelling on unlabelled text — applied to a new one, whatever we have labels for.

Two consequences, and they are why this is the paradigm the course ends on. The expensive part is already done, once, by someone else. And the labelled data needed is small, because it trains a head rather than a model.

For three-way sentiment with BERT-base, the head is a matrix WC of shape [768×3] — 2,304 parameters — against the 108 million session 32 counted in the encoder.

In practice the last few transformer layers are often updated too; SLP3 names that as typical. What is not typical is retraining those 108 million from scratch on the labelled set.

Correct: the head, with the pretrained weights frozen or barely touched — §10.4's definition, and the reason fine-tuning is cheap. The 108 million parameters already encode language; the labelled set trains a few thousand more.

The scheme has not changed. What has is the encoder underneath it — twelve unmasked transformer blocks instead of the spaCy tagger session 7 ran — and that the loss training WK can also make small updates in the last few of those blocks.

The large object is shared and unchanged; the small object on top varies

BERT-base is 108 million parameters. Fine-tuning it is cheap compared with pretraining it — but running it is still running 108 million parameters, and this course's labs run on Colab.

Sanh et al. 2019 compress the model during pretraining, then fine-tune the smaller copy the same way this session has just fine-tuned the large one.

Sanh et al. 2019, §2

"A compression technique in which a compact model — the student — is trained to reproduce the behaviour of a larger model — the teacher — or an ensemble of models."

The student is not trained on one-hot labels alone but on the teacher's full output distribution.

Asked to fill I think this is the beginning of a beautiful [MASK], BERT-base puts most of its mass on day and life, and a long tail on future, story, world. That ranking is information a one-hot target throws away.

The syllabus line names both DistilBERT and fine-tuning, and it is easy to fuse them. Distillation is how the student is pretrained. Fine-tuning is what we then do with it, and it is §10.4 unchanged.

DistilBERT is the student — a smaller BERT, pretrained by distillation from BERT-base, then fine-tuned like BERT. Lab 12 loads distilbert-base-uncased and sits a classification head on it. It does not re-run distillation.
Question 2 of 2

Why does the student train on the teacher's full distribution rather than on one-hot labels alone?

Correct: the distribution is a richer target. A one-hot vector says this class; the probabilities also say and that one is close, and that one is not.

The second is the natural misreading and it is false — DistilBERT is distilled on the same corpus BERT was, and the original tokens are still there as hard targets under Lmlm. The fourth inverts the design: masked language modelling is the second term of the triple loss.

Three losses at once

losswhat it matches
Lcedistillation — cross-entropy against the teacher's soft targets, with a softmax temperature that flattens both distributions so the long tail is not near-zero
Lmlmthe ordinary masked-LM loss from session 32, against the original tokens
Lcoscosine embedding loss, aligning the directions of student and teacher hidden vectors

The paper's ablation finds dropping any of them lowers the GLUE macro-score, with the distillation term carrying most of the load and the masked-LM term the least.

Lcos "will tend to align the directions of the student and teacher hidden states vectors". Direction, not magnitude — the distinction session 19 spent an hour establishing.

The two vectors are a student hidden state and the teacher's at the same position, and the loss asks them to point the same way even if they are not the same length. Third time the same geometric fact has been the thing an equation depends on.

Same general architecture as BERT. Sanh removes the token-type embeddings and the pooler, and halves the number of layers — twelve blocks become six.

The paper is explicit that the cut is layers rather than width: variations in hidden size "have a smaller impact on computation efficiency… than variations on other factors like the number of layers". DistilBERT also drops NSP — the same finding session 32 recorded from RoBERTa, arrived at independently.

Derivable from session 32's arithmetic, so a check rather than a quotation

39.2% against the paper's "40%", and 65.9M against DistilBERT's commonly quoted 66M. Two independent routes to the same number.

Halving twelve blocks to six removes half the block parameters — but the 23 million embedding table is untouched.

Session 32 already established that those embeddings are 21% of BERT, so the model shrinks by less than half. "Halve the layers, halve the model" is the natural inference, and it is wrong for a reason session 32 already supplied.

Exercise

Session 32's numbers: 7,077,888 per block, embeddings 23,040,000 + 393,216

Compute DistilBERT's parameter count with the layers halved and the embeddings kept, then the reduction as a percentage. Second: why is it not 50%?

The count. Six blocks: 6×7,077,888=42,467,328. Embeddings unchanged: 23,433,216. Total 65,900,544, and 1−65,900,544/108,367,872=0.392 — 39.2%, Sanh's "40%" to the precision the headline uses.

Why not 50%. Halving the layers halves the block parameters, 84,934,656 → 42,467,328. The embedding table does not shrink. Those 23 million are 21% of BERT-base and a still larger fraction of DistilBERT, and they are a lookup over the same 30,000-token vocabulary. Cutting layers cannot cut a table whose rows are word types.

The architecture of Unit III is finished. Sessions 34–36 are applications of it — machine translation, question answering and summarisation, then chatbots.

Lab 12 fine-tunes distilbert-base-uncased, not BERT. The 40% is why, and the worked example in this session is the model the lab actually loads.

Summary

Fine-tuning adds a small head and trains it on labelled data with the pretrained weights frozen or barely updated — 2,304 parameters against 108 million. Classification and pair classification read [CLS]; labelling reads every token, with session 7's BIO tags as the label set.

Distillation trains a student to match a teacher's full distribution. DistilBERT is BERT with the layers halved, a triple loss including a cosine term from session 19, and 39.2% smaller rather than 50% because the embedding table does not shrink.
