Bag of Words and the vector space model

You built this table in session 8 and used it as a lookup. Change the 0s and 1s to counts, read the columns instead of the rows, and it becomes a map with documents as places on it.

Definition

A bag of words represents a document by which words it contains and how often, discarding the order entirely.

The name is precise: a bag has contents but no sequence, unlike a list.

Opposite meanings, identical representations

"the film was good, not bad"   ->  the·1 film·1 was·1 good·1 not·1 bad·1
"the film was bad, not good"   ->  the·1 film·1 was·1 good·1 not·1 bad·1

No later processing can recover a distinction destroyed here. We accept the loss for session 15's reason — word identity carries most of the signal — but knowingly.

Session 8 built a table with one row per term and one column per document, holding a 1 where the term appears and a 0 where it does not.

We now make a single change: instead of whether a word appears, record how many times.

Four plays, four words, real counts

As You Like ItTwelfth NightJulius Caesar Henry V

These are four different plays from session 8's six — only Julius Caesar is in both — because these are the ones with published counts.

The change is small, but it changes what the table records. A 1 recorded that a play contains the word; a 58 says the play is substantially concerned with fools.

We have moved from contains to how much about.

Read the table in columns, not rows

As You Like It   =   [ 1, 114, 36, 20 ]
                       ^   ^    ^   ^
                    battle good fool wit

A list of four numbers is a vector, and a vector of length four is a point in four-dimensional space: one axis per word.

The central idea

Every document becomes a point in a space with one dimension per vocabulary word. Documents lying close together use words in similar proportions, which is to say they concern similar subjects.

The representation turns a document's subject matter into a position. This is the vector space model, and the rest of the course operates inside it.

Session 8's matrix answered: does this document contain these words?

The vector space model answers a different question — which of these documents resemble each other? Nothing in Unit I could answer that.

Four dimensions cannot be drawn — so take two

As You Like It and Twelfth Night are comedies; Julius Caesar and Henry V are histories.

Nobody supplied that. The grouping follows from counting two words. Real vectors have tens of thousands of dimensions and cannot be drawn — the proximity is still there, and session 19 measures it.

The rows behave the same way: fool is [36, 58, 1, 4], a word represented by the documents it appears in.

That observation matters more later than it does now. If words have positions, then words with similar positions are used in similar contexts, which is much of what a word means. Sessions 21 and 22 develop this.

Sparsity, and where we already solved it

  • A real vocabulary has 50,000 words, so every document vector has 50,000 components
  • A 200-word review is non-zero in at most 200 of them — over 99% of every vector is zero
  • Such a vector is sparse, and storing it directly is impractical

This is why session 9 built the inverted index. The vector space model is how we reason about documents; the inverted index is how we store them.

Exercise

Look at the table again

good has by far the largest counts — 114, 80, 62, 89 — in every play. If one of the four words had to be dropped, which should it be, and why?

good, despite having the largest values by a wide margin.

It occurs often in all four plays and so never helps distinguish them. On the plot it would push every play far along the same axis and separate nothing. battle, whose counts are small, separates histories from comedies cleanly.

A word is useful in proportion to how well it distinguishes documents, not to how often it occurs. Raw counts reverse this, and worst for the commonest words.

Question 1 of 2

In the vector space model, what does a single column of the term-document matrix represent?

Correct: one document, placed by how often it uses each word.

Columns are documents, rows are words. The first option describes a row, which is also a useful vector — it represents a word, and it is the basis of the embeddings in sessions 21 and 22.

Question 2 of 2

Why can bag of words not tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog"?

Correct: it records only which words occur and how often, not their order.

Both sentences hold the same three words once each, so both produce the same vector. The information was destroyed at representation time and nothing downstream recovers it — a known cost, accepted deliberately.

Summary

Bag of words turns each document into a vector: a point in a space with one dimension per vocabulary word. Points close together use words in similar proportions and so concern similar subjects.

Search, similarity and every embedding later in the course are built on this representation.

good has the largest counts in the table and is the least informative word in it. Raw counts reward words for being common, when we want words that are distinctive.

The correction has already appeared: session 10's TF-IDF. The next session puts it inside the vector rather than inside a score, and re-weighting this same table changes which words dominate it.
