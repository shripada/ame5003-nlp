MSIS · AME 5003 · Unit III · Session 34

Machine translation

Session 33 closed by saying the architecture of Unit III is finished. This session is the first application of it: everything a working translation system needs that a transformer diagram does not show.

§12.2 in one clause: "the standard architecture for MT is the encoder-decoder transformer". That architecture is already in hand, so the clause is a summary rather than a preview.

What remains is two pieces of new machinery — a decoding rule more careful than taking the best word at each step, and the first evaluation metric since session 14 that compares an output against a human reference — plus why the task is hard, and a finding about gender.

One pair was enough to motivate the encoder-decoder

source (English) :  the    green   witch   arrived
target (Spanish) :  llegó  la      bruja   verde
glossed          :  arrived the    witch   green

Alignment 4, 1, 3, 2. §12.1 is the general case: that reordering is one instance of a systematic phenomenon, not a quirk of Spanish.

Linguistic typology, organised into four kinds

  • Word order. English, Spanish, French, Mandarin are SVO; Hindi and Japanese SOV; Irish and Arabic VSO. Two SVO languages can still disagree about adjectives — green witch against bruja verde, which is the 4, 1, 3, 2
  • Lexical. bass is lubina or bajo; brother is gege or didi. Sometimes there is no word at all — Mandarin xiào, a lexical gap
  • Morphological. Vietnamese is isolating; a polysynthetic language packs a whole sentence into one word. Translating across that range means working below the word
  • Referential density. English requires a pronoun; Spanish can drop it, Japanese and Chinese drop more. SLP3: cold against hot languages

Session 27 needed one pair to show that sequence labelling cannot translate. The four divergences say why a pair like that is normal.

Word order, lexical choice, morphology and dropped referents all require the output to be a function of the whole source, of a length the source does not determine. That is the job the encoder-decoder was built for. Nothing here is a new model.

§12.2.1

"A shared vocabulary is used for the source and target languages, which makes it easy to copy tokens (like names) from source to target."

Built by running a subword algorithm on a corpus containing both languages — session 3's BPE, whose introducing paper was titled Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units.

A name such as Manipal should survive the trip unchanged. With a shared subword vocabulary the model can copy the pieces rather than invent a translation of them.

It also makes English or Hindi, which use spaces, and Chinese or Thai, which do not, the same kind of input: the units are subwords either way, so the model never has to be told which side of that line it is on.

The training data is a parallel corpus, or bitext: sentences paired with their translations. Europarl and the United Nations Parallel Corpus are the standard large examples.

The model maximises the probability of the target tokens given the source tokens — session 12's chain rule pointed at a pair of languages rather than a single sentence.

§12.4

"What looks high probability at word t might turn out to have been the wrong choice once we get to word t + 1."

A search tree over yes, ok, EOS

pathprobability
ok ok EOS 0.4 × 0.7 × 1.0 = 0.280 — the true best
yes yes …0.5 × 0.4 = 0.200
yes ok …0.5 × 0.3 = 0.150
yes EOS0.5 × 0.2 = 0.100

Greedy takes yes, because 0.5 beats 0.4. No continuation of yes can reach 0.280. The best first word is not the first word of the best sentence.

The two paths part company immediately

Keep the top k partial hypotheses at each step rather than one. Extend all of them by every word, score the k×|V| candidates, keep the best k again.

k is the beam width — 5 to 10 for MT. When a hypothesis emits EOS it is complete and the beam shrinks by one; search continues until the beam is empty.

At k=1 this is greedy decoding.

Beam search generalises the rule the obvious choice already was. It does not replace it with a different kind of object.

A wider beam explores more and still prunes, and the globally best sequence can be lost at any step — exactly as ok is lost at step 1 when k=1.

Exhaustive search would find the optimum at a cost of |V|T. Dynamic programming does not apply either — generation has long-distance dependencies, so the Viterbi argument does not carry over. Beam search keeps the most probable prefixes it has not yet discarded.

Minimum Bayes risk decoding inverts the objective rather than widening the search: choose the translation likely to score best on the evaluation metric, not the most probable one.

SLP3 says it can work better than beam search, and names chrF and BERTScore as the metrics to maximise — which is the next section's machinery pointed back at this one.
Question 1 of 2

Why does greedy decoding sometimes miss the most probable sentence?

Correct: the best first word need not begin the best sequence. yes is the best first word and ok ok EOS is the best sentence.

The second is backwards: beam search with k=1 is greedy. The third confuses two gaps — teacher forcing is about what the decoder is fed; greedy versus beam is about how the next token is chosen.

Adequacy is whether the translation captures the meaning; fluency is whether it is natural in the target language. Humans produce the best evaluations of both, and are slow and expensive.

That is session 14's argument restated. Twenty sessions ago the stand-in was perplexity; here it scores a translation against a human reference. Same trade — and a gain on the proxy does not guarantee a gain on the thing we care about.

SLP3 leads with chrF, character F-score (Popović, 2015), calling it "the simplest and most robust metric for MT evaluation".

BLEU — the name nearly every student has heard — is filed among "many other earlier related metrics like BLEU, METEOR, TER". We name BLEU in a moment, because a five-minute search will produce it. We do not lead with it, because the reading does not.

Lab 6's precision, recall and F1 — now over character n-grams

chrFβ=(1+β2)chrP⋅chrRβ2⋅chrP+chrR(12.20)
chrF2=5⋅chrP⋅chrR4⋅chrP+chrR

chrP is the average of the k precisions — what fraction of the hypothesis n-grams occur in the reference. chrR is the average of the k recalls.

Easy to mix up: k is how long the n-grams go; β weights recall against precision. Lab 6's F1 is the case β=1; chrF commonly uses β=2.

These are character n-grams, not word n-grams — so chrF ignores spaces.

A student who has met BLEU will tokenise into words and get every count wrong. Punctuation stays; spaces come out first.

k=β=2, one preposition different

REF:  witnessforthepast,      18 unigrams, 17 bigrams
HYP:  witnessofthepast,       17 unigrams, 16 bigrams

Matching unigrams: 17. Matching bigrams: 13.

Precision and recall at each n

precisionrecall
unigram17/17 = 1.00017/18 = 0.944
bigram13/16 = 0.81313/17 = 0.765

Average, then apply the formula

chrP=(1.000+0.813)/2=0.906chrR=(0.944+0.765)/2=0.855chrF2=5⋅0.906⋅0.8554⋅0.906+0.855=0.864

SLP3 prints this as .86; the unrounded value is .864.

A second hypothesis in the chapter, past witness, scores .62 against the same reference — worse, as it should, because the words are there and the order is not.

BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) is word-based rather than character-based, and "purely precision-based rather than combining precision and recall".

It also needs a brevity penalty, clipped n-gram counts, and a standardised tokeniser before two numbers can be compared. chrF is what we compute; BLEU is what a paper from 2015 or a leaderboard may still report. BERTScore goes further, comparing BERT embeddings — session 32's contextual vectors doing evaluation work.

Exercise

Compute chrF2 with k=2, following the worked example — including the first step

REF: the green witch against HYP: the witch green. Report both precisions, both recalls, chrP, chrR and chrF2.

Spaces out first. thegreenwitch is 13 unigrams and 12 bigrams; thewitchgreen is the same counts. The two strings are anagrams, so every unigram matches: 13.

The bigrams are not. Reference: th he eg gr re ee en nw wi it tc ch. Hypothesis: th he ew wi it tc ch hg gr re ee en. Ten match.

Unigram P = R = 1. Bigram P = R = 10/12 = 0.833. chrP = chrR = 0.917, and when precision equals recall chrF2 equals that common value: 0.917.

The instructive wrong answer. Tokenising into words gives three matching unigrams and zero matching bigrams, so chrP = chrR = 0.5 and chrF2 = 0.5. A plausible-looking number, and it is BLEU's instinct applied to chrF. The spaces had to come out first.

Question 2 of 2

What does chrF compare?

Correct: character n-gram overlap, precision and recall combined. Both halves matter: characters, not words; F-score, not precision alone.

The second is BLEU, and it is what most people who have heard of a translation metric will say. The third is session 14's metric pointed at the wrong object. The fourth is BERTScore.

The referential-density divergence from the start of the hour, now pointed at a person: English pronouns are obligatory and gendered, so the system must choose.

The easy claim is that the model reflects its training data. Prates et al. found something stronger: the stereotypes "can't completely be accounted for by gender bias in US labor statistics, because the biases are amplified… with a probability higher than if the mapping was based on actual labor employment statistics".

The system is more biased than the data it learned from. That is a measurable claim, it is checkable, and it is a different finding from "models reflect society".

The WinoMT challenge set (Stanovsky et al. 2019) shows MT systems performing worse on non-stereotypical roles — The doctor asked the nurse to help her in the operation.

Where the pronoun and the occupation disagree with the stereotype the table records.

The section does not end on a debiasing recipe. MT is used in medical and legal settings where a wrong translation can cause harm.

"In order to 'do no harm', systems need ways to assign confidence values to candidate translations, so they can abstain from giving incorrect translations that may cause harm." Knowing when not to answer is the open problem the chapter names.

Summary

Four divergences say why translation needs an output that is a function of the whole source. A shared subword vocabulary lets names copy. Greedy decoding misses the best sentence because the best first word need not begin it; beam search keeps k prefixes, is greedy at k=1, and still guarantees nothing.

chrF scores character n-gram F against a human reference — session 14's proxy trade pointed at a new task. And when the source does not mark gender, the system does not merely inherit stereotypes: it amplifies them.
