Judging a language model: perplexity

We have built three models and have no way to say which is best. The measure that settles it is a single number, but it means something only when computed on text the model has never seen.

Three sessions of choices, none of them settled

  • Bigram or trigram?
  • Add-one, backoff, or interpolation?
  • If interpolation — which λ values?

Each is an empirical question. None can be resolved by argument.

Extrinsic evaluation

Put model A inside the speech recogniser and measure the system. Swap in model B and measure again. The model producing the better system is the better model.

This is the only kind of evaluation that answers the question we actually care about.

Running a full speech recogniser over a test corpus twice can take hours or days — and we may need to compare twenty models, not two.

Tuning λ that way is not feasible. We also want a cheap measure that scores the language model on its own, with no application attached: an intrinsic evaluation. The standard one is perplexity.

A model cannot be evaluated on the text it was trained on.

The point of a language model is to predict text it has not seen. Scored on its own training corpus it is being asked to recall, not to predict, and it will look far better than it is.

The corpus is split three ways

setwhat it is forhow often you touch it
training setget the counts; learn the probabilitiesconstantly
development setchoose between models; tune λ, k, nas often as you like
test setthe final, honest number once, at the very end

Evaluating on the test set fifty times while adjusting λ lets its quirks steer those choices. That is what the development set is for.

This also explains session 13's held-out set, the one the λ values came from.

A held-out set is exactly this: data withheld from training so it can answer a question the training data cannot answer without bias.

Training on the test set

Also called data contamination. It does not produce a small error; it produces excellent numbers that mean nothing.

It happens by accident: duplicate documents in a scraped corpus, or a shuffled split putting two halves of one article on both sides. When a result is reported, the first question is what it was measured on.

The criterion

The better model is the one that assigns higher probability to the test set.

Higher probability means the model was less surprised by real text — precisely the ability we want it to have.

A sentence probability is a product of numbers below 1, so every extra word makes it smaller. A 10-word test set gives about 0.0001; a 1,000-word one gives hundreds of zeros after the point.

The magnitude reflects the length of the text, not the quality of the model. Normalise by length and what remains is a per-word figure.

Perplexity: inverted, and normalised per token

PP(W)=P(w1w2…wN)−1/N=1P(W)N

Two separate operations

  • The −1 inverts the probability, so a high probability becomes a low number. Lower perplexity is better — the most commonly misremembered property of the measure
  • The 1/N takes the N-th root, removing the effect of length and turning a whole-text probability into a per-token quantity

N is the number of tokens the model predicted — so it includes </s> and excludes <s>.

The same rule that made V=10 rather than 11 last session. Our four-sentence corpus has 26 such tokens. If N is wrong, every perplexity below is wrong and nothing will say so.

Perplexity is a branching factor

Roughly, the number of choices the model was effectively selecting between at each step.

A model that treats all 10 tokens as equally likely

PP=((110)N)−1/N=10

Ten, because at every position it picks blindly among ten options. A model scoring 4 has cut ten options to about four; a model scoring 1 named the next word exactly, every time.

This is a reference point, not an upper bound — a model can do worse than 10 by being confidently wrong. Guessing uniformly is only the performance obtainable without effort.

This is why perplexity is a weighted branching factor.

A raw branching factor counts the options. Perplexity weights them by probability, so a model that formally allows ten words but concentrates on one scores low.

Four measurements, from the corpus we know

<s> I want to eat Indian food </s>
<s> I want to eat Chinese food </s>
<s> I want Indian food </s>

The four numbers that follow build a single argument rather than four separate examples.

The trained bigram model, on a training sentence

PP=(13)−1/7=31/7=1.17

1.17 against a reference of 10 does not mean an excellent model. It means the model was scored on its own training data and reproduced it.

This is how contamination presents in practice — not as an obvious error but as an implausibly good result. A perplexity that looks too good usually means a flawed experiment.

A sentence it has not seen, of words it does know

P(I want Chinese food)=34×33×03×11×44=0

Which makes the perplexity a division by zero

PP=0−1/5=10=∞

Session 13's zero problem, with its consequence made explicit: an unsmoothed model cannot be evaluated at all.

One unseen pair anywhere in a million-word test set sends the perplexity of the whole set to infinity. Without smoothing there is no number to report.

Perplexity is normally computed over a whole test set: all its sentence probabilities multiplied together, with N the total token count.

Single sentences appear here only because four training sentences are all we have. A one-sentence test set is far too small to be representative.

Read as branching factors: no context means choosing among a thousand; one previous word cuts it to 170; two cut it to 109.

The table measures what would otherwise be an assertion, and it shows the returns diminishing: the first word of context is worth far more than the second.

The trend cannot continue. A 10-gram model would have almost no counts. More context helps only while there is data enough to estimate it.

Three ways perplexity can mislead

  • Only comparable on the same test set and vocabulary. A weak model on easy text beats a strong model on hard text
  • <UNK> can be gamed. Shrink the vocabulary, replace most words with <UNK>, and perplexity drops sharply while the model becomes less useful
  • Better perplexity does not guarantee a better system. A model can predict slightly better on average without changing a single decision the recogniser makes

Use perplexity routinely — it is fast and generally right about large differences.

Then confirm the decision that matters with an end-to-end test. A figure is meaningful only reported alongside its vocabulary size and its <UNK> policy.

Exercise

Session 12's two sentences

I want Indian food scored 1/4 = 0.250 and she wants to eat Indian food scored 1/6 = 0.167. By raw probability the first ranks higher. What does perplexity give? Take N as 5 and 7.

PP(I want Indian food)=(14)−1/5=41/5=1.320PP(she wants to eat Indian food)=(16)−1/7=61/7=1.292

Perplexity reverses the ranking. The longer sentence had the lower raw probability but the better per-token score — it simply had two more words to account for. Raw probability was partly measuring length, and perplexity divides that out.

Note what the −1/N is for: it lets one model be compared across test sets of different sizes. Perplexity measures models. It is not a way to decide which sentence is better English.

Question 1 of 3

A model gets perplexity 1.2 on the corpus it was trained on. What does that tell you?

Correct: almost nothing — it was scored on text it memorised.

Perplexity on training data measures recall, not prediction, and is always misleadingly low. It indicates a flawed experiment rather than a good model. The only figure worth reporting comes from held-out data.

Question 2 of 3

Why is the perplexity of an unsmoothed model on a real test set usually infinite?

Correct: one unseen n-gram makes P = 0, and perplexity inverts it.

The test-set probability is a product, so one zero anywhere zeroes the whole thing, and perplexity raises that to a negative power — division by zero. Any real test set contains unseen n-grams.

Question 3 of 3

Model A has perplexity 140 and model B has perplexity 95. Which is the better language model?

Correct: model B, if both were measured on the same test set with the same vocabulary.

Lower is better, so model B — but only under that condition. Perplexity depends on the text as well as the model, and shrinking the vocabulary lowers it without improving anything.

Summary

Perplexity is the test-set probability, inverted and normalised per token, so lower is better, and it reads as the average number of words the model was choosing between. On training data it looks excellent and means nothing; without smoothing it is infinite.

That completes the n-gram model: count, assume a short history, smooth, and measure.

Session 15 changes the question. Instead of asking how likely a piece of text is, we ask what it is about — is this review positive or negative?

The probability machinery carries over directly, aimed at a different target.
