Naïve Bayes for sentiment, worked through

Five short reviews, one new sentence, and every number done by hand. The training is nothing but counting, and the first attempt fails completely.

The classifier, with no numbers in it

cˆ=argmaxc∈CP(c)∏w∈dP(w|c)

Two quantities have to come from data: the prior P(c) and the likelihoods P(w|c). Both are counts.

There is no training loop and nothing to tune. We count, we divide, and the classifier is complete.

The prior divides by 5, the number of documents. The likelihood divides by 14 or 9, the number of words in that class.

Different questions, different denominators. Confusing them is the most common source of error in this calculation.

Both classes score exactly zero, so there is nothing to compare and the classifier cannot choose at all.

Nothing unusual has happened. Every word is ordinary and appears in the training data. They just did not all appear in both classes, which is expected with five short documents.

This failure has now appeared three times: session 12's zero-probability sentence, session 13's treatment of it, and here.

The cause is the same each time — a product is zero if any factor is zero, and estimation by counting produces zeros readily.

Session 13's correction, applied here

P(w|c)=count(w,c)+1total words in c+|V|

Session 13 objected to add-one on scale

A language model has V around 50,000, so add-one gave a bigram seen on every opportunity a probability of 0.008%. Here V is 20.

The correction is small, the values stay sensible, and add-one is what is actually used for Naïve Bayes in practice. The method has not changed; the vocabulary size has.

|V| is the distinct word types across both classes

and · boring · energy · entirely · few · film · fun · just · lacks · laughs
most · no · of · plain · powerful · predictable · summer · surprises · the · very

Twenty of them.

It is tempting to use the negative class's own vocabulary for negative likelihoods. That is wrong: both classes use the same |V|=20.

Two classes' scores are comparable only over the same set of possible words. Per-class V compares probabilities defined over different outcomes — and the numbers still look reasonable, which is what makes it hard to catch.

with appears nowhere in the training data, in either class. It is not in V, so there is nothing to smooth. The rule is to drop it.

A classifier only compares classes, and an unknown word contributes equally to every class, so it cannot change the winner. Dropping it is not an approximation.

A language model needed <UNK>, because it must assign the sentence a probability and cannot pretend part of it is absent.

The right treatment depends on what the model is for, not only on the problem it runs into.

A real review has two hundred words, not three

0.01 ^ 200   ->   0.0     (not "very small" — zero, in ordinary floating point)

This is underflow. It would zero every class again, this time for a purely arithmetic reason rather than a missing count.

Logarithms turn the product into a sum

cˆ=argmaxc∈Clog⁡P(c)+∑w∈dlog⁡P(w|c)

Our example, in natural logarithms

classlog prior+ log likelihoodstotal
−−0.51−2.83 − 2.83 − 3.53−9.70 ← larger
+−0.92−3.37 − 3.37 − 2.67−10.33

Moderate negative numbers, around −3 rather than 10⁻¹⁴⁷, so nothing underflows. The negative class again scores higher, as it must.

The logarithm is monotonic: if a > b then log a > log b, always. The winning class cannot change.

What is lost is the interpretable scale, and that was already given up in session 15 when P(d) was dropped. Every practical implementation works in log space — which is why predict_log_proba exists in scikit-learn.
Question 1 of 2

In add-one smoothing for Naïve Bayes, what is the |V| added to the denominator?

Correct: distinct word types across all classes together.

Both classes use the same |V| — 20 in our example. Give each class its own vocabulary and the two scores are computed over different sets of possible words. The numbers still look reasonable, which is why this error often goes unnoticed.

Question 2 of 2

Why does working in log space not change which class the classifier picks?

Correct: the log function is monotonic, so it preserves score order.

A larger number always has a larger logarithm, so the winner cannot change. Logarithms address underflow and are not a modelling decision. The last option is wrong: the two classes are not shifted by the same amount, and they do not need to be.

Summary

Training is two sets of counts: the prior from documents, the likelihoods from words within each class's combined text. Unsmoothed they produce zeros that leave both classes scoring nothing. Add-one fixes it, using the whole training vocabulary. Unknown words are dropped, and the products are computed as sums of logarithms.

This answers VADER's failure on nightmare: a classifier that learns from labelled examples rather than a hand-written lexicon.

Consider what we did to the documents: discard word order, keep a count for each word. We have not named that operation.

It is a way of representing a document as numbers, and it is not specific to classification — search, similarity and recommendation all need the same thing. Session 17 names it and develops it into a vector.
