MSIS · AME 5003 · Unit III · Session 27

Sequence-to-sequence: the encoder-decoder shape

Four sessions built a recurrent unit; this session leaves it alone. It is the first in Unit III about what is built out of two of them, and about a task that no improvement to the unit could have solved.

SLP3 writes g throughout §13.7 as "a stand-in for some flavor of RNN", and we do the same: the unit is a black box from here on.

This is the first session in Unit III about an architecture rather than a cell. Nothing below depends on which of the three units is inside the box.

SLP3's own pair

source (English) :  the    green   witch   arrived
target (Spanish) :  llegó  la      bruja   verde
glossed          :  arrived the    witch   green

Read the alignment off the gloss: 4, 1, 3, 2. Not monotonic, so no scheme that emits one output per input as it goes can produce it.

Everything in Unit III so far has been sequence labelling — one output per input — or sequence classification, one output at the end.

This example is unusually kind: in general the two sentences need not even be the same length, so there is no one-to-one correspondence to align in the first place.

Exercise

Session 6's POS tagger, asked to emit one Spanish word per English word

Give two separate reasons it cannot produce llegó la bruja verde.

One: the alignment is 4, 1, 3, 2. At the first position it would have to emit llegó, which comes from arrived — a word it has not read yet. A one-output-per-input model commits before the evidence arrives.

Two: the lengths are equal here by luck. "I don't know" is three words and "no sé" is two. A one-tag-per-token model emits exactly as many outputs as it read.

The second reason is the more fundamental. The first could in principle be patched by reading the whole sentence first; the second cannot, because it is a property of the shape.

Three parts

  • An encoder that takes the input x1:n and produces contextualised representations h1:n
  • A context vector c, a function of h1:n, which "conveys the essence of the input to the decoder"
  • A decoder that accepts c and generates an arbitrary-length sequence of hidden states, and from them the outputs

"Arbitrary-length" is the direct answer to the second reason above: the decoder stops when it decides to stop, not when the input runs out.

SLP3 is explicit that "LSTMs, convolutional networks, and transformers can all be employed as encoders", and the same for decoders. Nothing above says what is inside the boxes.

That is why this session survives session 31: when the transformer arrives it goes into this shape rather than replacing it, and session 34's machine translation reuses the same three components with a different unit inside them.

SLP3 does not build this from scratch

"We only have to make one slight change to turn this language model with autoregressive generation into an encoder-decoder model… add a sentence separation marker at the end of the source text, and then simply concatenate the target text."

The training example is one string

the green witch arrived <s> llegó la bruja verde
|__________ source _________|   |______ target ______|

Train an ordinary RNN language model on that. Everything before <s> is read; everything after it is generated.

A model that predicts the next word, given a string that happens to contain a separator, is a translation model.

Because predicting the words after the separator, given the words before it, is translating.

SLP3 starts §13.7 from the chain rule

p(y)=p(y1)p(y2|y1)p(y3|y1,y2)⋯p(ym|y1,…,ym−1)(13.28)

That is session 12's equation, unchanged. The n-gram models of Unit II factored a sentence's probability exactly this way, and made a Markov assumption to make the conditionals tractable.

Here the conditioning context is a hidden state instead, so no truncation is needed — but the factorisation is the one the course has used for fifteen sessions.

Per step, with g a stand-in for whichever unit you chose

ht=g(ht−1,xt)(13.29)
yˆt=softmax(ht)(13.30)

The context vector is the encoder's final hidden state

c=hne

Initialised from it, h0d=c, then

htd=g(yˆt−1,ht−1d,c)(13.32)

The decoder's state depends on the word it just produced, its own previous state, and c again. The context vector is not only used to start the decoder — it is supplied at every decoding step.

SLP3 says why: passing c to every step ensures "that the influence of the context vector, c, doesn't wane as the output sequence is generated". Left to the recurrence alone the source would fade — sessions 24 and 25's problem, for a third time in a new place.

Wide on both sides, narrow in the middle

The encoder is n states wide and the decoder m, and n need not equal m. Between them sits one state.

Every word of the source has to reach the decoder through it. The four fanned arrows are equation (13.32), c at every step rather than only the first. The pinch in the middle is the shape of this session's closing problem.
Question 1 of 2

What is the context vector c in this architecture?

Correct: the encoder's final hidden state. Earlier source words reach the decoder only through what survived the chain into that last state.

The first is a productive error worth remembering, because it is nearly what session 28 will do. The fourth is closer still and equally not this: keeping all n states is exactly what the fixed-size c refuses to do, and the refusal is the point.

Training is end-to-end on paired strings, and the loss is the average cross-entropy per target word — session 20's loss, unchanged.

The optimisation is unchanged. The training procedure, however, has one feature worth examining closely.

What the decoder is fed at step t

input to decoder step tconsequence
trainingthe gold word yt−1 every step is conditioned on a correct prefix
inferencethe model's own yˆt−1 errors compound as generation continues

Feeding the gold word during training is teacher forcing. SLP3 notes the consequence at inference: the decoder "will tend to deviate more and more from the gold target sentence as it keeps generating". One wrong word early, and everything after is conditioned on a mistake.

The model is trained in a condition it never encounters when used.

It has only ever seen correct prefixes, and at inference it will routinely be standing on its own errors.

One response is to narrow the gap during training: feed the gold word most of the time and the model's own output some of the time, so it gets practice at recovering.

That is scheduled sampling. You meet it in lab 10, where the decoder takes a sample_probability argument controlling exactly this mixture.
Question 2 of 2

What is teacher forcing?

Correct: the gold word is used as the decoder's input during training, keeping every step conditioned on a correct prefix.

The second is the one to rule out firmly. At inference there is no gold target — that is the thing being produced. If you had it, you would not need the model. The third describes a bidirectional encoder, a real technique and not this one.

c is a vector of fixed size — take d=300

source lengthnumbers entering the encodersize of c compression
4 words1,2003004 : 1
10 words3,00030010 : 1

50 words15,00030050 : 1

A fifty-word sentence is compressed more than twelve times harder than a four-word one, and nothing in the architecture notices. There is no mechanism that allocates more room, or that reports having run out.

Every word of the source must reach every word of the target through one fixed-size vector.

Whatever did not survive the encoder's chain into hne is not available to the decoder at all — not attenuated, not harder to reach, simply absent. The longer the source, the more that is.

The problem is real, and it is a consequence of the shape rather than of the unit inside it.

It has a fix — which is session 28, and which begins by quoting the paragraph of SLP3 that starts exactly here.

Summary

Sequence labelling cannot translate: the alignment need not be monotonic, and the lengths need not match. The encoder-decoder is a different shape — encoder, context vector, decoder — and it is a shape rather than a model. The construction is a small change to a language model we already had: separator, concatenate, train next-word.

Teacher forcing trains it in a condition it never meets in use. And c is one fixed-size vector however long the source: at d=300, fifty words squeezed 50 : 1 where four are squeezed 4 : 1.
