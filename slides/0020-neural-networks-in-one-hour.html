Neural networks in one hour

Nothing in this session is usable on its own, which is deliberate. It supplies the machinery session 21 requires: five ideas, each selected because Word2Vec uses it.

Sessions 17 to 19 built vectors by counting and weighting. Every step could be done by hand and every session ended with a usable method.

Session 21 learns its vectors. Nothing is counted into them: they start as random numbers and are adjusted until they do well on a prediction task.

Nothing here is usable on its own — no method, no dataset, nothing to run.

It exists so that no step in session 21 is unexplained.

Lab 7 left two baselines on the table

0.809 accuracy from raw counts, 0.823 from sublinear TF-IDF, on 2,000 real movie reviews. That is what counting and weighting achieve.

An hour of prerequisites is justified only if learned vectors improve on 0.823 — and that is a question we can answer by measurement rather than assertion.

Session 16's classifier, in log space

cˆ=argmaxc∈Clog⁡P(c)+∑w∈dlog⁡P(w|c)

And the object underlying every neural model to come

score=b+∑iwixi

log⁡P(c) is a constant added regardless of the document — that is b. Each log⁡P(w|c) is a fixed number attached to a word, times how often it occurs — that is wixi.

Naïve Bayes in log space is a weighted sum of features. We built one in session 16 without calling it a neural network.

Naïve Bayes' weights are counted. A neuron's weights are fitted.

Session 16 counted and divided, and the weights were fixed. A neuron's weights start arbitrary and are adjusted until the model predicts well.

Counting cannot detect that a feature is useless; fitting can drive its weight to zero. Counting cannot detect that two features matter only in combination; fitting a second layer can.

A weighted sum, a constant, and one function

y=f(∑iwixi+b)

A neuron, drawn

That is a neuron: x are the inputs, w the weights, b the bias, and f the activation function — the one non-linear step.

The sum inside is often written z and called the pre-activation. Everything left of the split is the arithmetic from session 16; the function on the right is the only addition.

Session 16 could not. It counted the in both classes and carried two nearly equal log terms for ever, which is why session 4 was spent on stop-word lists. Fitted weights can make some preprocessing unnecessary.

The sigmoid

σ(z)=11+e−z

The sigmoid maps any real number into (0,1): large positive z near 1, large negative near 0, and σ(0)=0.5 exactly.

That is why its output reads as a probability, and why we introduce it now — session 21's negative sampling scores a word pair as σ(u⋅v), this function applied to a dot product.

A stack of linear layers is one linear layer

layer 1:h=W1xlayer 2:y=W2h=W2(W1x)=(W2W1)x=Wx

W2W1 is simply another matrix. Three layers collapse the same way, and so do thirty.

Without a non-linearity, depth provides nothing — a hundred-layer linear network computes what one layer computes. The activation function is the only reason depth is meaningful.

A shallow model, deliberately

Word2Vec has no hidden non-linearity. CBOW averages some vectors, does one matrix multiply, and applies a softmax.

The collapse argument is about depth, and Word2Vec is not deep. Being shallow is what lets it train on a billion words on modest hardware. A sigmoid does appear, in negative sampling — not as a hidden layer, but to turn one dot product into one probability.

The word vectors are a weight matrix

E is a layer's weights, fitted by the same procedure as any other weights. When session 21 says "the word vectors", it means the rows of E.

There is no separate step where vectors are extracted from the model. The vectors are part of the model.

Ten thousand scores in, ten thousand probabilities out

softmax(z)i=ezi∑jezj

Exponentiate, then divide by the total

Everything comes out positive, because ez is positive whatever z was. And everything sums to 1, because we divided by the total. That is softmax, and what it produces is a distribution.

bank beat river by 2.0 on the scores, a modest margin. In probability it beats it by a factor of 7.4.

Not specific to these numbers: a gap of 2 always becomes a ratio of e2=7.39, and a gap of 5 a ratio of 148. Softmax turns small differences in score into large differences in probability — which is what the name refers to.

Exercise — calculator allowed

Three scores

z=[2,1,0]. What distribution does softmax give? And which is bigger — the gap between the first two probabilities, or between the last two?

e2=7.389, e1=2.718, e0=1.000, summing to 11.107. Dividing: 0.665, 0.245, 0.090.

The scores are evenly spaced, one step apart, and the probabilities are not: the first gap is 0.420, the second only 0.155.

Both gaps are the same ratio — 0.665/0.245≈0.245/0.090≈2.718, which is e1. The same ratio near the top of a distribution is far more absolute probability than near the bottom.

Cross-entropy loss, for a single prediction

loss=−log⁡(probability the model gave the correct answer)

The whole definition, applied

if the correct word waswe saidloss
bank 0.8360.179 — nearly right, small penalty
river 0.1132.180
money 0.0512.980 — badly wrong, large penalty

A probability of exactly 0 would give infinite loss, which is appropriate — and is the same reason session 13 could not tolerate a zero.

Session 14's perplexity was the exponentiated average of exactly this quantity.

Cross-entropy loss is not new machinery. It is what was inside perplexity all along, used directly rather than expressed as a branching factor. Minimising one is minimising the other.

Think of the loss as a surface: every setting of the weights is a position, and the loss is the height. We want the lowest point.

We cannot see the surface — it has hundreds of thousands of dimensions and we occupy one point. But at any point we can compute the gradient: which way is uphill, and how steeply.

Step the other way, and repeat

w←w−η⋅(gradient of the loss with respect to w)

The steps shrink on their own as the curve flattens

η=0.2, loss (w−3)2 — checkable by hand

It never exactly arrives, and does not need to. η is the learning rate: too small and convergence crawls, too large and each step overshoots.

Real training differs in scale, not in kind: millions of weights instead of one, so the gradient is millions of numbers updated together, and the loss is averaged over a batch.

The line w←w−η⋅gradient is gradient descent, and it trains every model in the rest of the course.

Backpropagation

Getting the gradient of a weight buried deep in a network means differentiating a function composed of many others. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied systematically, working backwards from the loss.

That much is enough here. SLP3 §6.6 derives it properly; no session in this course needs it by hand, and PyTorch does it automatically by exactly this procedure.
Question 1 of 2

Why does stacking layers without a non-linearity between them gain you nothing?

Correct: the stack collapses.

W2(W1x)=(W2W1)x, and that is simply another matrix. A hundred linear layers compute what one computes.

But note what this does not say. It is an argument about depth, and shallow models are respectable: Word2Vec has no hidden non-linearity at all, and being shallow is what lets it train on a billion words.

Question 2 of 2

Next session trains a model and then hands you "the word vectors". Which part of the trained model is the word vectors?

Correct: the weight matrix of the first layer.

There is no extraction step and no afterwards. E is 10000 × 300 and is fitted by the same gradient descent as everything else. When training stops you throw away the rest of the network and keep that matrix.

The softmax outputs are wrong for a simple reason: they are a distribution produced for one particular context and change with every input. A word vector has to be a fixed property of the word.

A sentence from session 21

"CBOW averages the context word vectors, multiplies by an output matrix, applies softmax, and does gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss."

Term by term

the phrasewhat it means, from today
the context word vectorsRows of the embedding matrix E — which is a layer's weight matrix. The vectors are weights.
averagesPull the rows for the surrounding words, add, divide by how many. One 300-number vector out.
multiplies by an output matrixA layer: z=W′x. 300 numbers in, 10,000 scores out.
applies softmaxThose 10,000 scores become 10,000 probabilities, positive and summing to 1.
cross-entropy loss−log⁡ of the probability given to the word actually there.
gradient descentw←w−η⋅gradient, applied to every weight — including every number in E.

The word vectors are not an output produced at the end of training. They are parameters being trained, moved slightly on every example until words appearing in similar contexts have similar vectors.

No information about meaning was supplied to the model.

A great deal was omitted: backpropagation is named rather than derived, and there is nothing here on optimisers, regularisation, dropout, batching, or how a learning rate is chosen.

The softmax over 10,000 words is also extremely expensive, which is why session 21 spends much of its time avoiding it. What this session establishes is enough to follow session 21 with no unexplained step, and that is all it sets out to do.

Summary

A neuron is a weighted sum plus a bias through one non-linear function — session 16's score, with fitted weights instead of counted ones. Stacking turns the sums into Wx+b, and the non-linearity stops the stack collapsing. Softmax makes any scores into a distribution, exaggerating as it goes.

Training is one line: measure error as −log⁡ of the probability given to the correct answer, then repeatedly apply w←w−η⋅gradient.
