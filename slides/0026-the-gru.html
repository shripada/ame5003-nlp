The GRU: two gates, one vector, and what the simplification cost

Session 25 ended by asking whether three gates and eight weight matrices were all necessary. The GRU is one answer. This session works out what it removed, what that costs in arithmetic, and what the evidence says about the trade.

Where we stopped

"Three gates and eight weight matrices, and the unit does one job: manage a context vector. Is all of it necessary? Could you get the same behaviour with fewer gates?"

The gated recurrent unit is one answer. Cho et al., 2014 — seventeen years after the LSTM, which is later than most people guess.

Start with the subtraction

Two things are gone: the separate context vector, and the output gate. Forget and add are merged into one update gate z, used once as z and once as 1−z.

This is a simplification of the LSTM, and it is fair to present it that way.

What matters is what the simplification cost, and that is a question the arithmetic answers rather than the description.

Cho et al. 2014, with the paper's own numbering

rt=σ(Wrxt+Urht−1)(5)
zt=σ(Wzxt+Uzht−1)(6)
ht=zt⊙ht−1+(1−zt)⊙h~t(7)
h~t=tanh⁡(Wxt+U(rt⊙ht−1))(8)

Read (7) aloud

ht is a weighted average of the previous state and the new candidate, and the two weights always sum to 1.

So z is a dial. At z=1 the unit keeps the old state exactly; at z=0 it throws it away and takes the candidate whole; in between it blends.

The LSTM's forget and add gates were two independent decisions.

Here there is one dial, and moving it toward keeping is the same act as moving it away from writing.

In (8), rt multiplies ht−1 before the candidate is computed. So the reset gate decides how much of the past the candidate is even allowed to see.

With r≈0 the candidate is computed from xt alone and the unit effectively starts fresh at this word, whatever it was carrying.

r controls what goes into the candidate. z controls how much of the candidate comes out.

Note that the gate which does the forgetting is z, not r. The reset gate does not erase memory — it hides memory from one calculation.

Sources disagree. In this course z multiplies the previous state, as in Cho's (7). PyTorch writes the same equation with the terms reversed, ht=(1−z)nt+zht−1, which agrees.

The flipped convention, with z gating the candidate instead, circulates widely. Check which one a source is using before comparing numbers with it.

Cost 1 — the GRU cannot remember without acting

The same number twice, because there is only one number. With no output gate and no context vector, retention is output — anything the GRU holds on to, it is also saying.

Session 25 treated the LSTM's ability to hold something silently as the core of the architecture, and the GRU does not have it.

In the LSTM, f=1 with i=1 keeps the old context and adds to it. Here the weights sum to 1 by construction: keeping 90% means writing at most 10%. There is no setting that does both.

ht can never leave the segment between the two

That constraint is what "the weights sum to 1" means geometrically, and it is exactly the constraint the LSTM does not have — with independent f and i, its context can hold what it had and take on more.

Neither cost is a defect. They are what the simplification bought. Whether the trade was worth making is answered by evidence, not argument.

Exercise

The LSTM held 0.7298 while emitting 0.0623

What values of z and h~ would make a GRU do the same — carry roughly 0.73 forward while emitting almost nothing?

There are none. ht is both the memory and the output, so "carry 0.73 forward" and "emit almost nothing" are contradictory. No choice of z, h~ or r separates them, because the separation is structural and the GRU does not have the structure.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not mean. The GRU can still retain information over many steps — z≈1 does that perfectly well. What it cannot do is retain information quietly.

Question 1 of 2

What did the GRU remove, relative to the LSTM?

Correct: the separate context vector and the output gate. Four matrix pairs become three, two vectors become one.

The second is the instructive wrong answer: the gate that forgets is z, not r. The reset gate controls what the candidate may see, a job the LSTM had no equivalent of. The first is false — (8) does compute a candidate and does read ht−1, filtered through r.

75% of the LSTM's parameters. Worth stating plainly, because it is smaller than the reputation suggests — "simpler architecture" tends to be heard as "much smaller model", and a quarter fewer is real but not dramatic.

Whether it costs accuracy

"The evaluation clearly demonstrated the superiority of the gated units; both the LSTM unit and GRU, over the traditional tanh unit… However, we could not make concrete conclusion on which of the two gating units was better."

Chung, Gulcehre, Cho and Bengio. Two of the four are authors of the GRU paper itself.

The people who proposed the GRU compared it against the LSTM and declined to claim a winner.

Session 18 checked idf against the Shakespeare features. Session 21 checked king−man+woman and found it landing on king. Session 22 checked GloVe's pitch against Levy & Goldberg. Here the check is one the authors ran themselves.

Four times a well-known claim has been checked against the evidence and come back weaker than advertised. The habit is more durable than any of the four results.

Try both, prefer the GRU when data or compute is tight, and do not expect the choice to decide anything.

In PyTorch it is a one-word change, which is worth doing in lab 9 to see the difference for yourself.

PyTorch's own documentation says so: "The calculation of new gate nt subtly differs from the original paper… the Hadamard product between rt and ht−1 is done before the multiplication with the weight matrix W…"

Cho applies the reset gate before the matrix; PyTorch after

Cho (8):h~=tanh⁡(Wx+U(r⊙ht−1))PyTorch:nt=tanh⁡(Winx+bin+r⊙(Whnht−1+bhn))

These are not algebraically equal. In Cho's form the gate masks the state and the matrix mixes what survives; in PyTorch's the matrix mixes everything and the mask is applied after. PyTorch says the change is deliberate, for efficiency.

It matters here because the course's own assigned source runs the PyTorch variant. Rao & McMahan call nn.GRU and never write the equations out — so a student who implements (5)–(8) by hand and compares is running two different architectures, and neither source says so.

A footnote in Cho's paper describes the LSTM as having four gating units. Session 25 taught three, following SLP3, and said g is the candidate content rather than a gate.

The equations are identical either way — the sources simply count the candidate differently. Nothing about the architecture is in dispute; only the noun is.
Question 2 of 2

What does the empirical evidence say about choosing between the LSTM and the GRU?

Correct: gating clearly wins, and neither gated unit consistently beats the other — Chung et al.'s conclusion in their own words, with two GRU authors on the paper.

The first is exactly the claim the evidence does not support, and the one most people carry: "simpler" is a hypothesis, not an argument, and this one was tested. The third is a plausible structural argument that did not show up as a consistent accuracy gap. The fourth over-corrects — the datasets are a caveat worth noting, not a reason to discard the finding.

What neither of them fixes

  • Both are strictly sequential. ht needs ht−1, so neither parallelises across time whatever hardware is available
  • Both push everything through one vector per position. Fewer gates does not change how much can be carried

Session 27 attacks neither limit. It keeps the sequential unit exactly as it is and changes what gets built out of it: two recurrent networks, one reading and one writing.

That is the shape that makes translation possible — and it is where Cho's paper reappears, since these equations come from §2.3 of a paper whose actual subject is the encoder-decoder. Bahdanau, session 28's author, is a co-author of it.

Summary

The GRU removes the separate context vector and the output gate. r decides how much of the past the candidate may see; z interpolates between the previous state and the candidate with weights summing to 1. Two costs follow from the arithmetic: it cannot remember without acting, and keeping and writing are coupled.

In exchange, 540,000 parameters against 720,000 — 75%. On accuracy, Chung et al. could not separate the two. And PyTorch's nn.GRU is not Cho's equation (8).
