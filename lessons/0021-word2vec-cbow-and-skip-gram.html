The distributional hypothesis

The idea underlying all of this is older than any of the machinery, and is due to J. R. Firth in 1957:

Definition

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Words occurring in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. This is the entire premise, and every embedding method in this course depends on it.

We have already seen this hold. Session 19 ended with the following table, giving counts of how often each word appears near three others:

piedatacomputer
cherry 4428 2
digital 5 16831670
information5 39823325
cos(cherry, information)   =  0.018
cos(digital, information)  =  0.996

No information about meaning was supplied. The counts record which words appeared nearby, and cosine ordered the results correctly.

One point should be established before we continue, as it prevents confusion later. Those vectors have three dimensions, chosen to illustrate the point. With only three axes, two words that both load on data and computer have very little room in which to differ, so the cosine is high at 0.996. The 300-dimensional vectors built in this session distribute similarity over far more room and do not produce values of that kind. A cosine of 0.996 in one representation and 0.996 in another are not equivalent claims, and later in this session we measure how differently the two representations behave.

The premise therefore already works. What Word2Vec changes is the verb.

What this session adds

Every method in Unit II so far has counted the company a word keeps — bag-of-words, TF-IDF, that table above. Word2Vec predicts it instead.

Rather than counting the context, we predict it, and keep whatever the model had to learn in order to predict well.

The prediction task is a pretext

The task Word2Vec trains on is as follows. Take a sentence, delete a word, and ask the model to identify what was deleted:

a  great  _____  about  hope

The task itself is of no practical use. Nobody requires a system that fills in blanks in sentences whose answers are already known, and the task is not the reason for building the model.

The reason is what a model must become in order to perform the task well. To predict film from that context, the model must have arranged its internal numbers so that words appearing in similar company have similar numbers, because that is the only structure in the data that helps. The prediction is a pretext. The weights are the product.

No labelled data is required

Session 16 trained a sentiment classifier and needed 2,000 reviews that somebody had read and tagged as positive or negative. That labelling is the expensive part of supervised learning, and it is why good labelled datasets are rare.

Word2Vec needs none. The label — the word that was actually there — is already in the text. Delete it, ask for it back, and check. Every sentence ever written is training data.The modern name for this is self-supervised learning: the supervision signal is manufactured from the input itself. It is how every large language model in Unit III is trained, at enormous scale, and Word2Vec is the first example of it you will meet.

This is why the approach became dominant: not because it is more sophisticated, but because the quantity of available training data is effectively unlimited.

CBOW

CBOW stands for Continuous Bag of Words, and it is the sentence from last session, made concrete. Take our example with a window of ±2 — two words each side of the target:

a  great  [film]  about  hope
└──────┘         └──────────┘
 context           context          target = film

"Bag of words" appears in the name for the usual reason: the four context words enter as an unordered set. a great and great a give exactly the same input, because they are about to be averaged.

The pipeline follows, with session 20's shapes unchanged, for a vocabulary of 10,000 and vectors of 300 numbers:

what it isshapenote
Eembedding matrix10000 × 300one row per word in the vocabulary
xaveraged context300mean of a few rows of E — one vector
W′output matrix10000 × 300one row per word again, on the way out
zscores10000z=W′x, one score per vocabulary word
pprobabilities10000p=softmax(z)

Four steps, all of them taught last session:

  1. Look up. Pull the four rows of E belonging to a, great, about and hope. Four vectors of 300 numbers.
  2. Average. Add them and divide by four. One vector of 300 numbers, called x. This is the "averages the context word vectors" in the sentence.
  3. One layer, then softmax. z=W′x gives a score for every word in the vocabulary — 10,000 of them. Softmax turns those into 10,000 probabilities that are positive and sum to 1.
  4. Loss, then descend. Cross-entropy: −log⁡ of the probability the model gave to film, the word that was actually there. Then w←w−η⋅gradient on every weight in the model.
Which weights are updated

All of them — including the four rows of E we just looked up. Those rows are weights, exactly as session 20 flagged, so gradient descent adjusts them like anything else.

That is the entire training loop. The window slides one word along and the process repeats. After a few billion repetitions, the rows of E have been adjusted into an arrangement in which words used in similar company point in similar directions. No definition of any word was ever supplied.

Skip-gram

Skip-gram reverses the arrows. One centre word goes in, and the model predicts the words around it — each context word as its own separate prediction:

The final sentence of that caption states the practical difference between the two, and it determines which is used in practice.

Choosing between CBOW and skip-gram

Skip-gram is the usual choice. It produces four training examples from a window where CBOW produces one, so the same corpus provides more signal, and the additional signal matters most for rare words, which occur too infrequently for CBOW to learn much about them.

The cost is time, since there are four predictions per position rather than one. CBOW is faster and performs slightly better on frequent words, because averaging four context vectors reduces noise.

Both appear in the same 2013 paper and both are available in every implementation. In the absence of a specific reason to prefer CBOW, use Skip-gram.

Each word has two vectors

Returning to the shapes given above, E has one row per vocabulary word and so does W′. These are two distinct sets of numbers for the same word, and both are trained:

vectorwrittenused when
word w's input vectorvw=row w of Ew appears as context
word w's output vectoruw=row w of W′w is the thing being predicted

The score the model assigns to word w is uw⋅x, the dot product of w's output vector with the averaged context. Every word therefore plays two roles, and the model maintains separate numbers for each.

As promised in session 20

Last session's second quiz said: when training stops, "you throw away the rest of the network and keep that matrix". This is the rest of the network. W' is discarded.

The word vectors in general use, including those in every downloadable Word2Vec file, are the rows of E. The output vectors serve their purpose during training and are then discarded. Some implementations offer to average the two sets together; this is rarely done.

The cost of the softmax

Everything above is correct and none of it is practical to run. The difficulty is the softmax denominator.

Converting scores into probabilities requires dividing by the sum over the whole vocabulary. Making one prediction, and therefore adjusting the weights by one training example, requires a score for every word in the vocabulary. With realistic figures:

vocabulary1,000,000 words
corpus1,000,000,000 tokens (a small corpus, by 2013 standards)
exponentials per example106
training examples109 (CBOW; Skip-gram makes about ten times more)
per pass over the corpus1015 exponentials

At a billion exponentials per second, which is a generous rate, 1015 exponentials require about 11.6 days for one pass over a corpus that was considered small at the time. Mikolov's paper trained on 100 billion words.

Note where the cost lies. It is not in producing the answer but in normalising it. We want information about one word, and softmax requires scoring a million to obtain it.

Negative sampling

The solution is not a faster softmax but a different question.

Instead of asking which of a million words belongs in this position, which is one very large decision, we ask whether a given pair is real, which is a binary decision, and ask it a small number of times:

real pair:        (film, about)      ← actually appeared together    → say YES
invented pairs:   (film, kubernetes)                                 → say NO
                  (film, wheat)                                      → say NO
                  (film, thirteen)                                   → say NO
                  ...  k of them, drawn at random

The model now has only to distinguish the true pair from k invented ones. The scoring function is a single dot product mapped into a probability:

P(this pair is real)=σ(u⋅v)where σ(z)=11+e−z

Train it to push σ(u⋅v) towards 1 for the real pair and σ(−u⋅v) towards 1 for each invented one — which is to say, push the true pair's vectors together and the fake pairs' apart.The negatives are not drawn uniformly. Mikolov samples them from the unigram frequency distribution raised to the power 3/4, which pulls rare words up and common ones down relative to their raw frequency. The exponent was found by trying some; the paper says as much. Typical k is 5 to 20 for a small corpus, 2 to 5 for a large one.

The sigmoid from session 20

Session 20 showed the sigmoid and then said, in a warn box, that it would appear here — and that it would not be a hidden activation. Here it is: one dot product turned into one probability. Word2Vec still has no hidden non-linearity anywhere. It is a shallow model, and that is what makes it fast enough to be useful.

What that buys, in the same units as before:

arithmetic per exampleper pass over 10⁹ tokensat 10⁹ ops/sec
full softmax10⁶ exponentials10¹⁵11.6 days
negative sampling, k=56 sigmoids6 × 10⁹6 seconds

This is roughly 167,000 times less arithmetic, and the resulting vectors are as good or better. This is why Word2Vec had the influence it did.

Exercise

Here is a sentence and a window of ±2:

the  cat  sat  on  the  mat

Take on as the target. What exactly is the input to CBOW, and what is the target? How many training examples does Skip-gram get out of the same position?

Two words each side of on gives the context cat, sat, the, mat.

CBOW: input is the average of those four words' rows of E — one vector of 300 numbers. Target is on. That is one training example.

Skip-gram: input is on's row of E, and there are four separate training examples — (on → cat), (on → sat), (on → the), (on → mat). Same position, four times the signal.

Note that the context is a set. CBOW would produce an identical input if the sentence read sat cat on mat the, because averaging destroys order. This is the "bag of words" in the name, and the same trade made in session 17.

Question 1 of 2

Word2Vec is trained to predict a missing word from its context. What do we actually keep when training finishes?

Correct answer: the embedding matrix.

The prediction task is a pretext. Nobody needs a machine that fills in blanks in sentences we already have. We set that task because a model can only do it well by arranging its weights so that words used in similar company end up with similar numbers — and those weights are the thing we came for.

The output matrix W' is thrown away with the rest of the network. And note that Word2Vec is not a language model in session 11's sense: it has no notion of word order inside the window, so it cannot tell you the probability of a sentence.

Question 2 of 2

What problem does negative sampling solve, and how?

Correct answer: it replaces the vocabulary-wide softmax with a handful of binary decisions.

The cost was never in producing the answer — it was in normalising. Softmax divides by a sum over every word you know, so a single training example costs a million exponentials on a realistic vocabulary. Negative sampling asks "is this pair real?" against the true word and k random fakes: six sigmoids instead of a million exponentials, about 167,000 times less arithmetic.

The third option describes something real but different — the 3/4-power sampling distribution, which is how negatives are drawn, not what the technique is for.

A limitation of the distributional hypothesis

The reason is straightforward. "The film was good" and "the film was bad" occur in the same positions in the same kinds of sentence. Antonyms appear in nearly identical company, which is what makes them antonyms rather than unrelated words, so a method that observes only company cannot separate them.

These vectors encode distribution, not meaning. That is usually close enough to meaning to be useful, and occasionally exactly wrong. This is the reason averaged word vectors turn out to be a mediocre sentiment feature, which lab 8 measures directly.

The king−man+woman analogy

This result is widely reported. Word vectors are said to support a form of arithmetic: take king, subtract man, add woman, and the resulting vector is queen, with gender behaving as a direction in the space.

Querying the vectors as the standard tools do gives:

most_similar(positive=['king', 'woman'], negative=['man'])

    queen           0.7118
    monarch         0.6190
    princess        0.5902
    crown_prince    0.5499
    prince          0.5377

The result holds: out of 300,000 words, from a model given no information about royalty or gender, queen ranks first.

The usual account omits one detail. That function removes the three input words from the candidates before ranking them. Constructing the same vector directly and ranking the whole vocabulary with nothing excluded gives:

The nearest word to king−man+woman is king, by a clear margin. This follows from the arithmetic: we began at king and made a modest adjustment, so the result remains nearest to its starting point. queen is the nearest word that is not one of the inputs.

What the analogy does and does not show

The result is genuine. Out of 300,000 words, second place is queen and the next three are monarch, princess, and crown_prince. That neighbourhood was not arranged by anyone and did not have to come out correctly. It is a substantial property of vectors trained only to predict missing words.

The usual description overstates it. The vector does not equal queen. It lands near king, within a neighbourhood of royal terms, and queen is the closest answer that is not an input. That is a weaker claim than exact arithmetic, and a more informative one.

It is worth establishing whether the first example was representative. Three further analogies are shown, each computed both ways:

One of the three places the expected answer first. The other two place it second, behind a word from the correct neighbourhood: Milan in place of Rome, and larger in place of smaller. Note that bigger−big+small comes out closest to larger, which is the opposite of the intended direction while still being the correct region of the space.

The analogy behaviour is therefore real but approximate, and describing it as arithmetic overstates it. This is the resolution these vectors provide.

A correction to session 19

Session 19 made a claim about embeddings that these vectors allow us to check, and the claim turns out to be half correct. It stated that, unlike count vectors, whose cosines run from 0 to 1, embeddings "have negative components, and their cosines really do go down to −1".

The first part is correct and the second is not. Taking 4,000 random words and every cosine between them, about sixteen million pairs:

mean cosine            +0.066
median                 +0.058
1st percentile         -0.079
99th percentile        +0.291
minimum found          -0.270
fraction negative       17.4%

Negative cosines do occur, so the lower bound of 0 that applies to count vectors is genuinely absent. No value approaches −1, however. Searching all 300,000 words for the vector most opposed to good gives −0.185, and for king, −0.202.

The correct statement

Trained embeddings do not spread over the whole sphere. They sit in a fairly narrow cone, and almost every pair of words is slightly positively aligned. The formula's range is −1 to 1; the data's range is roughly −0.3 to 1.

This matters in practice. With count vectors, a cosine of 0.3 was a weak signal; here it is above the 99th percentile and indicates unusual similarity. A cosine is interpretable only against the distribution of cosines that the representation actually produces.

What this does not fix

There are two limitations, both of which persist through the next several sessions.

Session 3's vocabulary problem is unaffected. A word absent from the training corpus has no row in E and therefore no vector at all, rather than a poor one. These vectors were trained in 2013:

podcast    in vocab          COVID        NOT IN VOCAB
tweeted    in vocab          selfie       NOT IN VOCAB
smartphone in vocab          blockchain   NOT IN VOCAB
                             emoji        NOT IN VOCAB

And each word gets exactly one vector, whatever it means. Ask for the neighbours of bank:

bank  →  banks 0.744, banking 0.690, Bank 0.670, lender 0.634, banker 0.609, depositors 0.603

Every one of these is the financial sense. The river sense is not ranked lower but absent, because there is a single vector and the more frequent sense has taken all of it. The same applies to bat, whose neighbours are batsman, batting, and wicket_keeper, with nothing relating to the animal.

One word, one vector, no matter how many things the word means. Fixing that is what contextual embeddings do, and that is Unit III.

Where Unit II stands

We now have dense vectors, learned rather than counted, which place similar words near each other well enough to be useful, and which also confuse antonyms, contain nothing after 2013, and assign bank a single meaning.

Session 20 opened by quoting lab 7's two baselines: 0.809 from raw counts and 0.823 from sublinear TF-IDF, on 2,000 movie reviews. The question is whether these vectors improve on that.

The current answer

This is not yet established, and the answer is not obvious. Lab 8 measures it.

There is a specific reason for doubt, already visible above: good and bad have a cosine of 0.719. Averaging the word vectors of a review causes the positive and negative words to partly cancel, which is not a problem TF-IDF has, since TF-IDF keeps them as separate features. A better representation of words is not automatically a better representation of documents.

Summary

The main points

Word2Vec sets up a task nobody wants solved — guess the missing word from its neighbours — and keeps the weights the model needed in order to do it, which means it trains on raw text with no labelling at all. CBOW averages the context vectors and predicts the centre word; Skip-gram reverses the arrows and predicts each context word from the centre, getting more training examples out of the same text and doing better on rare words. The softmax over a whole vocabulary makes either one impossible to train, so negative sampling replaces that one huge decision with a few yes/no ones — σ(u⋅v) against the true pair and k invented ones — for roughly a hundred-thousandth of the arithmetic. What comes out genuinely captures which words behave alike, and is honest enough about it to put bad second-nearest to good.

Reading

The primary sources are the two 2013 papers by Tomáš Mikolov and colleagues: Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space, which introduces CBOW and Skip-gram, and Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality, which adds negative sampling. Both are short and unusually readable for what they started.

Speech and Language Processing chapter 5 covers the same ground more slowly and is the gentler first read. Its section on bias in embeddings is worth your time even though we did not have room for it today.
