Word2Vec: CBOW and Skip-gram

Last session ended with a sentence nobody could have read an hour earlier. Today we build the thing it describes — and find out that the prediction task was never the point.

The sentence from last session

Here is what was on the board at the end of session 20:

Session 21, first ten minutes — as promised

"CBOW averages the context word vectors, multiplies by an output matrix, applies softmax, and does gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss."

An hour of prerequisites was spent so that this sentence would be readable, and by now it should be. Today we build the model it describes, and then we find out that the prediction it makes is something we throw away.

The premise: meaning from company

The idea underneath all of this is older than any of the machinery, and it is one line — J. R. Firth, 1957:

The distributional hypothesis

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Words that turn up in similar contexts tend to mean similar things. That is the whole premise, and every embedding method in this course rests on it.

You have already seen it work. Session 19 ended on this table — counts of how often each word appears near three others:

piedatacomputer
cherry 4428 2
digital 5 16831670
information5 39823325
cos(cherry, information)   =  0.018
cos(digital, information)  =  0.996

Nobody told that model what any word means. It counted which words appeared nearby, and cosine sorted the results correctly.

⚠️ One thing to notice before we move on, because it will save confusion later. Those vectors have three dimensions, hand-picked to make the point. With only three axes, two words that both load on data and computer have almost nowhere else to differ, so the cosine runs hot — 0.996. The 300-dimension vectors we build today spread similarity out over far more room and simply do not produce numbers like that. A cosine of 0.996 here and a cosine of 0.996 there are not the same claim, and later today we will see exactly how differently the two representations behave.

So the premise already works. What Word2Vec changes is the verb:

The move this session makes

Every method in Unit II so far has counted the company a word keeps — bag-of-words, TF-IDF, that table above. Word2Vec predicts it instead.

Stop counting the context. Start predicting it, and keep what the model had to learn in order to predict well.

The task is a pretext

Here is the task Word2Vec trains on. Take a sentence, delete a word, and ask the model to guess what was deleted:

a  great  _____  about  hope

Stop and notice how useless this is. Nobody has a problem that is solved by filling in blanks in sentences we already have the answers to. We are not building this because we want it.

We are building it because of what a model has to become in order to do it well. To guess film from that context, the model must have arranged its internal numbers so that words appearing in similar company end up with similar numbers — because that is the only structure in the data that helps. The prediction is a pretext. The weights are the product.

And it needs no labelled data at all

Session 16 trained a sentiment classifier and needed 2,000 reviews that somebody had read and tagged as positive or negative. That labelling is the expensive part of supervised learning, and it is why good labelled datasets are rare.

Word2Vec needs none. The label — the word that was actually there — is already in the text. Delete it, ask for it back, and check. Every sentence ever written is training data.The modern name for this is self-supervised learning: the supervision signal is manufactured from the input itself. It is how every large language model in Unit III is trained, at enormous scale, and Word2Vec is the first example of it you will meet.

That is the reason this approach took over. Not that it is cleverer, but that it can eat everything.

CBOW: context predicts the centre

CBOW stands for Continuous Bag of Words, and it is the sentence from last session, made concrete. Take our example with a window of ±2 — two words each side of the target:

a  great  [film]  about  hope
└──────┘         └──────────┘
 context           context          target = film

"Bag of words" is in the name for the reason it always is: the four context words go in as an unordered set. a great and great a would give exactly the same input, because we are about to average them.

Now the pipeline, with session 20's shapes unchanged — vocabulary of 10,000, vectors of 300:

E   embedding matrix    10000 × 300     one row per word in the vocabulary
x   averaged context      300           mean of a few rows of E — one vector
W'  output matrix       10000 × 300     one row per word again, on the way out
z   scores              10000           = W'x     one score per vocabulary word
p   probabilities       10000           = softmax(z)

Four steps, all of them taught last session:

  1. Look up. Pull the four rows of E belonging to a, great, about and hope. Four vectors of 300 numbers.
  2. Average. Add them and divide by four. One vector of 300 numbers, called x. This is the "averages the context word vectors" in the sentence.
  3. One layer, then softmax. z = W'x gives a score for every word in the vocabulary — 10,000 of them. Softmax turns those into 10,000 probabilities that are positive and sum to 1.
  4. Loss, then descend. Cross-entropy: −log of the probability the model gave to film, the word that was actually there. Then w ← w − η·gradient on every weight in the model.
Which weights get nudged

All of them — including the four rows of E we just looked up. Those rows are weights, exactly as session 20 flagged, so gradient descent adjusts them like anything else.

That is the whole training loop. Slide the window one word along, do it again. Do it a few billion times, and the rows of E have been pushed into an arrangement where words used in similar company sit in similar directions. Nobody ever wrote down what any word means.

Skip-gram: the same thing, backwards

Skip-gram reverses the arrows. One centre word goes in, and the model predicts the words around it — each context word as its own separate prediction:

That last sentence in the caption is the practical difference, and it decides which one people actually use.

Which one to reach for

Skip-gram, usually. It squeezes four training examples out of the window where CBOW gets one, so the same corpus teaches it more — and that extra signal matters most for rare words, which appear too seldom for CBOW to learn much about them.

The cost is time: four predictions per position rather than one. CBOW is faster and does slightly better on frequent words, because averaging four context vectors smooths out noise.

Both are in the same 2013 paper and both ship in every implementation. If you are choosing blind, choose Skip-gram.

A detail that trips everybody: each word has two vectors

Look back at the shape block. E has one row per vocabulary word, and so does W'. Those are two different sets of numbers for the same word, and both are being trained:

word w's INPUT vector    v_w  =  row w of E      used when w appears as context
word w's OUTPUT vector   u_w  =  row w of W'     used when w is the thing being predicted

The score the model gives to word w is u_w · x — the dot product of w's output vector with the averaged context. So every word is playing two roles, and the model keeps separate numbers for each.

Session 20 promised you this

Last session's second quiz said: when training stops, "you throw away the rest of the network and keep that matrix". This is the rest of the network. W' is discarded.

The word vectors everybody uses — the ones in every downloadable Word2Vec file — are the rows of E. The output vectors did their job during training and are dropped on the floor. Some implementations offer to average the two together instead; hardly anybody does.

The arithmetic that breaks it

Everything above is correct and none of it can be run. The problem is the softmax denominator.

To turn scores into probabilities you must divide by the sum over the whole vocabulary. So to make one prediction — to adjust the weights by one training example — you need a score for every word you know. Put real numbers on that:

vocabulary                   1,000,000 words
corpus                   1,000,000,000 tokens  (a small corpus, by 2013 standards)

exponentials per example         10⁶
training examples                10⁹           (CBOW; Skip-gram makes ~10× more)
                            ──────────────
per pass over the corpus         10¹⁵ exponentials

Ten to the fifteenth. At a billion exponentials a second — which is generous — that is about 11.6 days, for one pass over a corpus that was considered small. Mikolov's paper trained on 100 billion words.

And note where the cost is. It is not in the answer, it is in the normalising. We want to know about one word, and softmax makes us score a million to find out.

Negative sampling: change the question

The fix is not a faster softmax. It is a different question.

Instead of asking "which of my million words goes here?" — one enormous decision — ask "is this pair real?", which is a yes-or-no decision, and ask it a handful of times:

real pair:        (film, about)      ← actually appeared together    → say YES
invented pairs:   (film, kubernetes)                                 → say NO
                  (film, wheat)                                      → say NO
                  (film, thirteen)                                   → say NO
                  ...  k of them, drawn at random

Now the model only has to separate the true pair from k fakes. The scoring function is one dot product squashed into a probability:

P(this pair is real)  =  σ(u · v)          where σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))

Train it to push σ(u·v) towards 1 for the real pair and σ(−u·v) towards 1 for each invented one — which is to say, push the true pair's vectors together and the fake pairs' apart.The negatives are not drawn uniformly. Mikolov samples them from the unigram frequency distribution raised to the power 3/4, which pulls rare words up and common ones down relative to their raw frequency. The exponent was found by trying some; the paper says as much. Typical k is 5 to 20 for a small corpus, 2 to 5 for a large one.

⚠️ This is session 20's sigmoid, arriving exactly where it was promised

Session 20 showed the sigmoid and then said, in a warn box, that it would appear here — and that it would not be a hidden activation. Here it is: one dot product turned into one probability. Word2Vec still has no hidden non-linearity anywhere. It is a shallow model, and that is what makes it fast enough to be useful.

What that buys, in the same units as before:

arithmetic per exampleper pass over 10⁹ tokensat 10⁹ ops/sec
full softmax10⁶ exponentials10¹⁵11.6 days
negative sampling, k = 56 sigmoids6 × 10⁹6 seconds

Roughly 167,000 times less arithmetic, and the vectors that come out are as good or better. That is why Word2Vec was a moment rather than a paper.

Work this out before reading on

Here is a sentence and a window of ±2:

the  cat  sat  on  the  mat

Take on as the target. What exactly is the input to CBOW, and what is the target? How many training examples does Skip-gram get out of the same position?

Two words each side of on gives the context cat, sat, the, mat.

CBOW: input is the average of those four words' rows of E — one vector of 300 numbers. Target is on. That is one training example.

Skip-gram: input is on's row of E, and there are four separate training examples — (on → cat), (on → sat), (on → the), (on → mat). Same position, four times the signal.

One thing worth noticing: the context is a set. CBOW would produce the identical input if the sentence read sat cat on mat the, because averaging destroys order — which is the "bag of words" in the name, and the same trade session 17 made.

Question 1 of 2

Word2Vec is trained to predict a missing word from its context. What do we actually keep when training finishes?

Correct answer: the embedding matrix.

The prediction task is a pretext. Nobody needs a machine that fills in blanks in sentences we already have. We set that task because a model can only do it well by arranging its weights so that words used in similar company end up with similar numbers — and those weights are the thing we came for.

The output matrix W' is thrown away with the rest of the network. And note that Word2Vec is not a language model in session 11's sense: it has no notion of word order inside the window, so it cannot tell you the probability of a sentence.

Question 2 of 2

What problem does negative sampling solve, and how?

Correct answer: it replaces the vocabulary-wide softmax with a handful of binary decisions.

The cost was never in producing the answer — it was in normalising. Softmax divides by a sum over every word you know, so a single training example costs a million exponentials on a realistic vocabulary. Negative sampling asks "is this pair real?" against the true word and k random fakes: six sigmoids instead of a million exponentials, about 167,000 times less arithmetic.

The third option describes something real but different — the 3/4-power sampling distribution, which is how negatives are drawn, not what the technique is for.

Do the vectors actually work?

Everything below was computed from the real thing — the vectors Mikolov's team released in 2013, trained on about 100 billion words of Google News, 300 numbers per word. The distributional hypothesis being honest about itself

Ask why and the answer is uncomfortable but obvious. "The film was good" and "the film was bad" are the same sentence. Antonyms appear in nearly identical company — that is what makes them antonyms rather than unrelated words. So a method that only sees company cannot separate them.

These vectors encode distribution, not meaning. Usually that is close enough to meaning to be useful, and sometimes it is exactly wrong. Hold on to this: it is the reason averaged word vectors turn out to be a mediocre sentiment feature, which is a thing lab 8 gets to measure rather than take our word for.

The famous one: king − man + woman

You have probably met this result. Word vectors support a kind of arithmetic: take king, subtract man, add woman, and the vector you land on is supposed to be queen. Gender as a direction you can travel in.

Here is what the vectors say when you ask them the way the standard tools ask:

most_similar(positive=['king', 'woman'], negative=['man'])

    queen           0.7118
    monarch         0.6190
    princess        0.5902
    crown_prince    0.5499
    prince          0.5377

It works. Out of 300,000 words, from a model that was never told anything about royalty or gender, queen comes first.

Now the part the popular telling leaves out. That function silently removes the three input words from the answers before ranking them. Build the same vector by hand and rank the whole vocabulary with nothing excluded:

rankwordcosine with king − man + woman

The nearest word to king − man + woman is king, by a clear margin. Which makes sense the moment you look at the arithmetic rather than the story: we started at king and made a modest adjustment, so of course we are still nearest to where we started. queen is the nearest word that is not one of the words we put in.

⚠️ Both halves of this need saying

The result is real. Out of 300,000 words, the second-place finisher is queen and the next three are monarch, princess and crown_prince. That neighbourhood did not have to come out right and nobody arranged it. It is a genuinely remarkable thing about vectors trained to fill in blanks.

And the telling is overstated. "The vector equals queen" is not what happens — the vector lands near king, in a royal neighbourhood, and queen is the best non-input answer in it. That is a weaker and more interesting claim than arithmetic.

It is also worth asking whether the first example was lucky. Three more, each shown both ways:

One out of three lands the textbook answer in first place. The other two put it second, behind a word from the right neighbourhood — Milan for Rome, larger for smaller. Notice that bigger − big + small comes out closest to larger, which is the opposite of the intended direction and still obviously the right region.

So: the analogy behaviour is real, it is loose, and quoting it as arithmetic oversells it. That is about the resolution these vectors have.

A correction to session 19

Session 19 said something about embeddings that today's vectors let us check, and it is worth checking because it turns out to be half wrong. The claim was that unlike count vectors, whose cosines run 0 to 1, embeddings "have negative components, and their cosines really do go down to −1".

The first half is right and the second is not. Take 4,000 random words and every cosine between them — about sixteen million pairs:

mean cosine            +0.066
median                 +0.058
1st percentile         -0.079
99th percentile        +0.291
minimum found          -0.270
fraction negative       17.4%

Negative cosines do happen, so the 0-to-1 floor of count vectors is genuinely gone. But nothing comes remotely near −1. Searching all 300,000 words for the vector most opposed to good turns up −0.185, and for king, −0.202.

What is actually true

Trained embeddings do not spread over the whole sphere. They sit in a fairly narrow cone, and almost every pair of words is slightly positively aligned. The formula's range is −1 to 1; the data's range is roughly −0.3 to 1.

Which matters practically: with count vectors, a cosine of 0.3 was a weak signal. Here it is above the 99th percentile — unusually similar. A cosine only means something against the distribution of cosines that representation actually produces.

What this does not fix

Two limits, both of which the next few sessions live with.

Session 3's vocabulary problem survives untouched. A word that was not in the training corpus has no row in E, so it has no vector — not a bad vector, none at all. These vectors were trained in 2013, so:

podcast    in vocab          COVID        NOT IN VOCAB
tweeted    in vocab          selfie       NOT IN VOCAB
smartphone in vocab          blockchain   NOT IN VOCAB
                             emoji        NOT IN VOCAB

And each word gets exactly one vector, whatever it means. Ask for the neighbours of bank:

bank  →  banks 0.744, banking 0.690, Bank 0.670, lender 0.634, banker 0.609, depositors 0.603

Every one of them is the money sense. The river sense is not ranked lower — it is absent, because there is only one vector and the more common sense has claimed all of it. Same with bat, whose neighbours are batsman, batting and wicket_keeper, with nothing at all about the animal.

One word, one vector, no matter how many things the word means. Fixing that is what contextual embeddings do, and that is Unit III.

Where this leaves us

We have dense vectors, learned rather than counted, that put similar words near each other well enough to be genuinely useful — and that confuse antonyms, know nothing after 2013, and give bank one meaning.

Session 20 opened by quoting lab 7's two baselines: 0.809 from raw counts, 0.823 from sublinear TF-IDF, on 2,000 movie reviews. The obvious question is whether these vectors beat that.

The honest answer, for now

We do not know, and it is not obvious. Lab 8 measures it.

There is a real reason for doubt, and you have already seen it: good and bad have a cosine of 0.719. Average the word vectors of a review and the positive and negative words partly cancel — which is not a problem TF-IDF has, because TF-IDF keeps them as separate features. A better representation of words is not automatically a better representation of documents.

Do not let anyone tell you the answer before lab 8 does.

The one thing to remember

The whole lesson in four sentences

Word2Vec sets up a task nobody wants solved — guess the missing word from its neighbours — and keeps the weights the model needed in order to do it, which means it trains on raw text with no labelling at all. CBOW averages the context vectors and predicts the centre word; Skip-gram reverses the arrows and predicts each context word from the centre, getting more training examples out of the same text and doing better on rare words. The softmax over a whole vocabulary makes either one impossible to train, so negative sampling replaces that one huge decision with a few yes/no ones — σ(u·v) against the true pair and k invented ones — for roughly a hundred-thousandth of the arithmetic. What comes out genuinely captures which words behave alike, and is honest enough about it to put bad second-nearest to good.

Read this next

The primary sources are the two 2013 papers by Tomáš Mikolov and colleagues: Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space, which introduces CBOW and Skip-gram, and Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality, which adds negative sampling. Both are short and unusually readable for what they started.

Speech and Language Processing chapter 5 covers the same ground more slowly and is the gentler first read. Its section on bias in embeddings is worth your time even though we did not have room for it today.
