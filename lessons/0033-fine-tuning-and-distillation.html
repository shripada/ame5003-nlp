Fine-tuning, DistilBERT and knowledge distillation

Session 32 built BERT and stopped at what it is. This session is what it is for: a small head trained on labelled data, the same head pointed at session 7's tagging scheme, and then a smaller copy of the model trained to behave like the large one.

A head on a pretrained model

Session 32 closed by planting the split this session is built on. The expensive part — pretraining a bidirectional encoder on unlabelled text — is already done. What remains is to sit a small piece of task-specific circuitry on top of those representations and train it on a labelled task. SLP3 §10.4 names the move in three sentences:

SLP3 §10.4

"we add application-specific circuitry (often called a special head) on top of pretrained models, taking their output as its input. The finetuning process consists of using labeled data about the application to train these additional application-specific parameters. Typically, this training will either freeze or make only minimal adjustments to the pretrained language model parameters."

Fine-tuning is that process: a pretrained language model is reused for a downstream task by training a small task-specific head on labelled data, while the pretrained weights are frozen or only slightly updated. SLP3 places it under transfer learning — knowledge acquired on one task (here, masked language modelling on unlabelled text) is applied to a new one (here, whatever we have labels for).

Two consequences follow, and they are why the paradigm is the one this course ends on. The expensive part is already done, once, by someone else. And the labelled data needed is small, because it is training a head rather than a model. For three-way sentiment with BERT-base, the head is a matrix WC of shape [768×3] — 2,304 parameters — against the 108 million that session 32 counted in the encoder. In practice the last few transformer layers are often updated as well; SLP3 names that as typical. What is not typical is retraining those 108 million parameters from scratch on the labelled set.

Question 1 of 2

In fine-tuning, what is usually trained?

Correct answer: the head, with the pretrained weights frozen or barely touched. That is §10.4's definition, and it is the reason fine-tuning is cheap: the 108 million parameters already encode language, and the labelled set is being asked to train a few thousand more, not to rediscover English.

The second is what the word "training" suggests if you have only ever trained models from scratch, and it is the opposite of the paradigm's point. The third confuses a parameter split with a training decision: the embedding table is large, and it is already trained. The fourth confuses the position the head reads with the parameters it updates — [CLS]'s vector is an activation, produced by the frozen stack, not a lookup we retrain in isolation.

Sequence classification

The [CLS] token returns, with a second job. Session 32 introduced it as the place that carried next-sentence prediction: a classifier sat on the final-layer vector at that one position and output IsNext or NotNext. Fine-tuning for sequence classification — one label for the whole input — uses the same position for a different decision. Sentiment, topic, spam: the stack produces a vector h[CLS]L, and a classifier head maps it to class scores:

y=softmax(h[CLS]LWC)(10.11)

For three-way sentiment, WC is [d×3]. The input is packed as it was for NSP, minus the second sentence:

[CLS] entirely predictable and lacks energy [SEP]     →  negative

SLP3 §10.4.1 notes an alternative: "take the sum or the mean of the last output vector from each token" and classify that. BERT uses a dedicated token instead. The room has two prior results that make the alternative look familiar, and a reason they do not quite settle it.

Session 23 measured that averaging raw word vectors cannot see order: the vectors for dog bites man and man bites dog had cosine 0.9999999999999998. Lab 8 then put averaged static embeddings on a sentiment task and lost, 20 splits out of 20 — Word2Vec 0.731, GloVe 0.724, against TF-IDF at 0.851. Averaging BERT's last-layer vectors is not that operation. Each of those vectors is already contextualised by the whole sequence, which is closer to session 23's pooling of RNN hidden states than to lab 8's average of static rows. A dedicated token that is trained to summarise is a third option, and it is the one BERT uses. Next-sentence prediction is what trained it; even after RoBERTa dropped that objective, the token remains as the place to put a sequence-level decision.

Sequence-pair classification

The same head, pointed at two sentences. §10.4.2 is entailment, paraphrase, discourse coherence — anything whose label is a relation between A and B rather than a property of one sequence. The packing is the one session 32 already used for NSP. [SEP] marks the boundary; [CLS] still carries the decision:

[CLS] I'm confused. [SEP] Not all of it is very clear to me. [SEP]   →  entails
[CLS] Tourist Information offices can be very helpful. [SEP]
      Tourist Information offices are never of any help. [SEP]         →  contradicts

Those are SLP3's own MultiNLI examples. The classifier is (10.11) again. What NSP built — a pair packed around [SEP], a yes/no sitting on [CLS] — is exactly the input format this family of tasks needs. RoBERTa found the pretraining objective dispensable. The packing is still how a pair is presented to the model.

Sequence labeling

Session 32 named named entity recognition as the task that makes peeking ahead worth wanting: deciding whether Washington is a person or a geo-political entity may depend on words that come after it. That was the motivation for bidirectionality. This section is the other half of the same debt: how the model that can see ahead is pointed at that task.

Sequence labeling assigns a label to every token, chosen from a small fixed set. Sequence classification puts one head on one token; sequence labeling puts the same head on every token. Same fine-tuning paradigm, different number of predictions:

yi=softmax(hiLWK)(10.12)
ti=argmax(yi)

WK is [d×k], where k is the number of tags. The tags are session 7's. That session taught BIO as the scheme that converts finding a span into tagging each word, and it used this sentence:

The       B-ORG
Reserve   I-ORG
Bank      I-ORG
of        I-ORG
India     I-ORG
raised    O
rates     O
.

§10.5.2 is the same scheme, with the same argument for why a separate B- tag is needed: two names of the same type can sit next to each other, and without a begin-tag the boundary between them is not recoverable. Session 7's adjacent pair was Google Microsoft; SLP3's worked sentence is Jane Villanueva of United Airlines Holding. The scheme has not changed. What has changed is the encoder underneath it — twelve unmasked transformer blocks instead of the spaCy tagger session 7 ran — and that the loss that trains WK can also, if we let it, make small updates in the last few of those blocks.

Knowledge distillation

The rest of the hour is a different question, asked of the same model. BERT-base is 108 million parameters. Fine-tuning it is cheap compared with pretraining it, but running it is still running 108 million parameters, and this course's labs run on Colab, where that budget is not free. Sanh et al. 2019 compress the model during pretraining, then fine-tune the smaller copy the same way this session has just fine-tuned the large one.

Knowledge distillation is, in Sanh's words,

Sanh et al. 2019, §2

"a compression technique in which a compact model — the student — is trained to reproduce the behaviour of a larger model — the teacher — or an ensemble of models."

The student is not trained on one-hot labels alone. It is trained on the teacher's full output distribution — the soft targets. A one-hot label says this token is day. The teacher's distribution, Sanh notes, also says what else it might have been. BERT-base, asked to fill I think this is the beginning of a beautiful [MASK], puts most of its mass on day and life, and a long tail on future, story, world. That ranking is information a one-hot target throws away. Sanh: training against it "results in a rich training signal by leveraging the full teacher distribution."

Two things to keep in the right order, because the syllabus line names both DistilBERT and fine-tuning and it is easy to fuse them. Distillation is how the student is pretrained. Fine-tuning is what we then do with it, and it is §10.4 unchanged: add a head, train on labelled data. DistilBERT is the student — a smaller BERT, pretrained by distillation from BERT-base, then fine-tuned like BERT. Lab 12 will load distilbert-base-uncased and sit a classification head on it. It will not re-run distillation.

Question 2 of 2

Why does the student train on the teacher's full distribution rather than on one-hot labels alone?

Correct answer: the distribution is a richer target. A one-hot vector says this class; the teacher's probabilities also say and that one is close, and that one is not. Sanh's fill-in-the-blank is the concrete case: day and life share mass that a hard label would give to only one of them.

The second is the natural misreading and it is false: DistilBERT is distilled on the same Wikipedia-and-books corpus BERT was, and the original tokens are still there as hard targets under Lmlm. The third confuses a property of the target with a property of the compute — the student still has to produce a softmax; what it is matching is smoother, not cheaper. The fourth inverts the design: masked language modelling is the second term of the triple loss, not a thing distillation replaces.

The triple loss

Sanh trains the student with three losses at once. The paper's ablation finds that dropping any of them lowers the GLUE macro-score, with the distillation term carrying most of the load and the masked-LM term the least.

losswhat it matches
Lce distillation — cross-entropy against the teacher's soft targets, with a softmax temperature that flattens both distributions so the long tail is not near-zero
Lmlm the ordinary masked-LM loss from session 32, against the original tokens
Lcos cosine embedding loss, aligning the directions of student and teacher hidden vectors

Lcos is session 19 returning. Sanh: the term "will tend to align the directions of the student and teacher hidden states vectors." Direction, not magnitude — which is the distinction session 19 spent an hour establishing, and which session 28 reused when it scored alignment with a dot product. Cosine compares the angle between two vectors after their lengths have been divided out. Here the two vectors are a student hidden state and the teacher's hidden state at the same position, and the loss asks them to point the same way even if they are not the same length. Third time the same geometric fact has been the thing an equation depends on.

The student has the same general architecture as BERT. Sanh removes the token-type embeddings and the pooler, and halves the number of layers — twelve blocks become six. The paper is explicit that the cut is layers rather than width: variations in hidden size "have a smaller impact on computation efficiency… than variations on other factors like the number of layers." DistilBERT also drops next-sentence prediction, which is the same finding session 32 already recorded from RoBERTa, arrived at independently.

Why the model shrinks by 40%, not 50%

Sanh's headline, verbatim: DistilBERT is 40% smaller, 60% faster, and retains 97% of BERT's language understanding. The 40% is derivable from session 32's arithmetic, which makes it a check rather than a quotation.

Session 32 computed BERT-base at 108,367,872 parameters from V=30,000, d=768, N=512, L=12, ignoring norms and biases. One block was 7,077,888; twelve of them were 84,934,656; the embedding table (tokens plus positions) was 23,433,216. Halve the blocks and keep the embeddings:

blocksparameters
BERT-base12108,367,872
DistilBERT6 65,900,544
reduction39.2%

39.2% against the paper's "40%", and 65.9M against DistilBERT's commonly quoted 66M. Two independent routes to the same number. The embeddings are why it is 40% and not 50%. Halving twelve blocks to six removes half the block parameters, but the 23 million embedding table is untouched — session 32 already established that those embeddings are 21% of BERT — so the model shrinks by less than half. "Halve the layers, halve the model" is the natural inference and it is wrong, for a reason the room already has.

Exercise

Session 32's BERT-base configuration, ignoring norms and biases: vocabulary 30,000, d=768, context window 512, twelve blocks at 7,077,888 parameters each, token embeddings 23,040,000, position embeddings 393,216.

First: compute DistilBERT's parameter count when the layers are halved and the embeddings are kept. Then compute the reduction against BERT-base, as a percentage.

Second: why is the reduction not 50%?

The count. Six blocks: 6×7,077,888=42,467,328. Embeddings unchanged: 23,040,000+393,216=23,433,216. Total: 42,467,328+23,433,216=65,900,544. Reduction: 1−65,900,544/108,367,872=0.392, which is 39.2% — Sanh's "40%" to the precision the headline uses, and 65.9M against the commonly quoted 66M.

Why not 50%. Halving the layers halves the block parameters, 84,934,656 → 42,467,328. The embedding table does not shrink. Those 23 million parameters are 21% of BERT-base and a still larger fraction of DistilBERT, and they are a lookup over the same 30,000-token WordPiece vocabulary. Cutting layers cannot cut a table whose rows are word types.

Where this leaves us

T3.9 is complete. The room now has the second half of chapter 10: a pretrained bidirectional encoder is put to work by sitting a small head on it and training that head on labelled data, with the pretrained weights frozen or barely touched. Sequence classification and sequence-pair classification both read [CLS]; sequence labeling reads every token, with session 7's BIO tags as the label set. DistilBERT is the same architecture with the layers halved, pretrained to match BERT-base's behaviour rather than only its labels, and 39.2% smaller for a reason the embedding table explains.

The architecture of Unit III is finished. Sessions 34–36 are applications of it — machine translation, question answering and summarisation, then chatbots. Lab 12 fine-tunes distilbert-base-uncased, not BERT: the 40% is why, and the worked example in this session is the model the lab actually loads.

The main points

Fine-tuning adds a task-specific head on a pretrained model and trains it on labelled data, freezing or barely updating the pretrained weights — 2,304 parameters for three-way sentiment against BERT-base's 108 million. Sequence classification sits the head on [CLS]; sequence-pair classification uses the same head on two sentences packed around [SEP]; sequence labeling sits the same kind of head on every token and predicts session 7's BIO tags. Averaging last-layer vectors is an alternative §10.4.1 names; BERT's choice is a dedicated token trained to summarise. Knowledge distillation trains a smaller student to match a teacher's full output distribution, not just one-hot labels. DistilBERT is BERT with the layers halved, pretrained with a triple loss — distillation, masked LM, and a cosine term that aligns hidden-state directions — and is 39.2% smaller rather than 50%, because the 23 million embedding table does not shrink. Distillation is how it was pretrained; fine-tuning it afterwards is §10.4 unchanged.
