The GRU: two gates, one vector, and what the simplification cost

Session 25 ended by asking whether three gates and eight weight matrices were all necessary. The GRU is one answer. This session works out what it removed, what that removal costs in arithmetic, and what the evidence says about whether the trade was worth making.

The question session 25 left

Session 25's closing question

"Three gates and eight weight matrices, and the unit does one job: manage a context vector. Is all of it necessary? Could you get the same behaviour with fewer gates?"

The gated recurrent unit is one answer to that question. It comes from Cho et al. in 2014 — seventeen years after the LSTM, which is later than most people guess. Everything in this session is a comparison against session 25, because that is the order the course met them in and because the comparison is the point.

What the GRU removes

Start with the subtraction, before any equation:

LSTM (session 25)GRU
vectors carriedc and h h only
gatesforget, add, outputreset, update
matrix pairs43

Two things are gone. The separate context vector — the GRU carries one vector, as a plain RNN does. And the output gate — there is no separate decision about how much to emit. The forget and add gates have been merged into a single update gate z, used once as z and once as 1−z.

This is a simplification of the LSTM, and it is fair to present it that way. The real work of this session is finding out what the simplification cost, which is a question the arithmetic answers rather than the description.

Cho's four equations

These are equations (5) to (8) of Cho et al. 2014, with the paper's own numbering. Nothing here is new notation: σ, tanh⁡ and ⊙ are all from session 25.

rt=σ(Wrxt+Urht−1)(5)
zt=σ(Wzxt+Uzht−1)(6)
ht=zt⊙ht−1+(1−zt)⊙h~t(7)
h~t=tanh⁡(Wxt+U(rt⊙ht−1))(8)

Equation (5) is the reset gate, (6) the update gate, (7) the new state, and (8) the candidate.

Equation (7) is an interpolation

This is the one to read aloud. ht is a weighted average of two things — the previous state and the new candidate — and the two weights always sum to 1. So z is a dial. At z=1 the unit keeps the old state exactly and ignores the candidate; at z=0 it throws the old state away and takes the candidate whole; in between it blends them.

That single reading carries the session. The LSTM's forget and add gates were two independent decisions; here there is one dial, and moving it toward keeping is the same act as moving it away from writing.

Equation (8) is where the reset gate acts

Look at where rt sits: it multiplies h_{t-1} before the candidate is computed. So the reset gate decides how much of the past the candidate is even allowed to see. With r≈0 the candidate is computed from x_t alone and the unit effectively starts fresh at this word, whatever it was carrying.

Two gates, two different questions

r controls what goes into the candidate. z controls how much of the candidate comes out. They act at different points and it is worth being careful here: the gate that does the forgetting is z, not r. The reset gate does not erase memory — it hides memory from one calculation.

Which way round is z?

Sources disagree, and this is worth stating once and pinning down. In this course z multiplies the previous state, as in Cho's equation (7) above. PyTorch writes the same equation with the terms in the other order, ht=(1−z)nt+zht−1, which agrees. The flipped convention, with z gating the candidate instead, circulates widely. Check which one a source is using before comparing numbers with it.

A worked example, against session 25's

Same three steps as session 25, same starting point, one dimension, gate values given rather than derived from weights. The tables are built to be read side by side.

Step 1 lands on 0.7600, which is exactly where session 25's LSTM landed. The two units do the same thing when asked to write something into an empty memory. The differences show up afterwards, and there are two of them.

Cost 1: the GRU cannot remember without acting

Session 25's row 2 was the whole session: the context was held at c=0.7298 while the unit emitted h=0.0623. Put that beside the GRU's row 2:

row 2 · "hold"what is retainedwhat is emitted
LSTM (session 25)c=0.7298h=0.0623
GRUh=0.7348h=0.7348

The same number twice, because there is only one number. With no output gate and no separate context vector, retention is output — anything the GRU is holding on to, it is also saying. Session 25 called the LSTM's ability to hold something silently the point of the whole architecture. The GRU does not have it.

Cost 2: keeping and writing are coupled

In the LSTM, f and i are separate gates, so f=1 with i=1 keeps the old context and adds to it. The GRU's weights are z and 1−z, and they sum to 1 by construction. Keeping 90% of the old state therefore means writing at most 10% of the new one. There is no setting that does both.

Take h_{t-1} = 0.76 and a candidate h~=−0.50, and turn the dial:

Neither of these is a defect. They are what the simplification bought, and whether the trade was worth making is the next section's question — one that is answered by evidence rather than by argument.

Exercise

Session 25's LSTM held its memory at 0.7298 while emitting 0.0623 — remembering without acting. What values of z and h~ would make a GRU do the same thing: carry roughly 0.73 forward while emitting almost nothing?

There are none. ht is both the memory and the output, so "carry 0.73 forward" and "emit almost nothing" are contradictory requirements — whatever the unit carries is exactly what it emits. No choice of z, h~ or r separates them, because the separation is structural and the GRU does not have the structure.

This is the cost of dropping the second vector and the output gate, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not mean. The GRU can still retain information over many steps — z≈1 does that perfectly well. What it cannot do is retain information quietly.

Question 1 of 2

What did the GRU remove, relative to the LSTM?

Correct answer: the separate context vector and the output gate. Four matrix pairs become three, and two vectors become one.

The second option is the instructive wrong answer, and it is wrong in a specific way worth fixing now: the gate that does the forgetting is z, the update gate, not r. The reset gate controls what the candidate is allowed to see, which is a different job that the LSTM had no equivalent of at all. The first is false — equation (8) computes a candidate, and it does read h_{t-1}, filtered through r. The fourth is false: ⊙ appears in equations (7) and (8) and is how every gate in either architecture applies.

What to pick

The schedule line for this session asks a practical question, so here is the practical answer, in two parts: what the simplification saves, and whether it costs accuracy.

The GRU has 75% of the LSTM's parameters. That is the whole efficiency case, and it is worth stating the number plainly because it is smaller than the reputation suggests — "simpler architecture" tends to be heard as "much smaller model", and a quarter fewer parameters is a real saving but not a dramatic one.

Whether it costs accuracy

Chung et al. 2014 compared the plain tanh⁡ unit, the LSTM and the GRU on polyphonic music modelling and raw speech signal modelling. Their conclusion:

Chung et al. 2014

"The evaluation clearly demonstrated the superiority of the gated units; both the LSTM unit and GRU, over the traditional tanh unit… However, we could not make concrete conclusion on which of the two gating units was better."

Read the author list before reading the conclusion again: Chung, Gulcehre, Cho and Bengio. Two of the four are authors of the GRU paper itself. The people who proposed the GRU compared it against the LSTM and declined to claim a winner.

A pattern this course keeps finding

Session 18 checked idf's claim against the Shakespeare features. Session 21 checked king−man+woman and found it landing on king. Session 22 checked GloVe's own pitch against Levy & Goldberg. Here the check is one the authors ran themselves. Four times a well-known claim has been checked against the evidence and come back weaker than advertised. The habit is more durable than any of the four results.

So the practical answer is: try both, prefer the GRU when data or compute is tight, and do not expect the choice to decide anything. In PyTorch it is a one-word change, which is worth doing in lab 9 to see the difference for yourself.

What you will actually run, which is not equation (8)

PyTorch does not implement Cho's equation (8)

PyTorch's own documentation says so: "The calculation of new gate nt subtly differs from the original paper and other frameworks. In the original implementation, the Hadamard product between rt and the previous hidden state ht−1 is done before the multiplication with the weight matrix W…"

Side by side:

Cho (8):h~=tanh⁡(Wx+U(r⊙ht−1))PyTorch:nt=tanh⁡(Winx+bin+r⊙(Whnht−1+bhn))

Cho applies the reset gate before the matrix; PyTorch applies it after.

These are not algebraically equal. In Cho's form the reset gate masks the state and the matrix then mixes what survives; in PyTorch's form the matrix mixes everything and the mask is applied to the result. PyTorch states the change is deliberate, made for efficiency.

This matters here more than it would elsewhere, because the course's own assigned source runs the PyTorch variant. Rao & McMahan chapter 7 calls nn.GRU directly in both surname-generation notebooks and never writes the equations out. So a student who reads Cho §2.3, implements equations (5)–(8) by hand, and compares against the book's model is running two different architectures — and neither source will tell them so.

One more discrepancy, if you read Cho

A footnote in Cho's paper describes the LSTM as having four gating units. Session 25 taught three, following SLP3, and said explicitly that g is not a gate but the candidate content. The equations are identical either way — the sources simply count the candidate differently. Nothing about the architecture is in dispute; only the noun is.

Question 2 of 2

What does the empirical evidence say about choosing between the LSTM and the GRU?

Correct answer: gating clearly wins, and neither gated unit consistently beats the other. That is Chung et al.'s conclusion in their own words, and two of its authors proposed the GRU.

The first option is exactly the claim the evidence does not support, and it is the one most people carry — "simpler" is not an argument, it is a hypothesis, and this one was tested. The third is a plausible-sounding structural argument that also went untested-and-unconfirmed; the GRU's single vector is a real limitation, as row 2 showed, but it did not show up as a consistent accuracy gap. The fourth over-corrects: the datasets are a genuine caveat worth noting, not a reason to discard the finding.

What neither of them fixes

The GRU is smaller than the LSTM and behaves comparably. Neither addresses the two limits session 25 closed on:

  • Both are strictly sequential. ht needs h_{t-1}, so neither can be parallelised across time whatever hardware is available.
  • Both push everything through one vector per position. Fewer gates does not change how much can be carried.

Session 27 does not attack either limit. It keeps the sequential unit exactly as it is and changes what gets built out of it: two recurrent networks, one reading and one writing. That is the shape that makes translation possible, and it is also where Cho's paper reappears — the equations above come from §2.3 of a paper whose actual subject is the encoder-decoder, which is session 27's topic. Bahdanau, session 28's author, is a co-author of it.

The main points

The GRU answers session 25's question by removing two things from the LSTM: the separate context vector and the output gate. What remains is one vector and two gates. The reset gate r decides how much of the past the candidate may see; the update gate z interpolates between the previous state and the candidate, with weights z and 1−z that always sum to 1. Two costs follow from the arithmetic rather than from opinion. The GRU cannot remember without acting, because retention and output are the same vector — its row 2 holds 0.7348 and emits 0.7348, where the LSTM held 0.7298 and emitted 0.0623. And keeping and writing are coupled, so the new state can only ever land between the old state and the candidate. In exchange it uses three matrix pairs instead of four: 540,000 parameters against 720,000 at d=300, which is 75%. On accuracy, Chung et al. — including two authors of the GRU paper — found both gated units clearly better than a plain tanh⁡ unit and could not separate the two. One practical warning: PyTorch applies the reset gate after the weight matrix rather than before, so nn.GRU is not Cho's equation (8), and the assigned course source uses nn.GRU without saying so.
