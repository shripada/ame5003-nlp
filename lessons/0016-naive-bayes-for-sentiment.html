Naïve Bayes for sentiment, worked through

Five short reviews, one new sentence, and every number done by hand. The training is nothing but counting — and the first honest attempt fails completely.

Where we stopped

Last session ended with the whole classifier on one line and not a single number in it:

best class  =  argmax   P(c) · Π P(w | c)
                c in C          w in d

Today we fill it in. Two things have to be learned from data — the prior P(c) and the likelihoods P(w | c) — and both are counts. There is no training loop and nothing to tune. You count, you divide, and the classifier exists.

The training data

Five very short reviews, already labelled by a human. Three negative, two positive:

classdocument
−just plain boring
−entirely predictable and lacks energy
−no surprises and very few laughs
+very powerful
+the most fun film of the summer

And the sentence we want to classify, which the model has never seen:

predictable with no fun

Read it as a human first. It is negative — predictable and no fun. Whether the arithmetic agrees is the test.

Training, step 1 — the prior

The prior asks how common each class is, before reading anything. Count documents:

P(c)  =  number of documents in class c  /  total documents

P(−)  =  3/5  =  0.6           P(+)  =  2/5  =  0.4

That is the whole step. Note it is the only place we count documents — everything after this counts words.

Training, step 2 — the likelihoods

Now P(w | c): if a review is negative, how often is the next word boring? Glue all of a class's documents together into one big document, and count within it:

P(w | c)  =  count of w in class c  /  total words in class c
classbig documenttokens
−just plain boring entirely predictable and lacks energy no surprises and very few laughs14
+very powerful the most fun film of the summer9
Two denominators in two consecutive steps

The prior divided by 5 — the number of documents. The likelihood divides by 14 or 9 — the number of words in that class. Different questions, different denominators. Mixing them up is the most common way this calculation goes wrong.

Count the positive big document yourself: very powerful the most fun film of the summer is 9 tokens, not 8. the appears twice.

The first attempt fails

Our test sentence is predictable with no fun. Take with aside for a moment and try the other three words on the counts we have:

wordcount in −P(w | −)count in +P(w | +)
predictable11/1400
no11/1400
fun0011/9

Now multiply. The negative class dies on fun. The positive class dies on predictable, and again on no:

P(−) · P(S | −)  =  0.6 × 1/14 × 1/14 × 0   =   0
P(+) · P(S | +)  =  0.4 × 0    × 0    × 1/9 =   0
This is worse than a wrong answer

Both classes score exactly zero. The classifier does not pick badly — it cannot pick at all. There is nothing to compare, and no amount of staring at the tie will produce a decision.

And nothing unusual happened. Every word in the test sentence is an ordinary English word that appeared in the training data. They just did not all appear in both classes, which with five short documents is entirely expected.

We have met this failure twice before — session 12's zero-probability sentence and session 13's whole hour about it. Third appearance, same cause: a product dies if any factor is zero, and counting hands out zeros generously.

Add-one, and this time it is the right tool

The fix is the one from session 13. Pretend every word was seen once more than it was:

P(w | c)  =  ( count(w, c) + 1 )  /  ( total words in c  +  |V| )

Same shape as before: the +1 on top removes the zeros, and the denominator grows by |V| to keep the probabilities summing to 1.

Session 13 said add-one was bad. It was — for language models

The complaint in session 13 was about scale. A language model has V ≈ 50,000, so add-one handed almost all the probability to words that were never observed, and a bigram seen every single time got rated at 0.008%.

Here V is 20. The correction is gentle, the numbers stay sensible, and add-one is what practitioners genuinely use for Naïve Bayes — it is the standard method, not a teaching stand-in. The method did not change; the vocabulary size did.

This is worth holding on to as a general habit: a technique is not good or bad on its own. It is good or bad at a particular scale, on a particular problem.

What goes in |V|

|V| is the number of distinct word types across the whole training set — both classes together. Count them here and there are 20:

and · boring · energy · entirely · few · film · fun · just · lacks · laughs
most · no · of · plain · powerful · predictable · summer · surprises · the · very
The trap: V is not per-class

It is tempting to use "the vocabulary of the negative class" when computing negative likelihoods. Wrong. Both classes use the same |V| = 20.

Why it matters: the two classes' scores are only comparable if they were computed over the same set of possible words. Give each class its own V and you are comparing probabilities from two different universes — and the numbers still look perfectly reasonable, which is what makes this error dangerous rather than obvious.

What about with?

with appears nowhere in the training data, in either class. It is not in V, so it has no probability to smooth. The rule for Naïve Bayes is blunt: drop it. Remove the word from the test document and carry on with the rest.

Compare with session 13 — same problem, different right answer

For a language model, an unknown word was handled with <UNK>, because the model has to assign the sentence a probability and cannot simply pretend part of it is absent.

A classifier only needs to compare classes. An unknown word contributes the same nothing to every class, so it cannot affect which one wins — dropping it is not an approximation, it genuinely changes no decision. Worth noticing that the right answer depends on what the model is for, not just on the problem it hits.

The full calculation

With |V| = 20, the denominators become 14 + 20 = 34 and 9 + 20 = 29:

wordP(w | −)P(w | +)
predictable(1+1)/34 = 2/34(0+1)/29 = 1/29
no (1+1)/34 = 2/34(0+1)/29 = 1/29
fun (0+1)/34 = 1/34(1+1)/29 = 2/29

No zeros anywhere. Multiply each row through, with the prior in front:

P(−) · P(S | −)  =  3/5  ×  (2 × 2 × 1) / 34³  =  6.1 × 10⁻⁵     ← larger
P(+) · P(S | +)  =  2/5  ×  (1 × 1 × 2) / 29³  =  3.2 × 10⁻⁵

The classifier says negative, which is what a human said too. It got there knowing nothing about English, from five documents and some division.

Look at where the decision came from. Both classes had one helpful word and two unhelpful ones, so the margin is not dramatic — but predictable and no each appeared in negative reviews and never in positive ones, and two such words outweighed fun going the other way. The prior helped a little too: negative reviews were more common to begin with.

Work this one out before reading on

The negative calculation above is done in full. Do the positive one yourself and check you get 3.2 × 10⁻⁵. You need the prior 2/5, and the three probabilities 1/29, 1/29 and 2/29.

2/5 × (1/29) × (1/29) × (2/29) = 2/5 × 2/24389 = 4/121945 = 3.28 × 10⁻⁵

The denominator is 29 because the positive big document has 9 tokens and |V| is 20. If you got 28, you counted the positive documents as 8 tokens and missed the second the in the most fun film of the summer.

If you got a much larger number, check you did not use a per-class vocabulary in place of |V| = 20.

One more problem: the numbers get too small

Our test sentence had three words. A real review has two hundred, so the score is two hundred probabilities multiplied together — each one small, and the product astronomically smaller.

Computers store numbers with limited precision. Multiply enough small values and the result stops being small and starts being exactly zero:

0.01 ^ 200   →   0.0        (not "very small" — zero, in ordinary floating point)

That is called underflow, and it would zero out every class again — this time for a purely mechanical reason rather than a missing count.

The fix: add logarithms instead of multiplying

Logs turn multiplication into addition: log(a × b) = log a + log b. So take the log of the whole thing and the product becomes a sum:

best class  =  argmax   log P(c)  +  Σ log P(w | c)
                c in C             w in d

The numbers being added are moderate negative values — around −3, not 10⁻¹⁴⁷ — and nothing underflows.

Our example, in natural logs:

classlog prior+ log likelihoodstotal
−−0.51−2.83 − 2.83 − 3.53−9.70 ← larger
+−0.92−3.37 − 3.37 − 2.67−10.33

Negative wins again — as it must.

Why taking logs cannot change the answer

The logarithm is monotonic: if a > b then log a > log b, always. So whichever class had the bigger score still has the bigger log score. The ranking is preserved exactly, and the ranking is the entire output.

What you lose is the readable scale — −9.70 is not a probability and cannot be quoted as one. We already gave that up in session 15 when we dropped P(d), so nothing new is lost here.

Every real implementation works in log space. When you meet predict_log_proba in scikit-learn next week, this is why it exists.

Question 1 of 2

In add-one smoothing for Naïve Bayes, what is the |V| added to the denominator?

Correct answer: distinct word types across all classes together.

Both classes use the same |V| — 20 in our example. Give each class its own vocabulary and the two scores are computed over different sets of possible words, so comparing them is meaningless. The numbers still look reasonable, which is exactly why this error survives.

Question 2 of 2

Why does working in log space not change which class the classifier picks?

Correct answer: the log function is monotonic, so it preserves score order.

A bigger number always has a bigger logarithm, so the winner cannot change. That is the whole argument — logs are a fix for underflow, not a modelling decision. Note the last option is wrong: the two classes are not shifted by the same amount, and they do not need to be.

The one thing to remember

The whole lesson in two sentences

Training a Naïve Bayes classifier is two sets of counts — the prior from document counts, the likelihoods from word counts inside each class's combined text — and unsmoothed those counts produce zeros that leave both classes scoring nothing at all. Add-one fixes it with the whole training vocabulary in the denominator, unknown words are simply dropped, and the products are computed as sums of logs so they do not underflow.

That completes sentiment analysis with Naïve Bayes, and with it the second block of Unit II. You now have a classifier that learns from labelled examples instead of a hand-written lexicon — the thing VADER's missing nightmare asked for back in lab 1.

One thing worth noticing before we move on. Look at what we did to the documents: threw away word order, kept a count for each word. We never gave that a name, but it is a way of turning a document into numbers — and it is not specific to classification. Search, similarity and recommendation all need the same thing. Next session names it, and turns a document into a vector.
