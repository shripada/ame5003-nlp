Cleaning up text

Normalization and stop words: deciding which differences in text do not matter and removing them. What counts as irrelevant depends on the task.

Where session 3 left off

In session 3 we split text into tokens, so we now have a list of pieces. Consider the following tokens:

"US"   "us"   "U.S."   "Us"

A human reader regards some of these as the same and others as different. To the computer they are four distinct strings, with nothing connecting them. Before counting words or searching text, we usually want to collapse the differences that do not matter into a single form.

This collapsing is called normalization. The following definition holds for the whole lesson:

Normalization

Normalization deliberately destroys information that we have judged to be irrelevant.

More text to process means more resources such as memory and time are required. We discard detail on purpose, so that items we consider equivalent are counted as equivalent. The difficulty lies in deciding what is safe to discard, and that decision depends entirely on the task.

Case folding

The most common normalization step is to convert all text to lowercase.

"AMAZING"   "Amazing"   "amazing"   →   "amazing"

Three strings become one, so every capitalisation of the word is counted together when we count or search. For most of the work in Unit I, namely counting words and searching documents, this is the desired behaviour.

Note, however, what has been lost:

  • US (the country) and us (the word) both become us. They are now the same token.
  • Apple (the company) and apple (the fruit) merge too.
  • Polish (from Poland) and polish (for shoes) merge.

In each case the capitalisation carried real meaning, and lowercasing discarded it. For search this is usually an acceptable trade, since a user typing apple probably wants results about the company as well. For a task in which the capital letter is the signal, it is an error. The choice has to be made deliberately.

Exercise

Suppose we are building a system to extract company names from news articles, and the first step is to lowercase everything. What has this broken?

It removes the principal clue available. In English, a capital letter in the middle of a sentence is a strong indication that a word is a name: Apple, Rao, Manipal. After lowercasing, Apple becomes apple and the signal is gone.

This illustrates the general point: a cleaning step that helps one task (searching) harms another (finding names). There is no normalization that is correct in general, only normalization that is correct for a particular task.

Unicode normalization

Two strings can appear identical on screen and still compare as unequal, because Unicode sometimes provides more than one way to store the same character.

a = "café"     # é stored as ONE character
b = "café"     # é stored as  e + a separate accent mark

a == b    →    False
len(a)    →    4
len(b)    →    5

The two strings print identically. One stores é as a single character, while the other stores a plain e followed by a separate combining accent mark. If a user types one form and the stored text contains the other, the lookup fails without raising an error; the match simply never occurs.

The solution is Unicode normalization: convert every string to one standard form before comparing. The usual choice is Normalization Form C (NFC). NFC (Normalization Form C) is a Unicode Standard process that turns text into a canonical composed form

normalize("NFC", a) == normalize("NFC", b)    →    True
Why this matters for Indian scripts

This is not a peculiarity of European accents. Indian scripts are composed of base letters together with combining marks, so the same Kannada or Devanagari word can readily arrive in two encodings that look identical and compare as unequal.

This belongs to the same family of problems as the regex-versus-re issue in session 2: Indian text has structure that naive handling gets wrong. One rule covers both cases: use regex to split and NFC to compare. Normalizing text to NFC as it enters the system removes this entire class of error.

Punctuation, numbers, and accents

The remaining normalization steps are smaller, and each involves the same kind of decision: whether to keep or discard a distinction, depending on the task.

  • Punctuation. For counting words it is usually stripped, so that cat, and cat match. It is retained for finding sentence boundaries, or for reading emoticons in a review.
  • Numbers. Every number is sometimes replaced with a single marker such as <NUM>, because the exact value does not matter. In other tasks, such as those involving prices, dates, or amounts, the value is precisely what matters.
  • Accents and spelling. résumé → resume, colour → color. This helps search and harms any task in which the difference carries meaning.

There is no fixed procedure. Each of these decisions is an instance of the same question: for the task at hand, is this difference noise or signal?

Stop words

Stop words are very common words carrying little content, such as the, is, of, a, and and. They appear in almost every sentence, so individually they indicate little about what a text is about. There is a long tradition in text search of removing them so that the content words stand out.

Ready-made lists are available; NLTK's English list contains about 200 words. Removing them requires only keeping the tokens that are not on the list.

"this is one of the best phones I have ever bought"
        ↓  remove stop words
"best phones ever bought"

What remains, namely best, phones, and bought, conveys the substance of the sentence. For search and for identifying the topic of a document, which is most of the information-retrieval work in Unit I, this is a real improvement: there is less to store, and the informative words are more prominent.

When removing stop words is a mistake

We now examine what a standard stop-word list actually contains. NLTK's list includes the following words:

not    no    nor    against    only    very

Every one of these changes the meaning of a sentence. In particular not, the most important word available for expressing a negative judgement, is treated as noise and discarded. Consider the effect on a product review:

"would not recommend"
        ↓  remove stop words
"would recommend"

Removing stop words has reversed the meaning of the sentence. A sentiment system given the second line would classify a negative review as positive. This is the same phenomenon as VADER's failure on nightmare in lab 1: a routine preprocessing step can destroy the very information the task depends on.

When to remove stop words

Remove them when the goal is the topic or the keywords and function words are noise, as in search and information retrieval. This is what we do in lab 4.

Keep them when meaning depends on those words, as in sentiment analysis, question answering, and any task in which not matters. As the glossary notes, "to be or not to be" consists entirely of stop words.

Modern systems in fact remove fewer stop words than older ones did, because the models that process the text are now able to use these words rather than being confused by them.

Question 1 of 2

Why does lowercasing a document count as destroying information?

Correct answer: case can carry meaning, and merging it cannot be undone.

Once US and us have both become us, nothing in the text indicates which was which. The distinction is permanently lost. This is acceptable when the distinction was noise for the task and a genuine loss when it was signal. Normalization cannot be reversed, which is why the choice matters.

Question 2 of 2

For which task is removing stop words most likely to be a mistake?

Correct answer: deciding whether a review is positive or negative.

Sentiment is carried by exactly the words a stop-word list discards: not, no, and very. Removing them turns "would not recommend" into "would recommend". The other three tasks depend on content words, so removing function words helps them.

Summary

The main points

Every cleaning step — lowercasing, stripping punctuation, removing stop words — discards information deliberately. Whether this helps or harms depends entirely on the task, so there is no single correct way to clean text. The steps are assembled to suit the task at hand.
