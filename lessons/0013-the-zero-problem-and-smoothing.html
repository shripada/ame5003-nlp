The zero problem and smoothing

Counting says an unseen sentence is impossible. It is not. Fixing that means taking probability away from what we saw and giving it to what we did not.

Where we stopped

Last session ended on a broken model. Our four-sentence corpus, scoring an ordinary sentence:

P(I want to eat Indian pizza)  =  0

Not unlikely. Zero. One pair the corpus never happened to contain drove the whole product to nothing.

And this is not a toy-corpus problem that goes away with more data. It gets worse. A vocabulary of 50,000 words has 2.5 billion possible bigrams, and no corpus contains more than a small fraction of them. So most sentences you will ever test contain at least one unseen pair, and a model that calls most valid sentences impossible is worthless.

The cure is called smoothing: shave a little probability off the things we did see, and share it out among the things we did not.

Why it is called smoothing

Picture the probabilities as a bar chart over next words: a few tall bars for the words we saw, and a flat row of zeros for everything else. Smoothing pushes the tall bars down slightly and lifts the zeros just off the floor. The jagged shape becomes smoother — hence the name.

The total must still add up to 1. So every bit of probability given to an unseen word has to be taken from a seen one. There is no free probability.

Add-one smoothing

The simplest possible fix, and the one to understand first: pretend you saw everything one more time than you did. Also called Laplace smoothing.

MLE       P(w₂ | w₁)  =   count(w₁ w₂)      /  count(w₁)

add-one   P(w₂ | w₁)  =  (count(w₁ w₂) + 1) / (count(w₁) + V)

The +1 on top is the whole idea — no count is zero any more, because everything got one free sighting. The denominator has to grow too, or the numbers stop adding to 1. It grows by V, the vocabulary size, because we added 1 to each of V possible next words.

In our corpus there are 10 tokens a model might predict — the nine words (I, want, to, eat, Indian, Chinese, food, she, wants) plus </s>. So V = 10. Take the context eat, which occurs 3 times: the denominator becomes 3 + 10 = 13.

next word after eatcountMLEadd-one
Indian 22/3 = 0.6673/13 = 0.231
Chinese 11/3 = 0.3332/13 = 0.154
food 00 — impossible1/13 = 0.077
each other word 00 — impossible1/13 = 0.077

It works. Nothing is zero, so no single unseen pair can wipe out a sentence. And the ordering survives — Indian still beats Chinese, which still beats the unseen words.

But look at what it cost

P(Indian | eat) fell from 0.667 to 0.231. Two-thirds of the evidence we actually collected was given away to words that never once followed eat. That is a lot of probability to move on no evidence at all.

And with a realistic vocabulary it stops being "a lot" and becomes absurd. Here is P(food | Indian) — a pair seen 3 times out of 3 — as V grows:

Add-one is a teaching tool, not a working method

At a realistic vocabulary size, add-one takes a bigram we observed every single time and rates it at eight-thousandths of a percent. The 50,000 words that never followed Indian collectively soak up almost all the probability.

The flaw is that add-one treats every unseen word as equally likely. It has no way to know that curry is a plausible thing to follow Indian and the is not. It gives them the same weight, and there are so many implausible words that they drown out the evidence.

So add-one is genuinely used — but for problems with small vocabularies, like the Naïve Bayes classifier in session 16. For language models it is the method that shows what a real method has to do better.

The better idea: ask a shorter question

Add-one's mistake is falling back on nothing — a flat guess spread evenly. But we are never actually out of information. If we have never seen she wants Indian, we may still have seen wants Indian. And if not that, we certainly know how common Indian is on its own.

So when the evidence runs out, do not guess flat — ask the same question with less context. Two ways to do this.

Backoff: use the shorter n-gram only when you must

Backoff tries the longest n-gram first. If its count is zero, it drops to the next shorter one, and keeps dropping until it finds something it has seen.

Take P(Indian | she wants) in our corpus:

trycountsresult
trigram — she wants Indian0 / 1zero, back off
bigram — wants Indian0 / 1zero, back off
unigram — Indian3 / 260.115 — use this

We never saw that phrase, but Indian is a reasonably common word here, so the answer is a reasonable non-zero number. Compare that with add-one, which would have handed Indian the same value as a word that appears once.

One catch: the shorter n-gram's probabilities already add to 1 on their own, so borrowing them raw would push the total above 1. Real backoff scales the borrowed values down by a discount factor to keep the books balanced. The idea is what matters here; the bookkeeping is in the reading.

Interpolation: always mix all of them

Interpolation does not wait for a zero. It always blends the trigram, bigram and unigram estimates together, with weights (written λ, lambda) that add to 1:

P(w₃ | w₁ w₂)  =  λ₁ · P(w₃ | w₁ w₂)     ← trigram
               +  λ₂ · P(w₃ | w₂)         ← bigram
               +  λ₃ · P(w₃)              ← unigram

               λ₁ + λ₂ + λ₃ = 1

Worked through for P(Indian | I want), using λ = 0.5, 0.3, 0.2:

modelestimateweight
trigram P(Indian | I want)1/3 = 0.3330.5
bigram P(Indian | want)1/3 = 0.3330.3
unigram P(Indian)3/26 = 0.1150.2
0.5 × 0.333  +  0.3 × 0.333  +  0.2 × 0.115  =  0.290

Because the unigram term is always in the mix and is never zero, the blended result is never zero. The long context leads when it is trustworthy; the short one quietly holds the floor up.

(In this example the trigram and bigram happen to give the same value, 1/3 — an accident of a four-sentence corpus, where almost every context occurs only once or twice. The thing to take from the arithmetic is the unigram floor: whatever the longer contexts say, including nothing at all, the mix cannot fall to zero.)

Where do the λ values come from? Not from guesswork and not from the training corpus — you would just relearn the training data. You set aside a separate slice of text, the held-out set, and pick the λ values that score it best. Measuring "best" properly is next session.

Backoff vs. interpolation in one line

Backoff uses the shorter n-gram only when the longer one fails. Interpolation uses all of them every time, weighted. Interpolation is usually the better performer and is simpler to implement, which is why it is the more common choice.

The other kind of zero

Everything above fixes an unseen pair of known words. But lesson 12's failure was Indian pizza, and pizza is not in our vocabulary at all. Smoothing cannot help: the word has no count to smooth, and it is not among the V words the probability was shared out over.

The standard fix is to make a slot for it. Before training, replace rare words in the corpus — say every word appearing only once — with a single special token, <UNK>. Then train normally. <UNK> becomes an ordinary vocabulary item with real counts, and at test time any word the model has never seen is swapped for <UNK> and gets that probability.

So the model no longer says "impossible". It says "a word I do not know", which is both true and useful.

Think about this before reading on

Someone suggests: instead of adding 1 to every count, add 0.01. Does that fix add-one's problem with a 50,000-word vocabulary?

It helps a lot, and it is a real method — add-k smoothing. The denominator becomes count(w₁) + 0.01V, so with V = 50,000 that is 500 rather than 50,000, and observed bigrams keep far more of their probability.

But it does not fix the real flaw. Every unseen word still gets exactly the same share. The model still cannot tell that curry is a better guess after Indian than the is — it has only made the wrong guess smaller.

That is why backoff and interpolation are different in kind, not just in degree: they use evidence about the words themselves instead of spreading a flat amount. Tuning k is adjusting the mistake; backing off is not making it.

Question 1 of 2

Why does add-one smoothing add V to the denominator?

Correct answer: 1 was added to each of V possible next words, so V was added.

The probabilities over all next words must still sum to 1. We handed out V extra counts in total — one per possible next word — so the denominator has to grow by exactly V to match.

Question 2 of 2

What is the main advantage of backoff over add-one smoothing?

Correct answer: it uses evidence about the word instead of one flat share for all.

Both methods avoid the unseen-pair zero, so that is not the difference. (Neither rescues a word that is missing from the vocabulary altogether — that is what <UNK> is for.) The real difference: add-one gives every unseen word the same probability, while backoff drops to a shorter n-gram, so a common word gets more than a rare one. It is guessing with evidence rather than guessing evenly.

The one thing to remember

The whole lesson in two sentences

Counting assigns zero to everything it never saw, which is most of language, so smoothing moves probability from the seen to the unseen — and since the total must stay 1, that probability always comes out of the observed counts. Add-one does it by spreading a flat share over every word, which is far too blunt at real vocabulary sizes; backoff and interpolation do it by falling back on a shorter context, so the fallback still carries evidence about which words are actually likely.

That is the n-gram language model complete: count, assume a short history, smooth. One question is left, and it is the one that decides everything else — is a given model any good? Bigram or trigram? Which λ values? You cannot answer any of it without a way to measure. Next session: perplexity.
