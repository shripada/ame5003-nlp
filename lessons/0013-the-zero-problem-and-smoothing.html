The zero problem and smoothing

Counting says an unseen sentence is impossible. It is not. Fixing that means taking probability away from what we saw and giving it to what we did not.

Where session 12 left off

Session 12 ended with a model that fails. Applied to an ordinary sentence, our four-sentence corpus gives:

P(I want to eat Indian pizza)=0

The result is not a small probability but zero. A single pair that the corpus did not happen to contain reduced the whole product to nothing.

This is not an artefact of a small corpus that disappears with more data; it becomes more severe. A vocabulary of 50,000 words admits 2.5 billion possible bigrams, and no corpus contains more than a small fraction of them. Most test sentences therefore contain at least one unseen pair, and a model that assigns zero probability to most valid sentences is of no use.

The remedy is smoothing: removing a small amount of probability from the events we observed and distributing it among those we did not.

The term "smoothing"

Consider the probabilities as a bar chart over possible next words: a few tall bars for the words observed, and a flat row of zeros for the rest. Smoothing lowers the tall bars slightly and raises the zeros above the floor, making the distribution less jagged. This is the origin of the term.

The total must still sum to 1, so every unit of probability given to an unseen word must be taken from a seen one. No probability is created.

Add-one smoothing

The simplest approach, and the one to understand first, is to add one to every count, as though every possible event had been observed once more than it actually was. This is also called Laplace smoothing.

MLEP(w2|w1)=count(w1w2)count(w1)add-oneP(w2|w1)=count(w1w2)+1count(w1)+V

The +1 in the numerator is the whole idea: no count is zero any more, because every event has received one additional observation. The denominator must also grow, or the probabilities will no longer sum to 1. It grows by V, the vocabulary size, because 1 was added to each of V possible next words.

In our corpus there are 10 tokens a model might predict — the nine words (I, want, to, eat, Indian, Chinese, food, she, wants) plus </s>. Hence V=10. For the context eat, which occurs 3 times, the denominator becomes 3+10=13.

next word after eatcountMLEadd-one
Indian 22/3 = 0.6673/13 = 0.231
Chinese 11/3 = 0.3332/13 = 0.154
food 00 — impossible1/13 = 0.077
each other word 00 — impossible1/13 = 0.077

The method works. No probability is zero, so no single unseen pair can eliminate a sentence, and the ordering is preserved: Indian still ranks above Chinese, which still ranks above the unseen words.

The cost of add-one smoothing

P(Indian|eat) has fallen from 0.667 to 0.231. Two-thirds of the evidence actually collected has been transferred to words that never followed eat at all, on no evidence whatsoever.

With a realistic vocabulary the effect becomes much larger. The following shows P(food|Indian), a pair observed in 3 of 3 opportunities, as V increases:

Add-one in practice

At a realistic vocabulary size, add-one assigns a bigram observed on every opportunity a probability of eight thousandths of one per cent. The 50,000 words that never followed Indian collectively absorb almost all the probability mass.

The defect is that add-one treats every unseen word as equally likely. It has no means of knowing that curry is a plausible continuation of Indian and the is not, so it assigns them the same weight. There are so many implausible words that they overwhelm the evidence.

Add-one is used in practice, but for problems with small vocabularies, such as the Naïve Bayes classifier in session 16. For language models it serves mainly to establish what an adequate method must do better.

Using shorter n-grams

The error in add-one is that it falls back on no information at all, distributing a flat estimate evenly. In fact we are never entirely without information. If we have not observed she wants Indian, we may still have observed wants Indian; and failing that, we know how frequent Indian is on its own.

When the evidence for the full context is exhausted, therefore, we should ask the same question with less context rather than guessing uniformly. There are two ways to do this.

Backoff

Backoff uses the longest n-gram first. If its count is zero, it falls back to the next shorter one, continuing until it reaches an n-gram that has been observed.

Consider P(Indian|she wants) in our corpus:

trycountsresult
trigram — she wants Indian0 / 1zero, back off
bigram — wants Indian0 / 1zero, back off
unigram — Indian3 / 260.115 — use this

The phrase was never observed, but Indian is reasonably frequent in this corpus, so the result is a sensible non-zero value. Add-one, by contrast, would have assigned Indian the same probability as a word occurring once.

There is one complication. The shorter n-gram's probabilities already sum to 1 on their own, so using them directly would push the total above 1. Backoff as actually implemented scales the borrowed values down by a discount factor to preserve the total. The principle is what matters here; the details are in the reading.

Interpolation

Interpolation does not wait for a zero count. It always combines the trigram, bigram, and unigram estimates, using weights, written λ, that sum to 1:

P(w3|w1w2)=λ1P(w3|w1w2)trigram+λ2P(w3|w2)bigram+λ3P(w3)unigram

with λ1+λ2+λ3=1. Worked through for P(Indian|I want), with λ = 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2:

modelestimateweight
trigram P(Indian|I want)1/3 = 0.3330.5
bigram P(Indian|want)1/3 = 0.3330.3
unigram P(Indian)3/26 = 0.1150.2
0.5×0.333+0.3×0.333+0.2×0.115=0.290

Because the unigram term is always included and is never zero, the combined result is never zero. The long context dominates when it is reliable, and the short one establishes a lower bound.

In this example the trigram and bigram happen to give the same value, 1/3. This is an accident of a four-sentence corpus, in which almost every context occurs only once or twice. What the arithmetic demonstrates is the unigram floor: whatever the longer contexts contribute, including nothing at all, the combination cannot fall to zero.

The λ values are obtained neither by guesswork nor from the training corpus, since fitting them on the training corpus would simply reproduce the training data. A separate portion of text, the held-out set, is set aside, and the λ values that score it best are selected. Measuring what "best" means is the subject of session 14.

Backoff and interpolation compared

Backoff uses the shorter n-gram only when the longer one has a zero count. Interpolation uses all of them on every estimate, weighted. Interpolation generally performs better and is simpler to implement, which is why it is the more common choice.

Unknown words

Everything above addresses an unseen pair of known words. Session 12's failure, however, involved Indian pizza, and pizza is not in our vocabulary at all. Smoothing cannot help here: the word has no count to smooth and is not among the V words over which the probability was distributed.

The standard solution is to reserve a slot for such words. Before training, rare words in the corpus, for instance every word occurring only once, are replaced by a single special token, <UNK>, and training proceeds normally. <UNK> becomes an ordinary vocabulary item with real counts, and at test time any word the model has not seen is replaced by <UNK> and receives that probability.

The model therefore no longer reports that the sentence is impossible. It reports that the sentence contains a word it does not know, which is both accurate and useful.

Exercise

Suppose that instead of adding 1 to every count we add 0.01. Does this solve add-one's problem with a 50,000-word vocabulary?

It helps considerably, and it is a genuine method, called add-k smoothing. The denominator becomes count(w1)+0.01V, so with V=50,000 the added term is 500 rather than 50,000, and observed bigrams retain far more of their probability.

It does not, however, address the underlying defect. Every unseen word still receives exactly the same share, so the model still cannot determine that curry is a better continuation of Indian than the is. The incorrect estimate has only been made smaller.

Backoff and interpolation therefore differ in kind rather than in degree: they use evidence about the words themselves instead of distributing a flat amount. Tuning k reduces the error; backing off avoids it.

Question 1 of 2

Why does add-one smoothing add V to the denominator?

Correct answer: 1 was added to each of V possible next words, so V was added.

The probabilities over all next words must still sum to 1. V extra counts were distributed in total, one per possible next word, so the denominator must grow by exactly V.

Question 2 of 2

What is the main advantage of backoff over add-one smoothing?

Correct answer: it uses evidence about the word instead of one flat share for all.

Both methods avoid a zero for an unseen pair, so that is not the distinction, and neither recovers a word missing from the vocabulary altogether, which is the purpose of <UNK>. The actual difference is that add-one assigns every unseen word the same probability, whereas backoff falls back to a shorter n-gram, so a common word receives more than a rare one. Backoff estimates from evidence rather than distributing uniformly.

Summary

The main points

Counting assigns zero probability to everything it has not observed, which is most of language, so smoothing transfers probability from the observed to the unobserved. Since the total must remain 1, that probability always comes out of the observed counts. Add-one distributes a flat share over every word, which is far too crude at realistic vocabulary sizes. Backoff and interpolation instead fall back on a shorter context, so that the fallback still reflects evidence about which words are actually likely.

This completes the n-gram language model: count, assume a short history, and smooth. One question remains, and it determines every other choice: whether a given model is any good. Bigram or trigram? Which λ values? None of these can be settled without a way to measure model quality. Session 14 introduces perplexity.
