The LSTM: two vectors, and three gates that manage them

Session 24 found two problems. The first one — one vector asked to do two jobs — is what this architecture is built to fix. The gates are how it does the managing, not what the design is about.

Where session 24 left off

Session 24 gave two reasons why an RNN's effective memory is short. Both are worth having on the board before anything new is said, in SLP3's order:

  1. The hidden layer is asked to do two jobs at once — serve the current decision, and carry information forward for future ones. There is one vector for both, and no machinery separating them.
  2. The gradient is multiplied once per step on the backward pass, driving it toward zero over any real distance.

Session 24 was told to stop reading at a particular sentence. That sentence is where this session starts, and it states the design goal in the book's own words:

SLP3 §13.5

"To address these issues, more complex network architectures have been designed to explicitly manage the task of maintaining relevant context over time, by enabling the network to learn to forget information that is no longer needed and to remember information required for decisions still to come."

Notice what that sentence is about, and what it is not about. It is about managing context. It says nothing about gradients. The architecture that follows is a response to reason 1. It happens to help with reason 2 as well, and we will get to that, but taking the gradient as the motivation makes the rest of the hour look like an arbitrary list of parts.

The claim this session makes

SLP3 divides the work into two subproblems: "removing information no longer needed from the context, and adding information likely to be needed for later decision making." And then the clause that matters most:

The design decision

"The key to solving both problems is to learn how to manage this context rather than hard-coding a strategy into the architecture."

This course has made that move three times already. Session 4 hard-coded a strategy with a stop-word list — a fixed decision about which words do not matter. Session 5 hard-coded one with a stemmer's rules. Session 20 replaced counted weights with fitted ones. This is the same trade applied to memory: do not decide in advance what to forget, learn it. Fourth time, and by now the room has the vocabulary for it.

The one structural change: a second vector

Before any gate is mentioned, there is a single change to the shape of the unit, and it is the change that answers reason 1.

The RNN had one vector, ht. The LSTM adds an explicit context layer ct, which runs alongside it. Two vectors, one per job:

vectorjob
ctcarry information forward — the long-term memory of the unit
htserve the current step — what the unit outputs now, and what the next step's gates read

That is the architecture. Everything else in this session is the machinery that manages the two, and the gates exist because ct exists — not the other way round. A student who remembers one thing about the LSTM should remember that there are two lines running through it rather than one.

The gate design pattern, taught once

All three gates are the same construction with different weights. SLP3 states the pattern directly:

A feedforward layer, then a sigmoid, then a pointwise multiplication with the layer being gated.

The sigmoid is doing something specific here. It pushes its outputs toward 0 or 1, so multiplying by it acts as a soft binary mask: a component of the gate near 1 lets the corresponding value through nearly unchanged, and a component near 0 erases it. Values in between attenuate. Because the gate is computed from weights, the mask is learned rather than fixed, which is the "rather than hard-coding a strategy" clause made concrete.

The sigmoid's third job in this course

Session 20 used it to make a score readable as a probability. Session 21 used it to score a word pair in negative sampling. Here it is a mask. Same function, three different jobs — the function does not carry a meaning, the surrounding architecture assigns it one.

One piece of notation, used by all three gates. ⊙ is the element-wise product: multiply two vectors of the same length position by position, producing a third vector of that length. SLP3 notes it is also called the Hadamard product. It is not a dot product and it is not a matrix multiply — nothing is summed, and no dimension changes.

The three gates

These are SLP3's equations 13.20 to 13.27, in the chapter's order and with the chapter's numbering, so the reading matches. Each gate takes the same two inputs — the previous hidden state ht−1 and the current input xt — and differs only in its weights and in what it multiplies.

The forget gate: what to delete

ft=σ(Ufht−1+Wfxt)(13.20)
kt=ct−1⊙ft(13.21)

The gate is computed, then multiplied into the previous context. Where ft is near 0, that part of the context is erased; where it is near 1, it survives. kt is the part of the old context that is being kept.

The add gate: what to write

gt=tanh⁡(Ught−1+Wgxt)(13.22)
it=σ(Uiht−1+Wixt)(13.23)
jt=gt⊙it(13.24)

Two things happen here and they are worth keeping apart. gt is the candidate — the new information extracted from this step's input, which could be written into context. it is the gate that decides how much of it actually is. Their product jt is what gets written.

gt is not a gate

It is content, and it is computed exactly as a plain RNN computes its hidden state: the same two weight matrices applied to the same two inputs, squashed by tanh⁡. The old RNN is still in there, as one line of a larger unit. It is also the only tanh⁡ of the four — the three gates all use σ, because a gate needs to be a mask between 0 and 1, while content needs to be able to be negative.

A naming collision you will meet immediately

SLP3 calls this the add gate. Olah — the second reading for this session — and essentially every other source call it the input gate, which is why its symbol is it. Same gate, same equation, two names. There is no fourth gate. This is the same kind of collision as session 23's, where one book's W and W′ were another's U, W and V.

The new context

ct=jt+kt(13.25)

What was kept, plus what was written. That is the whole update, and it is an addition. Hold on to that; it returns at the end of the session.

The output gate: what this step needs now

ot=σ(Uoht−1+Woxt)(13.26)
ht=ot⊙tanh⁡(ct)(13.27)

The context is now up to date, and the last question is how much of it this particular step should expose. ot answers that. This is the separation of the two jobs, in one line: the unit can hold something in ct and still emit almost nothing in ht. The next section shows exactly that happening.

A worked example

Everything here is one-dimensional, so ⊙ is ordinary multiplication and every value is a single number. The gate values are given, not derived from weights — the question being asked is what the context line does, not where a sigmoid comes from. Session 20 did the same thing with its chosen weights [1.4,0.0,−1.9], and for the same reason.

One value connects the two. If a gate's pre-activation were −4, then σ(−4)=0.0180 — which is the sort of number appearing in the f column below. The gates are computed; we are simply starting after the computation.

Start from c0=0. Three steps: write something, hold it, then wipe it.

Row 2 is the entire session. The context arrives at 0.7600 and leaves at 0.7298 — a loss of 4% — while the unit emits h=0.0623, which is nearly nothing. Whatever step 2's input was, it was not written into memory (i=0.03 shut the candidate out) and it was not reported (o=0.10). The unit is remembering without acting.

Why row 2 matters

A plain RNN cannot do that. In an RNN, h is the memory — the vector it emits is the vector it carries. Emitting nearly zero therefore means remembering nearly zero. The two jobs are one vector, which is session 24's reason 1 stated as arithmetic. Here they are two vectors, and row 2 is what the separation buys.

Rows 1 and 3 are the other two behaviours. Row 1 writes: the forget gate is near 0 but there is nothing yet to forget, and i=0.95 lets the candidate through almost intact. Row 3 wipes: f=0.01 deletes what was held, and the context drops to 0.0123. Note that o=0.95 in row 3 — the output gate is wide open, and the unit still emits almost nothing, because there is nothing left in context to emit. The gates control different things and they are genuinely independent.

Exercise

You want a unit to carry its context forward completely untouched while emitting almost nothing at this step. What would f, i and o have to be?

Then answer a second question: what would a plain RNN have to do to achieve the same thing?

f≈1 so nothing is deleted, i≈0 so nothing new is written, and o≈0 so nearly nothing is emitted. That is row 2 of the table, which used 0.98, 0.03 and 0.10 and lost 4% of the context. g does not matter — with i≈0 the candidate is masked out whatever it is.

A plain RNN cannot do it. Its memory and its output are the same vector, so "emit almost nothing" and "carry everything forward" are contradictory instructions. There is no setting of U and W that satisfies both, because the problem is not the weights — it is that there is only one vector. That is session 24's reason 1, and this is the fix for it.

Question 1 of 2

Why does the LSTM add a second vector ct alongside ht?

Correct answer: the hidden state was doing two jobs at once. That is SLP3's framing and session 24's reason 1, and it is the reason the unit has the shape it has.

The first option is the most tempting one and it is true as a consequence — the additive path is real and we come to it below — but it is not the motivation, and it is not the book's. Remember the LSTM as a gradient trick and its three gates become an arbitrary list. The third describes something closer to attention, which is session 28: nothing is stored per word here, and ct is one vector however long the sequence gets. The fourth is a size explanation for a structural problem — making h bigger does not separate the two jobs, it just gives one overloaded vector more room.

The gradient consequence, and a health warning

Session 24 established that a quantity multiplied by the same factor once per step goes to zero or to infinity. Look again at equation 13.25:

ct=jt+kt

That is a sum. Travelling backward along the context line, the gradient passes through an addition and an element-wise multiplication by a gate — not through a weight matrix, and not through the same weight matrix once per step. A path built from additions does not decay exponentially the way a repeated product does. This is why the LSTM also helps with reason 2.

Where this explanation comes from

This is the standard explanation, not SLP3's framing, and it should not be attributed to the book. Chapter 13 motivates the architecture as context management and does not present the additive path as the reason for the design. Take it as a consequence of the design rather than its purpose. Session 24 said the same thing in its closing callout, and the two sessions agree deliberately.

What this does not fix

The LSTM improves long-range memory. It does not deliver it unconditionally, and three limits matter for what comes next.

  • It is still sequential. Computing ct requires c_{t-1}, so the steps cannot be run in parallel across time however much hardware is available. That is the limitation the transformer removes, and it is what sessions 29–31 are about.
  • It is still one vector per position. Everything from the left of the sequence is compressed into ct, whatever the sequence's length. Long-range dependency is better, not solved.
  • It costs four times the parameters. An RNN unit has U and W. The LSTM has (U_f, W_f), (U_i, W_i), (U_g, W_g) and (U_o, W_o) — eight matrices against two. At d=300 that is 720,000 parameters against 180,000.
Which leads directly to the next question

Three gates and eight weight matrices, and the unit does one job: manage a context vector. Is all of it necessary? Could you get the same behaviour with fewer gates? That question has an answer, it is called the GRU, and it is session 26.

Question 2 of 2

What does the output gate ot control?

Correct answer: how much of the context is emitted now, which is a separate question from how much is kept. Row 2 of the worked table is the evidence: context 0.7298 held, output 0.0623 emitted.

The third option is the forget gate and the fourth is the add (input) gate — both are real gates, just not this one, and mixing them up is the ordinary way to get 13.20–13.27 wrong. The first describes nothing in the architecture; the loss sees ht whatever the gates do. The three gates answer three different questions: what to delete, what to write, what to say.

The main points

The LSTM exists because a single hidden state cannot both serve the current decision and carry information forward. Its one structural change is a second vector, the context ct, running alongside ht. Three gates manage it, all built from the same pattern — a feedforward layer, a sigmoid, and an element-wise multiplication — so that each acts as a learned soft mask. The forget gate decides what to delete from context, the add gate (called the input gate everywhere except SLP3) decides what to write, and the output gate decides how much of the result this step emits. gt is not a gate but the candidate content, computed exactly as a plain RNN computes its hidden state. Because the context update ct=jt+kt is an addition rather than a multiplication by a weight matrix, the gradient also survives better along it — a consequence of the design rather than its purpose. What remains unfixed: the unit is still sequential, still compresses everything into one vector, and costs four times the parameters. That last cost is session 26's opening question.
