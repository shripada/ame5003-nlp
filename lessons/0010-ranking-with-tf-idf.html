Ranking results with TF-IDF

Not just which documents match, but which match best — and why the score needs a logarithm in two different places.

The limits of Boolean search

The inverted index answered the question of which documents match, returning an unordered set: a document either matched or it did not. This is adequate for six plays. A web search query, however, matches millions of pages, and an unordered set of a million results is of no use. The best-matching documents must come first.

We therefore move from matching to ranking: each matching document is assigned a score for the query, and the documents are sorted by that score. The standard scoring method, still used as a baseline, is TF-IDF. It combines two ideas, which we introduce in turn.

Term frequency

A document containing car ten times is probably more concerned with cars than one containing it once. The first idea is therefore to score a document by the number of times the query term appears in it. This count is the term frequency (tf).

Why the raw count is unsuitable

The raw count rewards repetition too heavily. A document containing car 100 times is not 100 times more relevant than one containing it once. It is more relevant, but by nothing approaching that factor. Relevance increases with the count, but at a decreasing rate: the tenth occurrence contributes much less than the second.

The solution is a logarithm, a function that grows quickly at first and then flattens. We use a log-weighted term frequency:

weight = 1 + log₁₀(tf)      (when tf > 0)

The effect on the count is as follows:

appears (tf)1101001000
weight1.02.03.04.0

Each tenfold increase in the count adds exactly 1 to the weight, so an increase from 1 occurrence to 10 counts for as much as an increase from 10 to 100. This is the purpose of the first logarithm: it expresses diminishing returns arithmetically.

Inverse document frequency

Term frequency alone has a more serious problem. Consider the query the best car. The word the appears many times in every document, so it receives a very high term frequency while indicating nothing about which document is a good match. A common word is a poor discriminator and a rare word is a good one: two documents sharing the word insurance is a far stronger signal than two documents sharing the.

We therefore want to down-weight common words and up-weight rare ones. How common a word is can be measured by its document frequency (df), the number of documents containing it. We then weight by the inverse of this quantity, again compressed with a logarithm:

idf = log₁₀(N / df)        N = total number of documents

The following values come from a collection of N = 806,791 documents:

termin how many docs (df)idf
auto 6,723 2.08
car 18,165 1.65
insurance 19,241 1.62
best 25,235 1.50

The rarer the word, the higher its idf: auto, occurring in fewer documents, outranks best, which occurs in more. One limiting case is worth stating explicitly.

How idf handles stop words

A word in every document has df = N, so its idf is log₁₀(N/N) = log₁₀(1) = 0. Its weight is zero.

This addresses the stop-word problem from session 4 without additional machinery. No hand-written list is needed to suppress the: it appears in nearly every document, so its idf is close to zero and it contributes almost nothing to any score. The weighting achieves what the stop-word list was intended to achieve, without the list.

Combining tf and idf

The TF-IDF weight of a term in a document multiplies the two ideas:

tf-idf  =  (1 + log₁₀ tf)  ×  log₁₀(N / df)
             how often it appears          how rare it is
             IN THIS document              ACROSS all documents

This quantity is high in precisely the desired case: a term that appears often in this document and is rare across the collection, that is, a word distinctive to this document. It is low for a word that is rare here and close to zero for a word that occurs everywhere.

To score a whole document for a query, we sum the tf-idf weights of the query terms and sort the matching documents by that total, placing the highest-scoring document first. This is ranked retrieval.

The two logarithms compared

Both logarithms compress a large range into a manageable one, but for different reasons:

  • On tf, because relevance shows diminishing returns: the hundredth occurrence adds little to the tenth.
  • On df, because word rarity spans a very large range, from a word in ten documents to one in a million, and a raw ratio would allow rare words to dominate the score.
Exercise

Two documents both match the query car insurance. Document A contains insurance 9 times; document B contains the 50 times and insurance once. Under TF-IDF, which ranks higher, and why is that the correct outcome?

Document A. Its insurance weight is (1 + log 9) × 1.62 ≈ 1.95 × 1.62 ≈ 3.2. Document B's the contributes almost nothing — its idf is near zero, no matter that it appears 50 times — and its single insurance scores 1 × 1.62 = 1.62.

Document A therefore ranks higher, which is the correct outcome: A is genuinely concerned with insurance, whereas B merely uses a common word frequently. This comparison shows the whole behaviour of TF-IDF: it rewards the distinctive word and disregards the common one.

Question 1 of 2

Why is the term frequency put through a logarithm instead of used raw?

Correct answer: relevance grows with count, but less and less each time.

A word appearing 100 times does not make a document 100 times more relevant than one in which it appears once. The logarithm captures these diminishing returns: each tenfold increase in the count adds the same fixed amount, 1, to the weight.

Question 2 of 2

A word appears in every single document in the collection. What is its idf?

Correct answer: zero, so it contributes nothing to any score.

If df = N then idf = log(N/N) = log(1) = 0. A word occurring in every document cannot distinguish between documents, and idf assigns it exactly zero weight. This is why TF-IDF suppresses stop words without requiring a stop-word list.

Summary

The main points

TF-IDF assigns a high score to a term that appears often in a given document and rarely across the collection, so that distinctive words determine the ranking and common words contribute little. The two logarithms serve to model diminishing returns on the count and to compress the large range of word rarity into a usable weight.
