Frames and slots

A traveller wants a flight. A system that books it has to know where from, where to, and when. SLP3 Appendix K, Frame-Based Dialogue Systems, calls the knowledge structure that holds those facts a frame: a collection of slots, each of which can take a set of possible values. The system's job is to fill the slots with the fillers the user intends, and then act — book the flight, set the alarm, put the appointment on the calendar.

The architecture is 1977. Appendix K opens on GUS (Bobrow et al.), a travel-planning system whose frames became the industrial pattern for task-oriented dialogue for the next thirty years. Fig. K.2 is the air-travel frame this hour uses:

slottypethe system's question
ORIGIN-CITYcity "From what city are you leaving?"
DESTINATION-CITYcity "Where are you going?"
DEPARTURE-TIMEtime "When would you like to leave?"
DEPARTURE-DATEdate "What day would you like to leave?"
ARRIVAL-TIMEtime "When do you want to arrive?"
ARRIVAL-DATEdate "What day would you like to arrive?"

Filling that frame from an utterance is three jobs, and K.0.1 names them. Domain classification asks which frame we are in: airlines, an alarm clock, a calendar. Intent determination asks what general task the user is trying to accomplish: show a flight, set an alarm, remove an appointment. The first two together decide which frame we are filling. Slot filling extracts the particular values the utterance supplies.

K's own worked utterance:

Show me morning flights from Boston to San Francisco on Tuesday

DOMAIN:       AIR-TRAVEL
INTENT:       SHOW-FLIGHTS
ORIGIN-CITY:  Boston
DEST-CITY:    San Francisco
ORIGIN-DATE:  Tuesday
ORIGIN-TIME:  morning

The simplest systems write the extractor by hand. K.0.1 gives a regular expression for the SET-ALARM intent:

wake me (up) | set (the|an) alarm | get me up

That is sessions 1 and 2. Intent detection can be a pattern. The course opened by pulling facts out of text that way; the last hour's simplest chatbot is the same tool. Most systems do not write those rules. They learn the three jobs from labelled utterances. Practical NLP chapter 6, the prescribed source, is six notebooks that do exactly two of them: intent detection on ATIS, and slot filling on SNIPS. The chapter's Outline section is still empty. Appendix K is the architecture those notebooks implement without naming.

Intent detection

Look at INTENT: SHOW-FLIGHTS in the Boston example. One label for the whole utterance, chosen from a small fixed set. Session 33 called that sequence classification and sat a head on a single position's encoding:

y=softmax(h[CLS]LWC)(10.11)

Sentiment, topic, spam: the same recipe. Intent is that recipe pointed at SHOW-FLIGHTS rather than at negative. Practical NLP's first three notebooks — a CNN/RNN on ATIS, then BERT, then a binary variant — are classifiers. The prescribed chatbot's first half is a head the room trained three sessions ago.

Slot filling

The fillers are spans. San Francisco is two tokens; Tuesday morning would be two. Session 7 faced the same problem with named entities, and solved it with BIO: one tag per token, a B- where a span begins, I- where it continues, O outside any span. Session 33 implemented that scheme as fine-tuning, equation (10.12), on the tags session 7 printed:

The       B-ORG
Reserve   I-ORG
Bank      I-ORG
of        I-ORG
India     I-ORG
raised    O
rates     O
.

Appendix K recalls the same scheme from chapter 17 — session 7's reading — and points it at slots instead of organisations. B-ORG becomes B-DES; the encoder underneath is a pretrained language model rather than the spaCy tagger session 7 ran. The scheme has not changed. This is its third appearance, twenty-nine sessions after the first.

Practical NLP's notebooks treat the two jobs as two models: intent on ATIS, slots on SNIPS. Fig. K.3 does both in a single pass. The input words go through a pretrained encoder; a feedforward layer and a softmax at each token position emit BIO tags; and the domain concatenated with the intent is the desired output at the final EOS token.

A working system wraps a small loop around that extractor: ask questions until the slots are full, query a database, report back, using templates for the replies. K.0.1 says that much and this hour stops there. Dialogue acts, state tracking and sentence realisation are the rest of Appendix K, and they are not this clause.

Exercise

(a) Given book me a flight to Bengaluru on Tuesday morning, which part is the intent, which parts are slots, and which session taught the machinery for each?

(b) Now: I need to be in Bengaluru for a meeting on Wednesday morning. Which slots can the tagger fill?

(a). Intent SHOW-FLIGHTS or BOOK-FLIGHT, from a whole-sequence classification head — session 33, §10.4.1, equation (10.11). Slots DESTINATION-CITY = Bengaluru, DEPARTURE-DATE = Tuesday, DEPARTURE-TIME = morning, via BIO tagging — sessions 7 and 33, §10.5, equation (10.12). The Boston utterance on the board is K's; this one is the same frame with a Karnataka destination.

(b). Destination, yes: Bengaluru is a span to tag. Departure date — there is no token to tag. The intended answer is Tuesday, and it comes from knowing that people arrive the day before a meeting, not from the sentence. Same machinery as (a), and it has just run out. The next section is why.

Two kinds of chatbot

Practical NLP is a 2020 book. Its chatbot fills a frame and then acts. In 2026 the word "chatbot" names something else: an assistant that generates a response to an open-ended request. Both are real. They are not the same system, and the prescribed source is only the left-hand column.

Question answering, last session, is not the right-hand column either. The syllabus lists machine translation, question answering, chatbots and summarisation as four applications. Session 1 named a search engine that finds a page without the query's exact words, and a chatbot, as two different bullets. Practical NLP chapter 6 contains no question answering at all. A chatbot that answers from documents is session 35's RAG pointed at a conversation; it is not QA under a new name.

The left-hand column fails in a specific way, and it is the answer to part (b) of the exercise. K's tagger fills slots by tagging words in the utterance. A travel agent and a client, recorded in SLP3 chapter 25, have this exchange:

A:  And, what day in May did you want to travel?
C:  OK uh I need to be there for a meeting that's from the 12th to the 15th.

The client never states a travel date. The right answer is the 11th: people arrive the day before a meeting. There is no token to tag. Everything this session has taught — frames, BIO, a head on EOS — stops at the words that were said. The right-hand column exists because the left-hand column cannot draw that inference.

How the right-hand column is built is SLP3 chapter 9, Post-training: Instruction Tuning, Alignment, and Test-Time Compute. This course does not cover it. A pretrained model continues text; it does not follow instructions. Chapter 9's own demonstration, from early GPT:

Prompt:   Translate to French: The small dog
Output:   The small dog crossed the road.

Session 34's translator would not do that. It was trained to translate. A base language model given the same instruction ignores it and keeps writing English. Everything this course has built stops at the base model. Chapter 9 is the step across, and it is the next thing to read, not a thing this hour teaches.

Evaluating a task-based system

Appendix K evaluates a task-based system two ways, and they are session 14's names. Session 14: intrinsic evaluation is inexpensive and approximate; extrinsic evaluation is expensive and definitive.

The extrinsic number is task success rate — K also writes task error rate — the percentage of times the system booked the right plane flight. It is the only thing the user cares about, and it needs a whole dialogue to measure.

The intrinsic number is slot error rate: the percentage of slots filled with the correct values, counted as insertions, deletions and substitutions over the reference slot count. K's own words: "a more fine-grained, but less extrinsic metric."

SlotErrorRate=nerrnref(K.1)

Here nerr counts insertions, deletions and substitutions, and nref is the number of slots in the reference. K's worked example:

Make an appointment with Chris at 10:30 in Gates 104

PERSON:  Chris
TIME:    11:30 a.m.
ROOM:    Gates 104

Slot error rate 1/3, because the time is wrong. The number does not say that the one wrong slot is the one that makes the appointment useless. Session 35 closed on the same habit: every metric is a proxy. Perplexity, chrF, token F1, and now slot error rate.

K notes that slot precision, recall and F-score can also be used. Lab 6 defined those three, in week 6, with a hand computation from a confusion matrix. This is week 12. The names have not changed.

When not to answer

Session 34 closed on a requirement from SLP3 §12.7: systems need confidence values so they can abstain from giving incorrect translations that may cause harm. Knowing when not to answer was the open problem that chapter named. Appendix K.1.3 teaches the same decision, with thresholds, for a 1977-descended architecture:

the system's confidencewhat it does
below α reject — "I'm sorry, I didn't understand that"
≥α confirm explicitly — "…Is that correct?"
≥β confirm implicitly — "When do you want to travel to Shanghai?"
≥γ act, and say nothing

Explicit confirmation makes errors easy to correct and is, K says, "time-consuming and awkward". A frame-filler from 1977 and a translator from 2026 have the same problem. The oldest architecture in the session and the newest share an open question, and the course can say so because session 34 already named it.

Question 2 of 2

Why can a task-oriented chatbot not answer an open-domain question?

Correct answer: the frames are defined in advance. Domain classification picks among known frames; slot filling extracts known slots. An open-domain question has no such frame. That is a structural limit, not a size one.

The second is a size explanation for a structural limit, which is the more instructive error. The third is the syllabus confusion already named: QA is session 35, listed separately. The fourth confuses a tagging scheme with a domain ontology — BIO will tag as many slot types as the label set contains; what it will not do is invent a slot the frame does not have.

Three habits

The architectures will date. Three habits the course kept finding will not.

Check the claim against the evidence. Session 26 told the room that four times a well-known claim had been checked and come back weaker than advertised: session 18's idf against the Shakespeare features, session 21's king−man+woman landing on king, session 22's GloVe-versus-Word2Vec, session 26's LSTM-versus-GRU in a paper two of the GRU's authors signed. Session 32 made next-sentence prediction the fifth, and said so. Session 34 ran the same move without numbering it: the easy claim is that machine translation reflects its training data; Prates et al. found amplification. The habit is more durable than any of the results.

Know what a method cannot do. Every session in Unit III closed on a limitation, and each limitation was the next session's subject. This session's own limitation — the meeting that implies a flight date — is the last one, and it has no next session. Chapter 9 is the pointer, not the patch.

Every metric is a proxy. Session 35 already listed the chain: perplexity, chrF, token F1. This hour adds slot error rate, and puts the names session 14 coined back on. Intrinsic evaluation is inexpensive and approximate; extrinsic evaluation is expensive and definitive. Session 34 restated that trade for translation; K.0.2 uses it almost verbatim, twenty-two sessions after it was taught. Lab 6 remains the reference for precision, recall and F1.

Where this leaves us

T3.12 is complete, and with it the last theory clause in the syllabus. A task-oriented chatbot is a frame: domain and intent decide which frame, slot filling fills it. Intent is sequence classification; slots are BIO tags; Fig. K.3 does both on one encoder. The simplest filler is a regular expression. The classical architecture cannot tag a date that was never said. An LLM assistant is a different system, built by a chapter this course names and does not teach, and a modern assistant answering from documents is the retriever-plus-generator session 35 assembled. Slot error rate is a proxy; task success is the thing the user cares about; confidence thresholds decide when not to act.

Session 1 promised five applications and a way of turning language into numbers a machine can compare. The five landed. The numbers ran from one bit per word to a contextual vector per occurrence, and then to a generator conditioned on documents the weights never saw. And knowing when not to answer is still open, for the oldest architecture in this hour and for the newest alike.

The main points

A frame is a collection of slots. Filling it is domain classification plus intent determination plus slot filling. Intent is session 33's sequence classification; slots are session 7's BIO tags, implemented as session 33's sequence labeling. Fig. K.3 does both on one encoder, with domain and intent at EOS rather than [CLS]. A 1977 architecture and a 2026 assistant are different columns; question answering is not a third name for either. Slot error rate is intrinsic; task success rate is extrinsic; lab 6 still holds precision, recall and F1. Confidence thresholds decide when to reject, confirm, or act. Session 22's six representations extend by four; the three debts that session named are paid. Chapter 9 is named and not taught.
