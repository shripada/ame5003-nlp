Question answering and text summarisation

Two syllabus clauses in one hour. The first half points Unit I's retriever at the generator Unit III built. The second is a taxonomy, and one architecture the room already has.

Two clauses, one hour

Session 34 closed by saying this hour carries two syllabus clauses and opens on a retrieval problem the course built in Unit I. Both of those are true, and the first of them is a constraint rather than a courtesy. Question answering and text summarisation are listed separately in the syllabus; the calendar gives them one sitting. Neither half gets the treatment an application deserves. We will say what each one is, what it cannot do, and which pieces of the course already built it.

The two halves are not equally served by their sources. Question answering has SLP3 chapter 11 behind it — dense retrieval, retrieval-augmented generation, and two evaluation metrics. Summarisation has no SLP3 chapter in this release. Chapter 12 is machine translation, last session's. The assigned source is Practical NLP chapter 7, a notebook. QA gets the conceptual weight; summarisation gets a taxonomy and one honest note about the tooling.

The retrieval half is already built

Chapter 11 opens on information retrieval, and the opening is a recap. §11.1.1 is representing documents as vectors — session 17's bag of words, sitting on the term-document matrix session 8 built as an incidence matrix. §11.1.2 is term weighting with tf-idf and BM25 — session 10. §11.1.4 is the inverted index — session 9, which lab 4 implemented. Document scoring by cosine is session 19.

Unit I was not a detour before neural models. It was the retriever half of the system this session assembles. A query comes in; a collection is searched; the documents most likely to meet the information need come back, ranked. Everything from session 8 to session 10 is that pipeline, sparse: count vectors, weighted by TF-IDF, found through an inverted index.

Vocabulary mismatch

§11.3 names the flaw in the terms Unit I left it:

SLP3 §11.3

"The classic tf-idf or BM25 algorithms for IR have long been known to have a conceptual flaw: they work only if there is exact overlap of words between the query and document… an issue called the vocabulary mismatch problem."

Session 8's incidence matrix matched words exactly: a 1 where the term occurred, a 0 where it did not. Session 10's TF-IDF weighted the 1s, so a distinctive term counted for more than a common one, and still required the term itself to appear. The asker and the writer have to choose the same words. Lab 4's inverted index fails for the same reason — there is no postings list to merge if the query term is absent from the document.

The fix, in the same paragraph: "instead of (sparse) word-count vectors, using (dense) embeddings… implemented in modern times via encoders like BERT." Session 21 already placed synonyms near each other in a static space; session 32 built contextual embeddings in which the same word in two sentences is two vectors. §11.3 joins those facts to Unit I: the retriever a student built in lab 4 fails when the asker and the writer choose different words, and the encoder from session 32 is what a modern retriever encodes them with.

The concrete mechanism is session 33's sequence-pair classification, pointed at retrieval. Pack the query and the document around [SEP], read [CLS], sit a linear layer on it:

z=BERT(q;[SEP];d)[CLS]
score(q,d)=softmax(U(z))(11.17)

That is (10.11) with a different letter for the matrix. The pair task in session 33 asked whether sentence B follows from sentence A. Here the pair is a query and a document, and the label is relevance. SLP3 notes that running this over every document in a collection, for every new query, is too expensive to use except as a reranker of a shortlist; a cheaper bi-encoder encodes the documents once, stores the vectors, and scores by a dot product when the query arrives. The architecture we need to see is the first one, because it is a thing the room has already trained.

Exercise

A user types the query on the left. The only document that answers it is the one on the right. They share no content words.

Query:     How can I treat a headache?
Document:  Ibuprofen reduces pain in the temples.

Why does session 10's TF-IDF ranking return nothing for this query? What changes if the retriever encodes the query and the document with BERT?

After the stop words session 4 would drop — how, can, I, a, in, the — the query is treat headache and the document is Ibuprofen reduces pain temples. No term occurs in both, so every tf-idf weight in the overlap is zero, the cosine is zero, and the document does not rank. Session 8's incidence matrix is the same failure in binary: the bitwise AND of the two rows is the zero vector. Lab 4's inverted index has postings for treat and headache that do not include this document.

A dense retriever does not need the words to match. It encodes the query and the document — in the expensive version, together, as the pair above; in the cheap version, separately — and scores the pair in embedding space. treat sits near reduces, headache near pain, and the document comes back. That is the vocabulary-mismatch problem, and it is why Unit I's retriever is necessary but not sufficient.

The instructive wrong answer. Expanding the query by hand with synonyms — adding pain, Ibuprofen — would also retrieve this document, and that is how sparse IR used to paper over the problem. It does not scale: the asker cannot be expected to list every paraphrase, and the expansion will retrieve documents that match a synonym in the wrong sense. Dense retrieval moves the synonymy into the representation rather than into the query string.

Retrieval-augmented generation

Prompting a language model with the question is the obvious method, and it is the one SLP3 starts from. A pretrained model that has seen the location of the Louvre in its training data can generate Paris by conditional generation from the question as a prefix. Three things go wrong with that, and they are why the rest of the chapter exists.

The first is that language models hallucinate: they generate a response that is not faithful to the facts of the world. Dahl et al. (2024) found that, asked questions about the legal domain, models hallucinated from 69% to 88% of the time. They also do this when the correct fact is stored in the parameters and the feedforward layers fail to recall it. The models are not well calibrated, so a wrong answer often arrives with complete certainty.

The second is proprietary data. Medical records, internal documents, personal email, legal discovery — none of this was in the web crawl the model was pretrained on. A larger model does not help. Scale does not insert a document the training set never contained.

The third is that knowledge is dynamic and time-sensitive. The weights are frozen at the training date. Session 21 demonstrated the static version of this: Word2Vec's 2013 vectors have no entry for COVID, selfie, blockchain or emoji. Session 22 repeated it for GloVe's 2014 file. Retrieval is the answer that does not require retraining. The model's weights stay where they were; the documents are current.

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, is the method that addresses all three. SLP3's framing:

SLP3 §11.4

"we use IR techniques to retrieve documents from some specified store of documents that are likely to have useful information. Then we use a large language model to generate an answer conditioned on these documents in addition to the original query."

Two components: a retriever and a generator. The room has now built both halves — sparse retrieval in Unit I, dense retrieval from sessions 32–33, and generation from sessions 27–33. Nothing in the diagram below is a new model. RAG is those two things connected.

Given a collection D and a query q, the basic algorithm is three steps. Call the retriever for the top-k passages R(q). Build a prompt that contains those passages and the query. Call a language model with the prompt. SLP3's schematic, for the question What year was the premiere of The Magic Flute?:

retrieved passage 1
retrieved passage 2
...
retrieved passage k
Based on these texts, answer this question:
What year was the premiere of The Magic Flute?

The generation is then conditioned on the retrieved passages as well as on the query. RAG "can help mitigate hallucination, by giving the model a set of trusted documents"; it lets a model work over proprietary data it was never trained on; and it addresses the fact that knowledge is dynamic. Grounding the answer in a passage also lets the system show the user where the answer came from, which is a different kind of help: not a guarantee that the answer is true, but a citation the user can check.

It is not a guarantee. Retrieved passages can be irrelevant or wrong, and the generator can still hallucinate from them. A larger model does not fix a document it never saw; retrieval does not make generation faithful. Both halves remain necessary.

Question 1 of 2

What problem does RAG solve that a larger model does not?

Correct answer: proprietary or post-cut-off knowledge. A larger model still has a training date and a training set. Internal documents, last week's news, and a medical record were not in either.

The second is backwards: RAG adds a retrieval step, so it is slower, not faster. The third confuses the two components; the generator still needs an encoder-decoder, and the expensive retriever is an encoder. The fourth is the claim the last paragraph declined to make. Trusted documents reduce hallucination. They do not eliminate it, and a retrieved passage can itself be wrong.

Evaluating question answering

§11.6 gives two metrics, chosen by the form of the answer. For multiple-choice questions such as MMLU, we report exact match: the percentage of predicted answers that match the gold answer exactly. For free-text answers such as Natural Questions, we report token F1: treat the prediction and the gold as bags of tokens, compute F1 on each question, and average over questions.

Lab 6 defined precision, recall, and F1 as their harmonic mean, and computed all three by hand from a confusion matrix. That was week 6. Token F1 is the same three quantities, pointed at overlap of tokens rather than at class labels. Session 34's chrF was the same move pointed at character n-grams. The F1 from that lab is β=1; chrF commonly sets β=2. Token F1 is back at β=1.

A short illustration. Gold: the louvre is in paris. Prediction: the louvre museum is in paris.

gold tokens:  the, louvre, is, in, paris              (5)
pred tokens:  the, louvre, museum, is, in, paris      (6)
overlap:      5

precision = 5/6     recall = 5/5     F1 = 0.91
exact match = 0

Exact match scores the extra word as a complete miss. Token F1 scores it as a partial hit. Neither is the thing we actually care about, which is whether the answer is right. Session 14's trade, restated: intrinsic evaluation is inexpensive and approximate; extrinsic evaluation is expensive and definitive; a gain on the proxy does not guarantee a gain on the task. Perplexity, chrF, token F1 — every application session ends up needing a metric, and every metric is a proxy.

One more distinction from §11.5, because the lab depends on it. Closed-book QA answers from the model's parameters alone, with no retrieved document. Open-book QA is given one or more documents — via RAG, or because a dataset already attached a passage to the question. Natural Questions can be run either way; MMLU is closed-book. There is a third thing, and it is not either of those.

Lab 12 is a different task

SLP3 teaches question answering as prompting, improved with retrieval. Lab 12 asks for fine-tuning for question answering, which is a different task: extractive span prediction on a fine-tuned encoder. Given a question and a passage, the model marks the start and end of the answer span inside that passage — closer to session 7's span-finding than to generating a sentence. SLP3 mentions SQuAD only in its bibliography and does not teach the method. The HuggingFace course chapter on question answering is the lab's source. A student who meets span prediction in the lab has not missed a lecture; they have met the other half of the clause.

Extractive and abstractive summarisation

Practical NLP chapter 7 opens on a distinction, and the distinction is the teachable core. Extractive summarisation selects existing sentences from the source and arranges them. Abstractive summarisation generates new text.

Extractive cannot produce a sentence the document does not contain, so it cannot paraphrase, compress two facts into one clause, or repair a badly written source. It also cannot be wrong in the way abstractive summarisation can: it cannot hallucinate, because every sentence it emits was in the input. That is the trade, and it is the same shape as extractive-versus-generative throughout the course — span prediction versus RAG, a generation from a retrieved sentence versus a generation from parameters.

Abstractive summarisation is session 27's encoder-decoder, unchanged: a long document in, a short document out, different lengths, no word-to-word alignment. The notebook's abstractive example uses T5 (Text-To-Text Transfer Transformer), which is an encoder-decoder transformer. Session 31's architecture is doing the work.

Question 2 of 2

Which summarisation approach can hallucinate?

Correct answer: abstractive. Hallucination, as §11.4 defined it, is a response not faithful to the facts. Generating new text is what makes that possible. Extractive can select a misleading sentence, or miss the point of the document; it cannot emit a sentence that was not there.

The second uses the word for a different failure. The third flattens a structural guarantee. The fourth would be true of extractive and is the reason abstractive is the one that can invent: it is a generator, and a generator is not bound to the input tokens.

Sentence-scoring methods, and T5

The notebook demonstrates a family of extractive methods through the library sumy: Luhn, latent semantic analysis, LexRank and TextRank, plus a BERT-based extractive summariser. They are sentence-scoring methods. Each ranks the sentences of the document and selects the top few. We will not derive any of them. One is worth naming because the similarity it uses is already in the course: TextRank is PageRank over a sentence-similarity graph, and the similarity is cosine, from session 19.

One cell of that notebook will not run on this course's environment. It contains from gensim.summarization import summarize. That module was removed in gensim 4.0. Lab 8 and session 22 need gensim 4.4, which is what classroom/pyproject.toml pins, so the two requirements cannot both hold. The sumy and summa examples and the T5 example are unaffected. The gensim cell is not a summariser this room can call.

T5 takes the document as input and generates a shorter document. That is the encoder-decoder of session 27, with the transformer blocks of session 31, trained as a text-to-text model: the same weights, pointed at whatever prefix names the task. Summarisation is one such prefix. The notebook's 2020 claim that abstractive summarisation is "more of a research topic than a practical application" is a statement about the tooling of that year, not about the architecture. The architecture was finished in this course four sessions ago.

Where this leaves us

T3.11 and T3.13 are complete, in the form an hour that carries both can complete them. Question answering, as SLP3 teaches it, is retrieval plus generation: Unit I's pipeline, with session 32's encoder repairing vocabulary mismatch, feeding session 27's generator. The three reasons to retrieve rather than to prompt are hallucination, proprietary data, and a world that kept moving after the weights were frozen. Exact match and token F1 score the output against a gold answer, and like chrF and perplexity they are proxies. Lab 12's span-prediction task is a different thing.

Summarisation splits into extractive and abstractive. Extractive selects and cannot hallucinate; abstractive generates and can. The extractive family ranks sentences; TextRank does it with cosine. Abstractive is the encoder-decoder, and T5 is that architecture with a text-to-text prefix.

Session 36 is chatbots, and the last hour of the course. RAG is the bridge it will use: a modern assistant answering from documents is a retriever plus a generator, which is what this session assembled.

The main points

Sparse retrieval needs exact word overlap; that is the vocabulary mismatch problem, and dense retrieval with a BERT encoder is the modern repair. RAG is a retriever plus a generator: retrieve passages, condition generation on those passages and the query. It mitigates hallucination, reaches proprietary data, and tracks knowledge that post-dates the weights; it does not guarantee a faithful answer. Exact match and token F1 evaluate QA, the second being lab 6's F1 pointed at bags of tokens. Lab 12's QA is extractive span prediction, not RAG. Extractive summarisation selects sentences and cannot hallucinate; abstractive generates new text and can, and is session 27's encoder-decoder. T5 is that architecture. Every application session needed a metric, and every metric is a proxy.
