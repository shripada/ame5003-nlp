The inverted index

Keep only the ones. For each word, a list of the documents it appears in — and a fast way to answer a query by walking two lists at once.

Where session 8 left off

The incidence matrix was 99.8% zeros, and those zeros carried no information we needed. The correction we arrived at was to stop storing the zeros and keep only the ones. This session develops that principle into the data structure used by real search engines.

Building the inverted index

An inverted index has two parts:

  • A dictionary — every term in the collection.
  • For each term, a postings list — the document IDs where that term appears, kept in sorted order.

It is called inverted because it reverses the natural direction of the mapping. A document normally points to the words it contains; here each word points to the documents that contain it. Built from the same six Shakespeare plays as in session 8, numbered 1 to 6, the index is:

Antony     →  1 → 2 → 6
Brutus     →  1 → 2 → 4
Caesar     →  1 → 2 → 4 → 5 → 6
Calpurnia  →  2
Cleopatra  →  1
mercy      →  1 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6
worser     →  1 → 3 → 4 → 5

Compare this with the matrix row for Caesar, which was 1 1 0 1 1 1: six cells to record what the postings list 1 2 4 5 6 records in five numbers, with no zeros stored. A word appearing in three documents requires a list of three entries, whether the collection contains six documents or six billion. The size of a postings list depends on the word, not on the size of the collection. This is where the saving comes from.

Answering a query by merging postings lists

We now turn to evaluating a query, beginning with a single AND from the session 8 example:

Brutus AND mercy

We require the documents appearing in both postings lists, that is their intersection. Because both lists are sorted, it is not necessary to compare every pair. Instead we traverse both lists simultaneously with two pointers, at each step advancing the pointer at the smaller document ID:

Brutus:  1 → 2 → 4
mercy:   1 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6

step 1:  1 vs 1   equal   → keep 1, advance both
step 2:  2 vs 3   2 < 3   → advance Brutus (2 can't be in mercy now)
step 3:  4 vs 3   4 > 3   → advance mercy
step 4:  4 vs 4   equal   → keep 4, advance both
         Brutus is finished → stop

result:  1 → 4      (Antony and Cleopatra, Hamlet)

Each pointer only moves forward, so each list is traversed once. If the lists have lengths x and y, the work required is about x + y rather than x × y comparisons. This linear cost is why search returns results quickly, and it is possible only because the lists are sorted. Maintaining sorted postings at build time is what makes the query fast.

Why the postings lists are sorted

When 2 in one list is smaller than 3 in the other, it follows that 2 cannot appear later in the second list, since every remaining entry is larger. It can therefore be skipped, with no need to reconsider it. Unsorted lists provide no such guarantee, which forces a comparison of every pair.

Completing the query

The full query was Brutus AND Caesar AND NOT Calpurnia. The same operation is applied in sequence:

Brutus  1 2 4   INTERSECT   Caesar  1 2 4 5 6     →   1 2 4
        1 2 4   AND NOT     Calpurnia  2           →   1 4

NOT Calpurnia means removing any document in Calpurnia's list, so 2 is discarded, leaving 1 4: Antony and Cleopatra and Hamlet. This is the same answer the matrix produced in session 8, obtained without storing a single zero.

An optimisation: process the rarest term first

For Brutus AND Caesar AND Calpurnia, begin with the shortest postings list. Calpurnia occurs in only one document, so intersecting it first leaves at most one candidate to check against the others. Processing terms from rarest to commonest minimises the total work. This is a standard query optimisation.

Exercise

Using the index above, carry out the merge for Brutus AND Antony by hand. Brutus is 1 2 4 and Antony is 1 2 6. Which documents are returned?

Brutus:  1 → 2 → 4
Antony:  1 → 2 → 6

1 vs 1   equal   → keep 1, advance both
2 vs 2   equal   → keep 2, advance both
4 vs 6   4 < 6   → advance Brutus
         Brutus finished → stop

result:  1 → 2

The result is Antony and Cleopatra and Julius Caesar. The 4 and the 6 never match, and the two-pointer traversal passes them without ever comparing 4 with 1 or 2.

Question 1 of 2

What does a postings list contain?

Correct answer: the sorted IDs of the documents that contain one term.

The dictionary holds the terms, and each term points to its postings list, the documents in which it appears, in sorted order: Caesar → 1 2 4 5 6. The sorting is what makes the merge fast.

Question 2 of 2

Why can two postings lists be intersected in roughly x + y steps instead of x × y?

Correct answer: both lists are sorted, so each pointer only moves forward.

Sorted order means that when one ID is smaller than the other, it cannot appear later in the other list, so it can be skipped and never revisited. Each list is traversed once, for a total of about x + y steps. Without the sorting, every pair must be checked.

Summary

The main points

An inverted index stores, for each term, a sorted list of the documents that contain it — only the ones, never the zeros. Queries are answered by merging these sorted lists with a two-pointer walk that runs in linear time, which is exactly why web-scale search returns in milliseconds.

So far, however, a document either matches or it does not, and the result is an unordered set. Real search systems place the best-matching document first. Ranking is the subject of session 10, where numbers replace the 0s and 1s.
