Cosine similarity

Last session left a number on the board and refused to explain it. Today we derive it — and then use it to get session 17's clustering back.

Where session 18 left off

Session 18 ended with the following. Julius Caesar's raw column, and a document consisting of the same play printed twice:

d=[7, 62, 1, 2]length 62.432d=[14, 124, 2, 4]length 124.87

Euclidean distance62.43completely different documents
cosine1.0000identical documents

Two measurements on the same pair of vectors give opposite results. The first reports that a play and the same play printed twice are as far apart as anything in the collection. The second reports that they are identical.

The second is correct, and this session derives it.

Why Euclidean distance is unsuitable

Consider the vectors drawn rather than listed. Every document vector begins at the origin and ends at a point, and Euclidean distance measures the gap between the two endpoints. That is a well-defined quantity; it is simply not the one we want.

Doubling a document does not rotate its vector. It moves the endpoint further out along the same line, leaving the direction unchanged. If we measure the angle between two vectors instead of the gap between their endpoints, doubling a document changes nothing.

The idea, before the formula

Vector length here reflects mainly how much was written, which is not the quantity we set out to measure. We should compare direction rather than magnitude, so that a long document and a short document on the same subject are counted as similar.

The dot product

The dot product of two vectors multiplies them component by component and adds up the results:

a⋅b=∑iaibi=a1b1+a2b2+⋯+anbn

This is already an approximate similarity score. It is large when the two vectors have large values in the same positions, and exactly zero when there is no position at which both are non-zero, which is the case for two documents sharing no words.

It also has the defect we are trying to remove, in a pronounced form. Taking our two vectors:

d⋅d=7×7+62×62+1×1+2×2=3898d⋅2d=7×14+62×124+1×2+2×4=7796

The dot product involving the doubled document is twice as large, not because the document is twice as relevant but because it is twice as long. A frequent word, or a long document, scores highly on the dot product for reasons of size alone.

Normalising by vector length

The correction is to divide each vector by its own length:

cos⁡(a,b)=a⋅b|a||b|where |a|=∑iai2

The denominator is best read as an instruction rather than as algebra. Dividing a vector by its own length produces a unit vector: same direction, length exactly 1.

How to think about cosine similarity

Cosine similarity is the dot product of two unit vectors.

Both vectors are reduced to length 1, which discards everything except the direction in which they point, and only then compared. No trigonometry is required to use this.The name comes from a fact of linear algebra: a⋅b=|a||b|cos⁡θ, so dividing by the two lengths leaves exactly cos⁡θ, the cosine of the angle between them. Useful to know, never needed to compute.

Completing the calculation

Applying the formula to d and 2d, we can follow what becomes of the factor of 2:

d⋅2d=∑idi(2di)=2∑idi2=2|d|2=7796|d|=62.434|2d|=∑i(2di)2=4∑idi2=2|d|=124.868
cos⁡(d,2d)=2|d|2|d|⋅2|d|=2|d|22|d|2=1

The factor of 2 appears once in the numerator and once in the denominator and cancels. The result is not approximately 1 but exactly 1, and it would be exactly 1 for any multiple: tripled, halved, or multiplied by a thousand.

Nothing in that derivation depended on our particular numbers. Scaling a vector cannot change its cosine with any other vector. This is the property we required, and it is now proved rather than assumed.

Exercise (no calculator)

Two documents, two dimensions each:

a=[3,0]b=[30,0]

What is the Euclidean distance between them? What is the cosine?

Euclidean distance: (30−3)2+02=27.

Cosine: (3×30+0×0)/(3×30)=90/90=1.

The two measurements disagree as strongly as they can, on a pair of vectors small enough to check without a calculator. b is a multiplied by ten: same direction, ten times the length. Distance reports 27; cosine reports identity.

This is the doubling example with the arithmetic removed. It shows that the result of 1.0000 above did not depend on Julius Caesar's particular counts.

The range of cosine values

Cosine runs from 1, for vectors pointing the same way, through 0 for vectors at right angles, down to −1 for vectors pointing in opposite directions.

For the vectors built throughout this unit, namely counts, tf, and tf-idf, the range is only 0 to 1. The reason is worth stating, because it is a fact about our data rather than about the formula.

Why a count vector's cosine is never negative

Counts are never negative. A word occurs three times, or zero times; it cannot occur −3 times. So every component of every document vector is ≥0, and every document vector lives in the one corner of the space where nothing is negative.

The dot product ∑iaibi is then a sum of products of non-negative numbers, which cannot be negative. Lengths are always positive. So the whole fraction is ≥0.

Geometrically, two vectors in the same orthant can be at most 90° apart. They can never point away from each other, so the cosine has a lower bound of 0.

This holds for count vectors specifically, and ceases to hold shortly. The word embeddings in sessions 21 and 22 have negative components, so their cosines can be negative and the lower bound of 0 no longer applies. The formula is the same; the range is wider.How much wider is a question worth measuring rather than assuming, and session 21 measures it on real embeddings: negative cosines do occur, but they bottom out around −0.3 rather than −1. Trained vectors sit in a narrower cone than the formula allows.

For the present: 1 means the same direction, and 0 means no words in common.

It has no component on the one axis that still carries weight, and its direction is set by the remaining near-zero values. This is the source of 0.343 and 0.110, which represent not slightly lower similarity but a position close to perpendicular to the other three plays.

The central result

Cosine fixes a length problem, not a feature problem.

Session 18's difficulty was never that the vectors were the wrong length. It was that after weighting, good was exactly zero and fool and wit were reduced to hundredths, leaving battle as the only feature of appreciable size, so that direction is determined almost entirely by a play's battle count. Measuring the angle between poor features yields the angle between poor features. No similarity measure can recover information that is not present in the vector.

Comparing the first two rows against the last four, every play's nearest neighbour is now a play of its own genre: the comedies pair at 0.975, the histories at 0.993, and all four cross-genre pairs fall below both. The clustering from session 17 has been recovered.

It is worth comparing this against raw counts precisely. On the unlogged counts the ranking also comes out correct, but the margin is very small. As You Like It ↔ Twelfth Night is 0.9499 and As You Like It ↔ Henry V is 0.9490, so the comedy pair wins by nine ten-thousandths.

A margin of that size is not a usable result. good's very large counts dominate every vector and pull all four plays into nearly the same direction, so every cosine is compressed against 1 and the genre signal survives only in the fourth decimal place. The logarithmic damping is what separates the values: 0.975 against 0.934 is a difference that can be relied on.

The three comparisons together

Raw counts with cosine: correct in ranking and unusable in practice. The genre pairs do come out on top, by less than a thousandth, because one common word dominates and all four documents look alike.

tf-idf with cosine: worse. On this collection, idf reduced to zero the words carrying the genre signal.

log-tf with cosine: the genres separate cleanly.

This does not show that idf is a poor method. idf answers a different question, namely how distinctive a word is within a given collection, and across 37 Shakespeare plays fool and wit are not distinctive. Session 18's conclusions stand. What this session adds is that the weighting and the similarity measure are two separate choices, and both appear in the result.

Question 1 of 2

Why can the cosine between two raw word-count vectors never be negative?

Correct answer: because counts are never negative.

This is a fact about the data, not about the formula. ∑iaibi is a sum of products of non-negative numbers, so it cannot come out negative, and the lengths in the denominator are always positive.

The formula itself is perfectly happy to return −1 — no absolute value, and the division by lengths only guarantees the range −1 to 1, not 0 to 1. That matters in a few weeks: word embeddings have negative components, so their cosines really can come out negative. (Session 21 measures how negative they get in practice, and the answer is less dramatic than the formula permits.) The last option is wrong for a different reason — two documents sharing no words at all give a dot product of exactly 0, which is a perfectly ordinary answer.

Question 2 of 2

Cosine did not repair session 18's tf-idf Shakespeare vectors — a comedy is still closer to a history than to the other comedy. Why not?

Correct answer: cosine removes a length effect, and length was not the problem.

After tf-idf weighting, good was exactly zero and fool and wit were down to hundredths, so only battle still carried anything. Three of the four plays then point in nearly the same direction because one surviving word decides it, and Twelfth Night — which never uses battle — is left pointing off at almost a right angle to them. Neither of those is a fact about length, so switching from distance to angle cannot touch it.

Nothing about direction can recover information the weighting removed. The fix was not a better similarity measure — it was different features, or in our case dropping the reweighting that had destroyed the ones we had.

A second example, with word vectors

Everything so far has compared documents. The same formula compares words, and this is SLP3's own example — counts of how often each word appears near pie, data and computer in a corpus:

piedatacomputer
cherry 4428 2
digital 5 16831670
information5 39823325
cos(cherry, information)      =  0.018
cos(digital, information)     =  0.996

information is far closer to digital than to cherry, which is the correct result. No information about meaning was supplied; only the counts of which words occur nearby.

Note also that information's vector is much longer than digital's, 5188 against 2371, because it is simply a more frequent word. The raw dot product would have made every comparison involving information appear strong for that reason alone. Cosine divides this out and compares direction only.

This table is worth retaining. A word represented as a vector of the words it occurs alongside is the central idea of sessions 21 and 22, and cosine is how those vectors are compared.

Where cosine similarity is used later

This is the most frequently used formula in the course, and the places it recurs are worth naming.

  • Search. Session 10 ranked documents by summing tf-idf scores. A real engine instead represents the query as a vector as well and ranks every document by its cosine with that query vector. SLP3 works a tiny example: for the query sweet love, a document containing both words scores 0.747 and one containing only sweet scores 0.0779 — same formula, one vector of which happens to be a query.
  • Embeddings, sessions 21–22. Every "which words are similar to this one" question is a cosine. Every nearest-neighbour list is sorted by cosine.
  • Everything built on embeddings thereafter: retrieval, clustering, recommendation, and duplicate detection. When a modern system reports that two things are similar, a cosine has almost always been computed.

Summary

The main points

Cosine similarity is (a⋅b)/(|a||b|) — the dot product of the two vectors after each has been shrunk to length 1, which is to say a comparison of direction with length thrown away. Because scaling a vector cancels out of numerator and denominator, a document and the same document printed twice have a cosine of exactly 1 when the vectors are raw counts, and for count vectors the whole range is 0 to 1. But cosine only removes the length effect: on Shakespeare it recovered the genre clustering from log-tf vectors and could do nothing at all for session 18's tf-idf ones, because bad features stay bad however you measure the angle between them.
