MSIS · AME 5003 · Unit III · Session 34

Machine translation

Session 33 closed by saying the architecture of Unit III is finished. This session is the first application of it: everything a working translation system needs that a transformer diagram does not show.

What the architecture does not cover

Session 33 closed on a sentence this session is built to honour. The architecture of Unit III is finished: an encoder-decoder transformer, wired by the cross-attention of session 31, sitting in the three-component shape of session 27. SLP3 §12.2 states the consequence in one clause — "the standard architecture for MT is the encoder-decoder transformer" — and that is now a fact the room already has, rather than a preview.

What remains is everything a working translation system needs that a diagram of those blocks does not show. Two pieces of it are new machinery: a decoding rule more careful than taking the best word at each step, and the first evaluation metric since session 14 that compares a model's output against a human reference. The rest is why the task is hard, and a finding about what the system does with gender it was never given.

Why translation is hard

Session 27 already made the argument with one pair:

source (English) :  the    green   witch   arrived
target (Spanish) :  llegó  la      bruja   verde
glossed          :  arrived the    witch   green

The alignment is 4, 1, 3, 2. No one-label-per-token model can produce it, and the two sentences need not even be the same length. That was enough to motivate the encoder-decoder. §12.1 is the general case: the same reordering is one instance of a systematic phenomenon, not a quirk of Spanish.

The study of those systematic differences is linguistic typology, and §12.1 organises the ones that matter for translation into four kinds of translation divergence.

Word order. English, Spanish, French and Mandarin are SVO languages — subject, then verb, then object. Hindi and Japanese are SOV: the verb comes at the end. Irish and Arabic are VSO. Two SVO languages can still disagree about adjectives: English puts them before the noun (green witch), Spanish after it (bruja verde), which is exactly the 4, 1, 3, 2 the room already accepted. The alignment problem is what typology looks like in a single sentence.

Lexical. English bass is Spanish lubina (the fish) or bajo (the instrument). English brother is Mandarin gege or didi, older or younger. The source word does not name its own translation; the context does. Sometimes there is no word at all: SLP3's example is Mandarin xiào and Japanese oyakōkō, which English can only approximate with phrases like filial piety. That is a lexical gap.

Morphological. Vietnamese is isolating, roughly one morpheme per word; a polysynthetic language can pack a whole English sentence into one. Translating across that range means working below the word level, which is why modern MT systems use the subword tokenisation session 3 taught — and which is the next section, not a new algorithm.

Referential density. English requires a pronoun when the referent is given. Spanish can drop it; Japanese and Chinese drop far more. SLP3 calls languages that omit more referentially sparse, or cold, and languages that spell the referent out referentially dense, or hot. Translating from a cold language into English forces the model to insert a pronoun it was never shown. That returns at the end of the hour, as the setup for a measurable bias.

The point of the four

Session 27 needed one pair to show that sequence labelling cannot translate. The four divergences say why a pair like that is normal. Word order, lexical choice, morphology and dropped referents all require the output to be a function of the whole source, of a length the source does not determine. That is the job the encoder-decoder was built for. Nothing in this section is a new model.

A shared vocabulary, and parallel text

Session 3 taught byte-pair encoding and named the paper that introduced it to NLP: Sennrich, Haddow and Birch, 2016, Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units. The title is this session's task. Session 32 met WordPiece as BERT's 30,000-token vocabulary, which closed that loop for an encoder. Here the same family of algorithms solves a problem specific to translation.

MT systems use a vocabulary that is fixed in advance, built by running a subword algorithm on a corpus that contains both languages, and — this is the part that is not obvious — the source and the target share that vocabulary.

SLP3 §12.2.1

"A shared vocabulary is used for the source and target languages, which makes it easy to copy tokens (like names) from source to target."

A name such as Manipal should survive the trip unchanged. With a shared subword vocabulary the model can copy the pieces rather than invent a translation of them. The same choice also makes it natural to translate between languages like English or Hindi, which use spaces, and languages like Chinese or Thai, which do not: the units are subwords either way, so the encoder-decoder never has to be told which side of that line it is on.

The training data is a parallel corpus, or bitext: sentences in the source language paired with their translations in the target. Europarl, extracted from proceedings of the European Parliament, and the United Nations Parallel Corpus are the standard large examples. One line is enough; the model is trained to maximise the probability of the target tokens given the source tokens, which is session 12's chain rule pointed at a pair of languages rather than at a single sentence.

The problem with greedy decoding

Session 27 showed what the decoder is fed at inference: its own previous output, rather than the gold word teacher forcing had supplied during training. It did not say how that output is chosen. The obvious rule is to take, at each step, the word with the highest probability given the prefix so far. That is greedy decoding, and it is SLP3's equation (12.14):

wˆt=argmaxw∈VP(w|w<t)(12.14)

A problem with it is named directly:

SLP3 §12.4

"what looks high probability at word t might turn out to have been the wrong choice once we get to word t + 1."

SLP3's own illustration is a made-up search tree over a three-word vocabulary, yes, ok and EOS. The numbers below are Figure 12.7's, recomputed as products so the comparison is visible in one table.

pathprobability
ok ok EOS 0.4 × 0.7 × 1.0 = 0.280 — the true best
yes yes … 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.200
yes ok … 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.150
yes EOS 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.100

Greedy takes yes first, because 0.5 is larger than 0.4. No continuation of yes can reach 0.280: the best remaining multiplier is 0.4, which gives 0.200, and even a certain EOS after that cannot catch the other path. The best first word is not the first word of the best sentence.

Beam search

Beam search keeps the top k partial hypotheses at each step rather than one. Extend all of them by every word in the vocabulary, score the k×|V| candidates by the product of the path so far and the next-word probability, and keep the best k again. The number k is the beam width. When a hypothesis generates EOS it is complete, removed from the frontier, and the beam shrinks by one; search continues until the beam is empty. For MT, SLP3's usual widths are 5 to 10.

At k=1 this is greedy decoding. Beam search generalises the rule the obvious choice already was; it does not replace it with a different kind of object.

It is a heuristic, not a guarantee. A wider beam explores more and still prunes, and the globally best sequence can be lost at any step — exactly as ok is lost at step 1 when k=1. Exhaustive search would find the optimum, at a cost of |V|T sentences of length T, which is not feasible. Dynamic programming does not apply either: generation has long-distance dependencies between output decisions, so the Viterbi argument from tagging does not carry over. Students routinely believe beam search finds the most probable sentence. It keeps the most probable prefixes it has not yet discarded.

One alternative, which we will not derive, inverts the objective rather than widening the search. Minimum Bayes risk decoding chooses the translation likely to score best on the evaluation metric, not the most probable one. SLP3 says it can work even better than beam search, and names chrF and BERTScore as the metrics to maximise — which is the next section's machinery, pointed back at this one.

Question 1 of 2

Why does greedy decoding sometimes miss the most probable sentence?

Correct answer: the best first word need not begin the best sequence. In the table, yes is the best first word and ok ok EOS is the best sentence. That is the entire argument.

The second is backwards: beam search with k=1 is greedy, so greedy is the special case, not an approximation of the general one. The third confuses two different gaps. Teacher forcing is about what the decoder is fed (gold prefix versus its own output); greedy versus beam is about how the next token is chosen (one hypothesis versus k). Session 27 taught the first; this session is the second. The fourth is false on its face — greedy can emit EOS; in the table it is one of the three options at every step.

Evaluation: humans, then a proxy

Translations are judged on two dimensions. Adequacy is whether the translation captures the meaning of the source; fluency is whether it is grammatical, clear and natural in the target language. Humans produce the best evaluations of both. They are also slow and expensive, which is why automatic metrics exist.

SLP3 §12.6.2

"While humans produce the best evaluations of machine translation output, running a human evaluation can be time consuming and expensive. For this reason automatic metrics are often used as temporary proxies."

That is session 14's argument, restated for a new task. Extrinsic evaluation is true and expensive; intrinsic evaluation is a cheap approximate stand-in. Twenty sessions ago the stand-in was perplexity, scoring a language model on text it had not seen. Here the stand-in scores a translation against a human reference. Same trade: inexpensive and approximate against expensive and definitive, and a gain on the proxy does not guarantee a gain on the thing we actually care about.

chrF

SLP3 leads with chrF, character F-score (Popović, 2015), and calls it "the simplest and most robust metric for MT evaluation". BLEU — the name nearly every student has heard, and the one almost none of this room has computed — is filed among "many other earlier related metrics like BLEU, METEOR, TER". We will name BLEU in a moment, because a five-minute search will produce it. We will not lead with it, because the reading does not.

chrF is an F-score. Lab 6 defined precision and recall, and F1 as their harmonic mean, computed by hand from a confusion matrix. That was week 6; the same three quantities return here, pointed at character n-grams rather than at class labels. The F1 from that lab is the case β=1. chrF commonly sets β=2, which weighs recall twice as much as precision:

chrFβ=(1+β2)chrP⋅chrRβ2⋅chrP+chrR(12.20)

At β=2 that is:

chrF2=5⋅chrP⋅chrR4⋅chrP+chrR

Given a hypothesis and a reference, and a parameter k for the longest character n-gram to count, chrP is the average of the k precisions (what fraction of the hypothesis n-grams occur in the reference) and chrR is the average of the k recalls (what fraction of the reference n-grams occur in the hypothesis). The two parameters are easy to mix up: k is how long the n-grams go; β weights recall against precision.

The whole difference from BLEU is already in the name. These are character n-grams, not word n-grams, and chrF therefore ignores spaces. A student who has met BLEU will tokenise into words and get every count wrong. Punctuation stays; spaces come out first.

SLP3's own worked example, with k=β=2. The reference is witness for the past, and the hypothesis is witness of the past, — one preposition different, comma kept:

REF:  witnessforthepast,      18 unigrams, 17 bigrams
HYP:  witnessofthepast,       17 unigrams, 16 bigrams

Matching unigrams: 17. Matching bigrams: 13. Then:

precisionrecall
unigram17/17 = 1.00017/18 = 0.944
bigram13/16 = 0.81313/17 = 0.765

Average the two precisions and the two recalls, then apply the β=2 formula:

chrP=(1.000+0.813)/2=0.906chrR=(0.944+0.765)/2=0.855chrF2=5⋅0.906⋅0.8554⋅0.906+0.855=0.864

SLP3 prints this as .86; the unrounded value is .864. A second hypothesis in the chapter, past witness, scores .62 against the same reference — worse, as it should, because the words are there and the order is not. The book works out the first of the two and leaves the second as exercise 12.1, which is the same arithmetic on a different pair.

BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) is the metric the room will encounter elsewhere. It is word-based rather than character-based, and it is "purely precision-based rather than combining precision and recall". It also needs a brevity penalty, clipped n-gram counts, and a standardised tokeniser (SacreBLEU) before two numbers can be compared. chrF is what we compute; BLEU is what a paper from 2015 or a leaderboard for translation into English may still report.

Embedding-based metrics such as BERTScore go one step further: they compare BERT embeddings of the hypothesis and the reference rather than surface overlap, which handles paraphrase that chrF cannot. That is session 32's contextual embeddings doing evaluation work. One sentence is enough; we do not compute it.

Exercise

Compute chrF2 with k=2 for the pair below. Follow the worked example, including the first step.

REF:  the green witch
HYP:  the witch green

Report the unigram and bigram counts, both precisions, both recalls, chrP, chrR, and chrF2.

Spaces out first. thegreenwitch is 13 unigrams and 12 bigrams; thewitchgreen is 13 unigrams and 12 bigrams. The two strings are anagrams, so every unigram matches: 13.

The bigrams are not anagrams of each other. Reference: th he eg gr re ee en nw wi it tc ch. Hypothesis: th he ew wi it tc ch hg gr re ee en. Ten match; the hypothesis has ew and hg which the reference does not, and the reference has eg and nw which the hypothesis does not.

Unigram P = 13/13 = 1, unigram R = 13/13 = 1. Bigram P = 10/12 = 0.833, bigram R = 10/12 = 0.833. chrP = chrR = (1 + 0.833)/2 = 0.917. When precision equals recall, chrF2 equals that common value: 5 × 0.917 × 0.917 / (4 × 0.917 + 0.917) = 0.917.

The instructive wrong answer. Tokenising into words gives three matching unigrams and zero matching bigrams (the green versus the witch, green witch versus witch green), so chrP = chrR = 0.5 and chrF2 = 0.5. That is a plausible-looking number, and it is BLEU's instinct applied to chrF. The spaces had to come out first.

Question 2 of 2

What does chrF compare?

Correct answer: character n-gram overlap, precision and recall combined. That is the definition, and both halves of it matter: characters, not words; F-score, not precision alone.

The second is BLEU, and it is the answer most people who have heard of a translation metric will give. The third is session 14's metric, pointed at the wrong object — perplexity scores a language model on a corpus, not a hypothesis against a reference. The fourth is BERTScore, which this session named in one sentence as the metric that handles paraphrase chrF cannot see.

Bias, amplification, and abstention

Translating from a language with a gender-neutral pronoun, or one that drops pronouns, into English, where pronouns are obligatory and gendered, forces the system to choose a gender it was never given. That is the referential-density divergence from the start of the hour, now pointed at a person. Hungarian ő is gender-neutral; Spanish often drops the pronoun altogether. SLP3's Figure 12.12, from Prates et al. 2019:

Hungarian (gender-neutral)English MT output
ő egy ápolóshe is a nurse
ő egy tudóshe is a scientist
ő egy mérnökhe is an engineer
ő egy tanárshe is a teacher
ő egy vezérigazgatóhe is a CEO

The easy claim is that the model reflects its training data. Prates et al. found something stronger, and SLP3 states it directly: the stereotypes "can't completely be accounted for by gender bias in US labor statistics, because the biases are amplified by MT systems, with pronouns being mapped to male or female gender with a probability higher than if the mapping was based on actual labor employment statistics."

The system is more biased than the data it learned from. That is a measurable claim, it is checkable, and it is a different finding from "models reflect society".

A second result sits next to it. The WinoMT challenge set (Stanovsky et al. 2019) shows MT systems performing worse on non-stereotypical roles — The doctor asked the nurse to help her in the operation — where the pronoun and the occupation disagree with the stereotype the table above records.

The section does not end on a debiasing recipe. It ends on abstention. MT systems are used in medical and legal settings where a wrong translation can cause harm and a human translator may be unavailable or delayed. SLP3's closing requirement:

SLP3 §12.7

"In order to 'do no harm', systems need ways to assign confidence values to candidate translations, so they can abstain from giving incorrect translations that may cause harm."

Knowing when not to answer is the open problem the chapter names.

Where this leaves us

T3.10 is complete. The room already had the architecture; this session added what the architecture does not cover. Translation is hard because languages diverge in word order, in the lexicon, in morphology and in how much they say out loud. A shared subword vocabulary, the algorithm session 3 taught, is what lets a name copy rather than be invented. Greedy decoding misses the most probable sentence because the best first word need not begin it; beam search keeps k prefixes and still does not guarantee the optimum. chrF scores a hypothesis against a human reference by character n-gram F-score, which is session 14's proxy trade pointed at a new task, and BLEU is the older word-precision metric the rest of the literature still uses. And when the source does not mark gender, the system does not merely inherit the training data's stereotypes: it amplifies them.

Session 35 carries two syllabus clauses in one hour — question answering and text summarisation — and it opens on a retrieval problem this course built in Unit I. RAG waits; that session will say why Unit I is half of a modern system.

The main points

Translation divergences — word order, lexical choice, morphology, referential density — are why the output is a function of the whole source, of a length the source does not determine. A shared subword vocabulary across both languages lets the model copy names and makes space-separated and unsegmented languages the same kind of input. Training data is a parallel corpus. Greedy decoding takes the argmax at each step and can miss the globally most probable sentence; beam search keeps the top k prefixes, reduces to greedy at k=1, and is a heuristic, not a guarantee. chrF is character n-gram F-score against a human reference, spaces removed first; BLEU is the word-precision metric students will meet elsewhere. Automatic metrics are cheap proxies for human judgements of adequacy and fluency — session 14's trade. Gender-neutral sources into English produce stereotypes the system amplifies beyond the labour statistics in the training data, and the open problem SLP3 names is knowing when to abstain.
