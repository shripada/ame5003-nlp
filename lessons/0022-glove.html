GloVe

One loss function and one co-occurrence matrix. What makes the session worth an hour is the question it raises about Word2Vec, and the answer, which is not the one GloVe's own paper gives.

Where Unit II stands

In session 8 a document was a row of ones and zeros: the incidence matrix, recording presence or absence and nothing in between. Session 17 replaced the bit with a count. Session 18 reweighted that count by how rare the word was. Session 19 replaced comparison by length with comparison by angle. Session 21 set all of this aside and replaced counting with predicting: delete a word, predict it, and keep the weights. This session asks the question that move raises and does not answer: was prediction necessary, or would counting have worked all along, given the right quantity to count?

GloVe's objection to Word2Vec

Word2Vec slides a window across the corpus and, at each position, asks one question and then discards the window. Over a billion tokens it will encounter the pair (ice, steam) — not adjacent, but both near words such as water and cold — thousands of separate times, and the model never holds the total. Each window is treated independently.

GloVe's argument

The corpus already contains the total. Counting once how often every word appears near every other word produces a table that Word2Vec spends an entire training run approximating one window at a time. Global Vectors: build the global co-occurrence matrix, then fit vectors to it directly.

Co-occurrence ratios

Call the matrix X: Xᵢⱼ is how many times word j appears within some window of word i, counted across the whole corpus. That much is just session 19's pie/data/computer table, scaled up to every word against every word.

The paper's contribution is not the matrix, since co-occurrence matrices long predate it. It is the claim that a single probability is not the informative quantity, and a ratio of two probabilities is. Consider two words with different meanings from a shared domain, ice and steam, together with several probe words.

Exercise

Two drinks, espresso and juice, and two probe words, counted across some corpus. Both drinks happen to appear equally often — each has a total of 10,000 co-occurrences with all words — so their counts are directly comparable:

X(espresso, sugar)     =   5        X(juice, sugar)     =  45
X(espresso, caffeine)  =  80        X(juice, caffeine)  =   4

Work out the ratio P(k | espresso) / P(k | juice) for each probe word k. Which word is each ratio "about"? And if a third probe word gave a ratio close to 1, what would that tell you?

sugar: (5/10,000) ÷ (45/10,000) = 5/45 ≈ 0.11 — small, so sugar belongs to juice, not espresso.

caffeine: (80/10,000) ÷ (4/10,000) = 80/4 = 20 — large, so caffeine belongs to espresso.

A ratio near 1 would indicate that the probe word is equally associated with both, or with neither, and therefore carries no information for distinguishing the two target words. This is the central point: a plain probability states only that two words co-occur often or rarely, whereas the ratio states which of two candidate words a third word belongs with.

The equal totals are what allow the two values of 10,000 to cancel and the raw counts to be compared directly. That is a convenience of this exercise rather than a general rule: in the table below the two words have very different totals, and dividing one raw count by another would give the wrong answer.

The same calculation on real data follows, using text8 (Wikipedia text, about 17 million tokens), a window of 10, and the top 20,000 words by frequency:

solidgaswaterfashionrow total
ice 3014128232,068
steam 2 1230 08,024

The row totals are not decoration — they are what makes this a ratio of probabilities rather than a ratio of counts. ice occurs about four times as often as steam in this corpus, so every one of its raw counts is inflated by roughly the same factor. P(k | ice) is the row-ice count divided by 32,068, and P(k | steam) is the row-steam count divided by 8,024:

ratio = P(k | ice) / P(k | steam) = (Xᵢₖ / 32,068) / (Xₛₖ / 8,024)

solid    (30/32,068) / (2/8,024)  = 3.75    ← larger, correctly:   ice relates to solid more than steam does
gas      (14/32,068) / (12/8,024) = 0.29    ← smaller, correctly:  steam relates to gas more than ice does
water   (128/32,068) / (30/8,024) = 1.07    ← near 1, correctly:   both relate to water about equally
fashion  (2/32,068)  / (0/8,024)  = ∞       ← steam and fashion co-occur ZERO times in this corpus

Omitting the normalisation gives solid a value of 30/2 = 15 rather than 3.75. The direction is the same, but the number implies that ice is fifteen times more associated with solid than steam is, when most of that factor is simply ice being the more frequent word.

Three of the four probes fall where the paper's table indicates. The fourth did not fail but encountered session 13's zero problem in a new setting. fashion is intended as the probe that is irrelevant to both, giving a ratio near 1. In a corpus of 17 million tokens, steam and fashion never occur within ten words of each other, so the ratio divides by zero rather than approaching 1.

The zero problem, again

Pennington et al. trained on 6 billion tokens, about 350 times this corpus. At that scale two arbitrary words almost always co-occur some nonzero number of times by chance, so their ratio approaches 1 as the theory predicts. At 17 million tokens, rare pairings reach exactly zero. Sparse counts fail in the same place they always fail: this is session 13's smoothing problem in a different matrix.

From ratio to loss

The ratio argument requires that whatever the vectors encode, the ratio should be recoverable from them. GloVe's approach is to fit the vectors to the logarithm of the raw count directly, one pair at a time:

wᵢᵀ w̃ⱼ  +  bᵢ  +  b̃ⱼ   =   log Xᵢⱼ

wᵢ is word i's vector, w̃ⱼ is word j's vector playing the context role, and bᵢ, b̃ⱼ are per-word bias terms that absorb how frequent each word is on its own, so the dot product is left to encode the relationship rather than raw frequency. Fit every vector and bias to make that equation as true as possible, over every nonzero cell of X, and you have GloVe's loss (weighting term f next):

J  =  Σᵢⱼ f(Xᵢⱼ) · (wᵢᵀw̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ − log Xᵢⱼ)²

The logarithm is used because a difference of dot products corresponds exactly to a ratio of counts under exponentiation:

The algebraic identity

log(Xᵢₖ) − log(Xⱼₖ) = log(Xᵢₖ / Xⱼₖ). If the model fits wᵢᵀw̃ₖ ≈ log Xᵢₖ and wⱼᵀw̃ₖ ≈ log Xⱼₖ for every word, then wᵢᵀw̃ₖ − wⱼᵀw̃ₖ is automatically fitting the log of the ratio from the section above — the very quantity the paper argued was the informative one. The log is not decoration. It is the thing that turns "fit a count" into "fit the ratio that actually carries meaning".

The paper's derivation, which starts from the ratio and works forward to this equation, runs to three pages. None of it is required in order to use the result, and it is omitted here.

Weighting the loss

Squared error on log Xᵢⱼ has a problem session 18 already named. A small number of extremely frequent pairs, such as (the, of) and (a, the), would dominate the loss and suppress everything else, in the same way that one very large raw count previously overwhelmed idf. GloVe's correction has the same form as session 18's log damping: a weighting function that caps how much any single pair can contribute.

f(x) = (x / x_max)^α   if x < x_max
f(x) = 1                otherwise         (paper's values: x_max = 100, α = 3/4)

Frequent pairs are capped at full weight rather than growing without bound, and very rare pairs, co-occurring once or twice, are also down-weighted, since a single accidental co-occurrence is largely noise. The same problem as in session 18, the same form of correction, applied to a different matrix.

Two vectors, added

Returning to the loss, there are two matrices, w and w̃, corresponding to Word2Vec's E and W'. Session 21 noted that some implementations offer to average the two together and that this is rarely done. GloVe is the implementation that does it. The paper reports that summing w + w̃ for each word gives marginally better vectors than either matrix alone, since both were fit to the same data in slightly different roles, and every released GloVe file has this already applied.

Question 1 of 2

GloVe's loss pulls wᵢᵀw̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ toward a target value. What is that target?

Correct answer: the log of the raw co-occurrence count, log Xᵢⱼ.

Nothing here is a probability of anything — there is no softmax, no prediction step, no sampling. GloVe is closer to matrix factorisation than to a classifier: fit a matrix of numbers, reconstruct a matrix of numbers. The log matters specifically because a difference of two such fits reconstructs the co-occurrence ratio, which is the quantity the whole method is built to recover.

The difference on good

Session 21's most awkward finding was bad ranking second-nearest to good in Word2Vec, at 0.719. The same query in GloVe gives:

good  →  better 0.766, really 0.736, always 0.717, you 0.707, well 0.705,
         excellent 0.704, very 0.697, things 0.694, think 0.690, way 0.684

cos(good, bad) = 0.645          ← real, but doesn't crack the top ten
What this result does not show

bad does not appear in GloVe's top ten neighbours of good, where it ranked second in Word2Vec. It would be natural to conclude that GloVe has solved the antonym problem. It has not. 0.645 is still a high cosine, close behind the tenth-ranked word, and this GloVe file used different preprocessing (lower-cased), a different corpus (Wikipedia and Gigaword rather than Google News), and different training hyperparameters. The more defensible reading is that two representations of similarity, trained on different data with different settings, disagree at the margins. That reading supports the point made below: the observed differences concern corpus and tuning rather than which architecture is fundamentally better.

The current answer

About the same, and the theoretical distinction is smaller than either paper's framing suggests. Levy & Goldberg (2014) proved that Skip-gram with negative sampling is implicitly factorising a matrix of shifted pointwise mutual information — which is to say, it was using global corpus statistics the entire time, just never writing them down as a matrix. Levy, Goldberg & Dagan (2015) then showed that once you match hyperparameters — window size, negative samples, subsampling — across count-based and prediction-based methods, the gap between them mostly closes. The real lesson of GloVe is not "counting beats predicting". It is that counting and predicting were never as different as either paper's abstract made them sound.

Question 2 of 2

What does later research (Levy & Goldberg) say about GloVe vs. Word2Vec?

Correct answer: Skip-gram was already using global statistics, and matched hyperparameters close most of the gap.

GloVe's own paper argues the third option is false and its pitch is the first option. Levy & Goldberg's 2014 result is what makes that pitch hard to defend: SGNS is provably factorising a PMI-based matrix, so "counts vs. prediction" was never a clean split. The 2015 follow-up measured it directly and found hyperparameters mattered more than which of the two methods you picked.

Sparse to dense, and counted to learned. Every representation in this course from session 8 onward answers the same question: how to turn a word or a document into numbers a machine can compare. Each session's answer corrected something the previous one got wrong, at the cost of some of the previous one's interpretability. Sessions 21 and 22 are where that trade-off is most pronounced: a 300-dimensional vector contains no single number whose meaning can be stated.

What none of this fixes

Word2Vec and GloVe share every limitation session 21 identified, because both supply the same kind of building block: one dense vector per word type.

  • One vector per word, regardless of how many senses it has. bank receives a single point in space, wherever the river and financial senses average out to.
  • Fixed at training time. A word absent from the training corpus has no vector at all, which is session 3's vocabulary problem unchanged. A GloVe file trained in 2014 contains nothing coined after 2014, as with Word2Vec's 2013 file.
  • Blind to order. Both are fit from unordered or window-based co-occurrence, so neither represents a sequence, only which words tend to occur alongside which others.

All three are Unit III topics. Contextual embeddings address the first, and nothing before Unit III addresses the second. The third is why Unit III opens with a model built to process a sequence as a sequence, one step at a time.

Summary

The main points

GloVe builds the global word-word co-occurrence matrix once, then fits vectors so their dot product recovers the log of each count — because a difference of two such fits reconstructs the ratio of co-occurrence probabilities, which is the quantity that actually distinguishes one word from another. A weighting function stops frequent pairs from dominating the loss, the same complaint session 18 made about raw counts. In practice it produces vectors about as good as Word2Vec's, not better, and the "global counts beat local prediction" story does not survive Levy & Goldberg's result that Skip-gram was implicitly using global statistics the whole time. That closes Unit II: sparse counted representations have become dense learned ones, and what happens next is making those dense vectors aware of sequence, context and order — Unit III.

Reading

The primary source is Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher and Christopher Manning, GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation (2014) — short, and §3 is the only section that matters for this course.

The "so which one is actually better?" section draws on two papers worth citing by name if anyone asks: Omer Levy and Yoav Goldberg, Neural Word Embedding as Implicit Matrix Factorization (NeurIPS 2014), and Levy, Goldberg and Dagan, Improving Distributional Similarity with Lessons Learned from Word2Vec (TACL 2015).

Speech and Language Processing gives GloVe two sentences, in §5.5.3 "Other kinds of static embeddings" — it names the method, says it is based on ratios of probabilities from the co-occurrence matrix, and moves on. Useful for placing GloVe among the static embeddings, but it is not a substitute for the paper on this topic. Read §5.5.3 for the placement and Pennington §3 for the method. Worth noting that the same two sentences also state Levy & Goldberg's result below — the book agrees with the honest answer.
