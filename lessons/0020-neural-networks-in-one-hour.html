Neural networks in one hour

Nothing today is usable on its own, and that is deliberate. This hour is the machinery next session needs — five ideas, each one picked because Word2Vec uses it.

Why we stop building things for an hour

Sessions 17, 18 and 19 built vectors by counting and weighting. Count the words, damp the counts with a log, reweight with idf, compare with a cosine. Every step was something you could do by hand, and every session ended with a method you could use that day.

Session 21 builds word vectors a different way: it learns them. Nobody counts anything into the vector. The vector starts as random numbers and gets adjusted, over and over, until it is good at a prediction task. That is a completely different kind of machine, and it needs machinery we have not got.

So today is the machinery. Nothing in this hour is usable on its own — there is no method at the end, no dataset, nothing to run. It exists so that next session is not magic.

What is at stake, in numbers

Lab 7 measured sentiment classification on 2,000 real movie reviews and left two baselines on the table: 0.809 accuracy from raw counts, 0.823 from sublinear TF-IDF. Those are what counting and weighting get you.

Word2Vec is the next entry in that table. It is worth an hour of prerequisites only if learned vectors can beat 0.823 — and that is a question we will be able to answer, not assert.

You have already built a linear model

Here is session 16's classifier, in log space, exactly as we wrote it:

best class  =  argmax   log P(c)  +  Σ log P(w | c)
                c in C             w in d

And here is the object we are about to define, which is supposedly the foundation of modern NLP:

score  =  b  +  Σ wᵢ xᵢ
                 i

Look at the shape of them. log P(c) is a constant added on regardless of the document — that is b. Each log P(w|c) is a fixed number attached to a word, multiplied by how many times that word occurs — that is wᵢxᵢ. Naïve Bayes in log space is a weighted sum of features. You built one in session 16 and nobody called it a neural anything.

The one difference that matters

Naïve Bayes' weights are counted. A neuron's weights are fitted.

Session 16 got its weights by counting how often each word appeared in each class and taking a log. There was no choice involved and no adjustment — count, divide, done. A neuron's weights start as arbitrary numbers and are nudged until the whole thing predicts well.

That is what buys us things counting cannot do. Counting cannot notice that a feature is useless; fitting can drive its weight to zero. Counting cannot notice that two features only matter together; fitting a second layer can.

The neuron

A neuron is a weighted sum, plus a constant, pushed through one function. That is the entire object:

y  =  f( Σ wᵢxᵢ + b )

x are the inputs, w the weights, b the bias, and f the activation function — the one non-linear step. The sum inside is often written z and called the pre-activation.

A neuron with real numbers in it

Take three features from a movie review — how many times it contains great, the and awful — and a neuron with plausible weights already in place. These weights were chosen to make a point, not trained — nothing on this page fits anything; that is lab 8's job. The arithmetic below is real:

w  =  [ 1.4,  0.0, -1.9 ]      b = -1.0
                                        f = sigmoid

review A:  x = [2, 5, 0]
           z = 1.4×2 + 0.0×5 + (-1.9)×0 - 1.0  =  1.8
           y = sigmoid(1.8)   =  0.858

review B:  x = [0, 5, 3]
           z = 1.4×0 + 0.0×5 + (-1.9)×3 - 1.0  =  -6.7
           y = sigmoid(-6.7)  =  0.001

The output is a number between 0 and 1 and reads naturally as "probability this review is positive". Review A gets 0.858, review B gets 0.001.

Worth stopping on the middle weight. the carries a weight of essentially zero, so it contributes nothing to either verdict however often it appears — and a weight near zero is a thing fitting can arrive at. Session 16 could not. Naïve Bayes counted the in both classes and dutifully carried two nearly-equal log terms around forever, which is exactly why we spent session 4 on stop-word lists. Fitted weights can make some of our preprocessing unnecessary — the model can learn to ignore a word rather than being told to.

The sigmoid

σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

It takes any number at all and squashes it into (0, 1). Large positive z → near 1, large negative → near 0, and σ(0) = 0.5 exactly. That is why its output is readable as a probability, and it is why we are showing it today: session 21's negative sampling scores a word pair as σ(u·v), precisely this function on precisely a dot product.The other activation you will meet is ReLU, f(z) = max(0, z) — pass positives through, flatten negatives to zero. It is the default in everything from session 23 onward, because it is cheap and it does not squash gradients to nothing. We will introduce it properly when Unit III needs it.

Why the non-linearity is not optional

The obvious question: why bother with f? Why not just take the weighted sum and stack those?

Because a stack of linear layers is a linear layer. Feed one into another:

layer 1:   h  =  W₁x
layer 2:   y  =  W₂h  =  W₂(W₁x)  =  (W₂W₁)x  =  Wx

W₂W₁ is just some other matrix. Three layers collapse the same way, and so do thirty. Without a non-linearity, depth buys you nothing at all — a hundred-layer linear network can compute exactly what one layer can. The activation function is the thing that stops the collapse, and it is the entire reason "deep" learning is a phrase.

⚠️ But Word2Vec is deliberately shallow

Say this now, because next session will otherwise look like it contradicts the paragraph above. Word2Vec has no hidden non-linearity. CBOW is: average some vectors, one matrix multiply, softmax. That is it.

The collapse argument is about depth, and Word2Vec is not deep — being shallow is the point of it, and it is why the thing can train on a billion words on a laptop from 2013. Depth starts in Unit III.

You will still see a sigmoid in session 21, in negative sampling. It is not a hidden layer; it is turning one dot product into one probability.

A layer is a matrix multiply

Put several neurons side by side, all reading the same inputs. Each has its own row of weights. Rather than write out one sum per neuron, stack the rows into a matrix and write it once:

y  =  f( Wx + b )

    W is (out × in)     x is (in)     b is (out)     y is (out)

Nothing has changed. Row i of W times x is exactly neuron i's weighted sum, and there are as many rows as there are neurons. This is notation, not a new idea, and it is worth doing once so that Wx + b is readable on sight next session.

Here is that notation with session 21's actual shapes in it, so that when you see them you have seen them before. Vocabulary of 10,000 words, vectors of 300 numbers:

E   embedding matrix    10000 × 300     one row per word in the vocabulary
x   averaged context      300           mean of a few rows of E — one vector
W'  output matrix       10000 × 300     one row per word again, on the way out
z   scores              10000           = W'x     one score per vocabulary word
p   probabilities       10000           = softmax(z)
⚠️ Flag this now — it is the thing session 21 turns on

The word vectors are a weight matrix.

E above is a layer's weights, fitted by the same nudging as any other weights. When session 21 finishes training and says "here are the word vectors" — those are the rows of E. There is no separate step where vectors get extracted from the model. The vectors were the model.

This surprises everybody the first time. It is worth being surprised by now rather than next week.

Softmax turns scores into a distribution

A neuron's output is a single number. Session 21 needs something else: a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary, because it is predicting which word sits in the middle of a context. Ten thousand words in, ten thousand probabilities out.

We have been handling distributions over words since session 11, but always built from counts. Softmax builds one from arbitrary scores:

              e^(zᵢ)
softmax(z)ᵢ = ────────
              Σⱼ e^(zⱼ)

Exponentiate every score, then divide each by the total. Worked on three scores:

wordscore ze^zprobability
bank 3.020.0860.836
river 1.02.718 0.113
money 0.21.221 0.051
24.0251.000

Two properties, and they are the two we need. Everything comes out positive, because e^z is positive whatever z was — including for negative scores. And everything sums to 1, because we divided by the total. That is the definition of a distribution, so the output can be handed straight to anything that expects probabilities.

Notice what the exponential did. bank beat river by 2.0 on the scores — a modest lead. In probability it beats it by a factor of 7.4. That is not a fact about these numbers: a gap of 2 always becomes a ratio of e² = 7.39, and a gap of 5 becomes a ratio of 148. Softmax exaggerates. Small differences in score become large differences in probability, which is what the "soft" and the "max" in the name are about.Adding the same constant to every score changes nothing — it multiplies top and bottom by the same e^c. Real implementations exploit this by subtracting the largest score first, so e^z never overflows. Same underflow worry as session 16, same family of fix.

Work this out before reading on — calculator allowed

A model produces three scores:

z = [2, 1, 0]

What distribution does softmax turn them into? And which is bigger — the gap between the first two probabilities, or the gap between the last two?

e² = 7.389, e¹ = 2.718, e⁰ = 1.000. They sum to 11.107.

Divide each by that total: 0.665, 0.245, 0.090. (They sum to 1, as they must.)

Now the second question, which is the point of the exercise. The three scores are evenly spaced — 2, 1, 0, each one step apart. The probabilities are not: the first gap is 0.420 and the second is only 0.155. Equal steps in score, wildly unequal steps in probability.

Both gaps are the same ratio — 0.665/0.245 ≈ 0.245/0.090 ≈ 2.718, which is e¹ — but ratios near the top of a distribution are worth far more absolute probability than ratios near the bottom. This is the exaggeration, done by hand rather than asserted.

Loss: how wrong were we?

Softmax gave us a distribution. Suppose the correct word was river and we gave it 0.113. How bad is that, as a number?

The answer the whole field uses is cross-entropy loss, and for a single prediction it is remarkably plain:

loss  =  − log( probability the model gave the correct answer )

That is all of it. Every other word's probability is ignored — but only apparently, because raising the right word's probability necessarily lowers the others, softmax having made them sum to 1. Three cases from the table above:

if the correct word waswe saidloss
bank 0.8360.179 — nearly right, small penalty
river 0.1132.180
money 0.0512.980 — badly wrong, large penalty

A confident correct answer costs almost nothing; a confident wrong answer costs a lot; and if the model ever gave the correct word a probability of exactly 0, the loss would be infinite. Which is sensible, and is the same reason session 13 could not tolerate a zero probability.

You have met this loss before, under another name

Session 14 defined perplexity as the exponentiated average of exactly this quantity — the average of −log P(next word) across a test set, exponentiated.

So cross-entropy loss is not new machinery. It is the thing that was inside perplexity all along, used directly rather than dressed up as a branching factor. Minimising cross-entropy and minimising perplexity are the same act.

Gradient descent: nudge the weights downhill, repeatedly

We now have a number that says how wrong the model is, and the model is a pile of weights. The last question is how to change the weights to make that number smaller.

Think of the loss as a landscape. Every possible setting of the weights is a position, and the loss is the height at that position. We want to get to the bottom. We cannot see the landscape — it has hundreds of thousands of dimensions and we only ever stand at one point in it — but at any point we can work out the gradient: which direction is uphill, and how steeply.

So: work out which way is uphill, take a small step the other way, repeat.

w  ←  w  −  η · (gradient of the loss with respect to w)

η (eta) is the learning rate, and it is just how big a step to take. Too small and this takes forever; too large and you stride straight over the bottom and up the far side.

Those are real numbers, and they are small enough to check:

The gradient is negative throughout, so w − η·(negative) moves w to the right, towards 3. Notice it never quite arrives, and does not need to — the loss is 0.054 after five steps and heading to nothing.

Real training differs from that table in scale, not in kind. Instead of one weight there are millions, so the gradient is a list of millions of numbers, one per weight, all updated at once. Instead of one loss there is an average over a batch of examples. But the line w ← w − η·gradient is unchanged, and it is the line that trains every model in the rest of this course.

Backpropagation, in one sentence, and then we move on

Getting the gradient for a weight buried deep inside a network means differentiating a function composed of many others. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied systematically, working backwards from the loss, reusing each layer's result to get the one before it.

That is what you need to know today. It is derived properly in SLP3 §6.6 and it is worth reading eventually, but no session in this course asks you to do it by hand — PyTorch computes gradients for you, and it computes them by doing exactly that.

Question 1 of 2

Why does stacking layers without a non-linearity between them gain you nothing?

Correct answer: the stack collapses.

W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x, and W₂W₁ is simply another matrix. A hundred linear layers can compute exactly what one linear layer can compute — you have spent a hundred times the arithmetic to buy nothing. The activation function is what breaks the collapse, and it is the only reason depth is worth having.

⚠️ But note what this does not say. It is an argument about depth, and shallow models are perfectly respectable: Word2Vec has no hidden non-linearity at all and it is one of the most useful models in the field's history. Being shallow is what lets it train on a billion words.

Question 2 of 2

Next session trains a model and then hands you "the word vectors". Which part of the trained model is the word vectors?

Correct answer: the weight matrix of the first layer.

There is no extraction step and no afterwards. The embedding matrix E is 10000 × 300 — a row of 300 weights for each of 10,000 words — and it is fitted by the same gradient descent as every other weight in the model. When training stops, you throw away the rest of the network and keep that matrix. The word vectors were the model's weights all along.

The softmax outputs are wrong for a simple reason: they are a distribution over the vocabulary produced for one particular context, and they change with every input. A word vector has to be a fixed property of the word.

Assembling it — the sentence this hour was for

Here is a sentence from next session. An hour ago it was noise. Read it now:

Session 21, first ten minutes

"CBOW averages the context word vectors, multiplies by an output matrix, applies softmax, and does gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss."

Five nouns. Take them one at a time.

the phrasewhat it means, from today
the context word vectors Rows of the embedding matrix E — which is the weight matrix of a layer. The vectors are weights.
averages Pull out the rows for the words surrounding the target, add them, divide by how many. One 300-number vector comes out.
multiplies by an output matrix A layer: z = W'x. 300 numbers in, one score per vocabulary word out — 10,000 of them. Row w of W' is word w's vector on the way out.
applies softmax Turns those 10,000 arbitrary scores into 10,000 probabilities that are positive and sum to 1: a guess at which word sits in the middle.
cross-entropy loss −log of the probability it gave the word that was actually there. Small when the guess was good.
gradient descent w ← w − η·gradient, applied to every weight — including every number in E. Which is to say: including the word vectors themselves.

That last row is where the whole thing turns over. The word vectors are not an output of training, sitting somewhere at the end. They are parameters being trained, adjusted on every single example, pushed a little at a time until words that appear in similar contexts have ended up with similar vectors. Nobody ever told the model what any word means.

⚠️ One honest note about the hour you just spent

This was a compressed prerequisite and it left a great deal out: backpropagation is named and not derived; there is nothing here on optimisers, regularisation, dropout, batching, or how you actually choose a learning rate; and the softmax over 10,000 words we just described is ruinously expensive to compute, which is why session 21 spends half its time not computing it.

You now know enough to read session 21 without any step being magic. That is the whole and only claim.

The one thing to remember

The whole lesson in four sentences

A neuron is a weighted sum plus a bias pushed through one non-linear function, y = f(Σwᵢxᵢ + b) — the same shape as session 16's log-space Naïve Bayes score, except the weights are fitted rather than counted. Stack neurons and the sums become one matrix multiply, Wx + b; the non-linearity is what keeps a stack from collapsing into a single layer. Softmax turns any list of scores into a probability distribution by exponentiating and dividing by the total, exaggerating the gaps as it goes. And training is one line — measure how wrong you are with −log of the probability you gave the right answer, then repeatedly nudge every weight downhill by w ← w − η·gradient.
