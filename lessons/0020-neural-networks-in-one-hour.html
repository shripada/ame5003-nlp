Neural networks in one hour

Nothing in this session is usable on its own, which is deliberate. It supplies the machinery session 21 requires: five ideas, each selected because Word2Vec uses it.

Why this session introduces no new task

Sessions 17, 18, and 19 built vectors by counting and weighting: count the words, damp the counts with a logarithm, reweight with idf, and compare with a cosine. Every step could be carried out by hand, and every session ended with a usable method.

Session 21 builds word vectors differently: it learns them. Nothing is counted into the vector. The vector begins as random numbers and is adjusted repeatedly until it performs well on a prediction task. This is a different kind of system, and it requires machinery we do not yet have.

This session supplies that machinery. Nothing here is usable on its own: there is no method at the end, no dataset, and nothing to run. It exists so that no step in session 21 is unexplained.

The result this session is aiming at

Lab 7 measured sentiment classification on 2,000 real movie reviews and left two baselines on the table: 0.809 accuracy from raw counts, 0.823 from sublinear TF-IDF. Those are what counting and weighting get you.

Word2Vec is the next entry in that table. An hour of prerequisites is justified only if learned vectors can improve on 0.823, and that is a question we will be able to answer by measurement rather than assertion.

Where this has already appeared

Session 16's classifier, in log space, was:

cˆ=argmaxc∈Clog⁡P(c)+∑w∈dlog⁡P(w|c)

The object we are about to define, which underlies the neural models in the rest of the course, is:

score=b+∑iwixi

Compare their shapes. log⁡P(c) is a constant added regardless of the document, which is b. Each log⁡P(w|c) is a fixed number attached to a word, multiplied by the number of times that word occurs, which is wixi. Naïve Bayes in log space is a weighted sum of features. We built one in session 16 without calling it a neural network.

The essential difference

Naïve Bayes' weights are counted. A neuron's weights are fitted.

Session 16 obtained its weights by counting how often each word appeared in each class and taking a logarithm. No choice or adjustment was involved: count, divide, and the weights are fixed. A neuron's weights begin as arbitrary numbers and are adjusted until the model predicts well.

This is what makes possible things counting cannot do. Counting cannot detect that a feature is useless, whereas fitting can drive its weight to zero. Counting cannot detect that two features matter only in combination, whereas fitting a second layer can.

The neuron

A neuron is a weighted sum, plus a constant, passed through one function. That is the entire object:

y=f(∑iwixi+b)

x are the inputs, w the weights, b the bias, and f the activation function — the one non-linear step. The sum inside is often written z and called the pre-activation.

A worked example

Consider three features from a movie review, namely how many times it contains great, the, and awful, together with a neuron whose weights are already set. These weights were chosen to illustrate a point rather than trained; nothing on this page is fitted to any data, which is lab 8's subject. The arithmetic below is exact:

w=[1.4,0.0,−1.9]b=−1.0f=σ
review A:x=[2,5,0]z=1.4×2+0.0×5+(−1.9)×0−1.0=1.8y=σ(1.8)=0.858review B:x=[0,5,3]z=1.4×0+0.0×5+(−1.9)×3−1.0=−6.7y=σ(−6.7)=0.001

The output is a number between 0 and 1 and can be read as the probability that the review is positive. Review A receives 0.858 and review B 0.001.

The middle weight deserves attention. the carries a weight of essentially zero, so it contributes nothing to either result however often it appears, and a weight near zero is something fitting can arrive at on its own. Session 16 could not. Naïve Bayes counted the in both classes and carried two nearly equal log terms indefinitely, which is why session 4 was spent on stop-word lists. Fitted weights can make some preprocessing unnecessary, since the model can learn to ignore a word rather than being instructed to.

The sigmoid
σ(z)=11+e−z

It maps any real number into the interval (0,1). Large positive z gives a value near 1, large negative z gives a value near 0, and σ(0)=0.5 exactly. This is why its output can be read as a probability, and it is why we introduce it here: session 21's negative sampling scores a word pair as σ(u⋅v), which is this function applied to a dot product.The other activation you will meet is ReLU, f(z)=max⁡(0,z) — pass positives through, flatten negatives to zero. It is the default in everything from session 23 onward, because it is cheap and it does not squash gradients to nothing. We will introduce it properly when Unit III needs it.

Why the non-linearity is necessary

It is reasonable to ask why f is needed at all, rather than simply stacking weighted sums.

The reason is that a stack of linear layers is itself a linear layer. Feeding one into another:

layer 1:h=W1xlayer 2:y=W2h=W2(W1x)=(W2W1)x=Wx

W2W1 is simply another matrix. Three layers collapse in the same way, as do thirty. Without a non-linearity, depth provides nothing: a hundred-layer linear network computes exactly what one layer computes. The activation function is what prevents this collapse, and it is the reason depth is meaningful at all.

Word2Vec is deliberately shallow

This must be stated now, or session 21 will appear to contradict the paragraph above. Word2Vec has no hidden non-linearity. CBOW consists of averaging some vectors, one matrix multiply, and a softmax.

The collapse argument concerns depth, and Word2Vec is not deep. Being shallow is deliberate, and it is why the model can be trained on a billion words on modest hardware. Depth begins in Unit III.

A sigmoid does appear in session 21, in negative sampling. It is not a hidden layer; it converts a single dot product into a single probability.

A layer is a matrix multiply

Put several neurons side by side, all reading the same inputs. Each has its own row of weights. Rather than write out one sum per neuron, stack the rows into a matrix and write it once:

y=f(Wx+b)

Nothing has changed. Row i of W multiplied by x is exactly neuron i's weighted sum, and there are as many rows as neurons. This is notation rather than a new idea, introduced here so that Wx+b is immediately readable in session 21.

The point session 21 depends on

The word vectors are a weight matrix.

E above is a layer's weights, fitted by the same procedure as any other weights. When session 21 finishes training and refers to the word vectors, it means the rows of E. There is no separate step in which vectors are extracted from the model; the vectors are part of the model.

This is worth establishing now rather than in session 21, where it is easy to miss.

Softmax

A neuron's output is a single number. Session 21 needs something else: a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary, because it is predicting which word sits in the middle of a context. Ten thousand words in, ten thousand probabilities out.

We have worked with distributions over words since session 11, but always built from counts. Softmax constructs one from arbitrary scores:

softmax(z)i=ezi∑jezj

Every score is exponentiated and then divided by the total. Applied to three scores:

wordscore zezprobability
bank 3.020.0860.836
river 1.02.718 0.113
money 0.21.221 0.051
24.0251.000

Two properties, and they are the two we need. Everything comes out positive, because ez is positive whatever z was — including for negative scores. And everything sums to 1, because we divided by the total. That is the definition of a distribution, so the output can be handed straight to anything that expects probabilities.

Note the effect of the exponential. bank exceeded river by 2.0 on the scores, a modest margin. In probability it exceeds it by a factor of 7.4. This is not specific to these numbers: a gap of 2 always becomes a ratio of e2=7.39, and a gap of 5 becomes a ratio of 148. Softmax exaggerates, turning small differences in score into large differences in probability, which is what the name refers to.Adding the same constant to every score changes nothing — it multiplies top and bottom by the same ec. Real implementations exploit this by subtracting the largest score first, so ez never overflows. Same underflow worry as session 16, same family of fix.

Exercise (calculator allowed)

A model produces three scores:

z=[2,1,0]

What distribution does softmax turn them into? And which is bigger — the gap between the first two probabilities, or the gap between the last two?

e2=7.389, e1=2.718, e0=1.000. They sum to 11.107.

Divide each by that total: 0.665, 0.245, 0.090. (They sum to 1, as they must.)

The second question is the point of the exercise. The three scores are evenly spaced at 2, 1, and 0, each one step apart. The probabilities are not: the first gap is 0.420 and the second only 0.155. Equal steps in score produce very unequal steps in probability.

Both gaps represent the same ratio, since 0.665/0.245≈0.245/0.090≈2.718, which is e1. A given ratio near the top of a distribution corresponds to far more absolute probability than the same ratio near the bottom. This is the exaggeration described above, computed rather than asserted.

The loss function

Softmax has given us a distribution. Suppose the correct word was river and the model assigned it 0.113. We need a number expressing how poor that is.

The standard measure is cross-entropy loss, which for a single prediction is:

loss=−log⁡(probability the model gave the correct answer)

That is the whole definition. Every other word's probability is ignored, though only apparently: raising the correct word's probability necessarily lowers the others, since softmax constrains them to sum to 1. Three cases from the table above:

if the correct word waswe saidloss
bank 0.8360.179 — nearly right, small penalty
river 0.1132.180
money 0.0512.980 — badly wrong, large penalty

A confident correct answer costs almost nothing and a confident wrong answer costs a great deal. If the model ever assigned the correct word a probability of exactly 0, the loss would be infinite, which is appropriate and is the same reason session 13 could not tolerate a zero probability.

This loss under another name

Session 14 defined perplexity as the exponentiated average of exactly this quantity — the average of −log⁡P(next word) across a test set, exponentiated.

Cross-entropy loss is therefore not new machinery. It is the quantity that was inside perplexity all along, used directly rather than expressed as a branching factor. Minimising cross-entropy and minimising perplexity are the same operation.

Gradient descent

We now have a number expressing how wrong the model is, and the model is a collection of weights. The remaining question is how to change those weights so that the number becomes smaller.

Consider the loss as a surface. Every possible setting of the weights is a position, and the loss is the height at that position. We want to reach the lowest point. The surface cannot be observed directly, since it has hundreds of thousands of dimensions and we occupy only one point in it, but at any point we can compute the gradient: the direction of steepest increase, and its steepness.

The procedure is therefore to compute which direction is uphill, take a small step in the opposite direction, and repeat.

w←w−η⋅(gradient of the loss with respect to w)

η (eta) is the learning rate, which sets the size of each step. Too small and convergence is very slow; too large and each step overshoots the minimum and lands on the far side.

Real training differs from this table in scale rather than in kind. Instead of one weight there are millions, so the gradient is a list of millions of numbers, one per weight, all updated together, and instead of one loss there is an average over a batch of examples. The line w←w−η⋅gradient is unchanged, and it is what trains every model in the remainder of the course.

Backpropagation

Getting the gradient for a weight buried deep inside a network means differentiating a function composed of many others. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied systematically, working backwards from the loss, reusing each layer's result to get the one before it.

That is sufficient for our purposes. It is derived properly in SLP3 §6.6 and is worth reading eventually, but no session in this course requires it to be carried out by hand. PyTorch computes gradients automatically, by exactly this procedure.

Question 1 of 2

Why does stacking layers without a non-linearity between them gain you nothing?

Correct answer: the stack collapses.

W2(W1x)=(W2W1)x, and W2W1 is simply another matrix. A hundred linear layers can compute exactly what one linear layer can compute — you have spent a hundred times the arithmetic to buy nothing. The activation function is what breaks the collapse, and it is the only reason depth is worth having.

⚠️ But note what this does not say. It is an argument about depth, and shallow models are perfectly respectable: Word2Vec has no hidden non-linearity at all and it is one of the most useful models in the field's history. Being shallow is what lets it train on a billion words.

Question 2 of 2

Next session trains a model and then hands you "the word vectors". Which part of the trained model is the word vectors?

Correct answer: the weight matrix of the first layer.

There is no extraction step and no afterwards. The embedding matrix E is 10000×300 — a row of 300 weights for each of 10,000 words — and it is fitted by the same gradient descent as every other weight in the model. When training stops, you throw away the rest of the network and keep that matrix. The word vectors were the model's weights all along.

The softmax outputs are wrong for a simple reason: they are a distribution over the vocabulary produced for one particular context, and they change with every input. A word vector has to be a fixed property of the word.

Putting the pieces together

The following sentence is from session 21. It should now be readable in full:

Session 21, first ten minutes

"CBOW averages the context word vectors, multiplies by an output matrix, applies softmax, and does gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss."

It contains five terms, taken in turn below.

the phrasewhat it means, from today
the context word vectors Rows of the embedding matrix E — which is the weight matrix of a layer. The vectors are weights.
averages Pull out the rows for the words surrounding the target, add them, divide by how many. One 300-number vector comes out.
multiplies by an output matrix A layer: z=W′x. 300 numbers in, one score per vocabulary word out — 10,000 of them. Row w of W′ is word w's vector on the way out.
applies softmax Turns those 10,000 arbitrary scores into 10,000 probabilities that are positive and sum to 1: a guess at which word sits in the middle.
cross-entropy loss −log⁡ of the probability it gave the word that was actually there. Small when the guess was good.
gradient descent w←w−η⋅gradient, applied to every weight — including every number in E. Which is to say: including the word vectors themselves.

The last row is the essential point. The word vectors are not an output produced at the end of training. They are parameters being trained, adjusted on every example, moved slightly at a time until words appearing in similar contexts have similar vectors. No information about meaning was supplied to the model.

What this session has and has not covered

This was a compressed prerequisite and a great deal was omitted. Backpropagation is named rather than derived; there is nothing here on optimisers, regularisation, dropout, batching, or how a learning rate is chosen in practice; and the softmax over 10,000 words described above is extremely expensive to compute, which is why session 21 spends much of its time avoiding it.

What this session establishes is enough to follow session 21 with no unexplained step. That is its only claim.

Summary

The main points

A neuron is a weighted sum plus a bias passed through one non-linear function, y=f(∑iwixi+b). This has the same form as session 16's log-space Naïve Bayes score, except that the weights are fitted rather than counted. Stacking neurons turns the sums into a single matrix multiply, Wx+b, and the non-linearity is what prevents a stack from collapsing into one layer. Softmax converts any list of scores into a probability distribution by exponentiating and dividing by the total, exaggerating the differences in the process. Training is a single line: measure the error as −log⁡ of the probability assigned to the correct answer, then repeatedly adjust every weight by w←w−η⋅gradient.
