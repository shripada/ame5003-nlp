Training an RNN: backprop through time, and the vanishing gradient

Last session ended on a promise the architecture makes and a question nobody answered. This session answers it, and the answer is no — not by default. That "no" is the entire reason the next session exists.

Where session 23 left off

Session 23 closed on a claim it deliberately did not defend, restated here exactly as it was left:

Session 23's closing question

"The architecture allows a decision to depend on information from hundreds of words in the past — whether that actually happens once you try to train one is next session's entire hour."

This session answers it. The answer is no, not by default. What follows is the reason, and the reason is not a defect in any implementation: it follows from the architecture session 23 described.

What training an RNN means

U, W, and V are trained as session 20's neuron was trained: choose a loss, compute how much each weight contributed to it, adjust every weight against its gradient, and repeat. None of that is new. What is new is the distance the error signal must travel.

In sequence classification there is a single loss, computed at the end after the last word. To improve, the network must determine how much the weights used at step 1 contributed to an error made at step n. Session 23's unrolled figure supplies the answer: the unrolled network is an ordinary feedforward network, so we differentiate backward along every arrow in that figure.

Backpropagation through time

SLP3 describes it as two passes. Forward: run the sequence, computing h_t and y_t, accumulating the loss and saving every hidden state. Backward: walk the sequence in reverse, computing the gradient at each step and passing an error term back to the step before it. That is all "backpropagation through time" means — ordinary backpropagation, applied to the unrolled picture.

We do not derive it here. The mechanism matters for one consequence, which is the subject of the rest of the session: to reach step 1, the gradient must pass through every step in between.

Why the effective memory is short

SLP3 gives two reasons. They are different problems with different solutions, and the first is more important than it may appear.

Reason 1: the hidden state has two roles

At every step, h_t and the weights producing it are required to do two things at once: supply what the current decision needs and carry forward what a future decision will need. There is one vector for both purposes and no machinery separating them. Whatever the current step finds useful will overwrite what a later step depends on, not because of any numerical problem but because there is only one place to store both.

Reason 2: the gradient is multiplied once per step

h_t feeds into h_{t+1}, which feeds into h_{t+2}, and so on. During the backward pass the hidden layers are therefore subject to repeated multiplications, as many as the sequence is long. Multiplying any quantity by the same factor a hundred times sends it to one of two limits, zero or infinity. SLP3's name for the first outcome is the vanishing gradients problem.

An example sentence

The flights the airline was canceling were full.

This is SLP3's own example and is chosen carefully. Predicting was after airline is easy, since the singular noun is one word away and local context is what a hidden state handles well. Predicting were is difficult, for two separate reasons corresponding to the two above:

  • The word that determines it, the plural flights, sits many steps back, and everything between has been writing into the same hidden state.
  • The singular airline sits much closer and favours the wrong answer. The nearby incorrect cue is stronger than the distant correct one.

That distance is what reason 2's gradient must survive and what reason 1's overloaded hidden state must preserve. A single sentence exhibits both failures.

Returning to session 23's worked example

No new example is required to see reason 2 arithmetically, since session 23's hand-computed example already contains it. That example was:

h_t = U·h_{t-1} + W·x_t          with U = 0.5, W = 1, h₀ = 0

Differentiating one step gives ∂h_t/∂h_{t-1} = U. Every step contributes exactly one factor of U, so reaching back from step n to step 1 multiplies n−1 of them together:

∂h_n/∂h₁  =  U^(n−1)
The value of U used in session 23

U = 0.5, which is already below 1. The example computed forward by hand in session 23, with values chosen only for ease of arithmetic, was a vanishing case throughout. This is session 23's arithmetic, read backwards.

Twenty words back, a gradient of 0.0000019 is not a small learning signal but effectively none: the weight that would have captured the long-range dependency is updated by an amount that rounds to zero. A fifty-word sentence is not a long document; it is a single paragraph.

Exploding gradients

Changing one value, U = 1.5 instead of 0.5, makes the same recurrence run in the opposite direction: 38 after ten steps and four hundred million after fifty. The same architecture and the same formula produce the opposite failure. Below 1 the gradient vanishes; above 1 it grows without bound. Only a factor of exactly 1 is stable, and nothing in training keeps it there.

Exercise

Same toy, U = 0.5. What is ∂h₅/∂h₁ — the gradient reaching back four steps?

Then answer a second question: what would U have to be for this quantity to grow as you reach further back instead of shrinking?

∂h₅/∂h₁ = U⁴ = 0.5⁴ = 0.0625. Four steps back, the signal is already down to six per cent.

For the quantity to grow, |U| > 1. That is the exploding case in the table's right-hand column. Note that nothing in the training procedure keeps U near 1; it is a learned weight like any other. Vanishing is the more common outcome, not the only one.

What the example omits

The example has no activation function, which is why its arithmetic remained inspectable. A real RNN squashes every step with tanh, which adds one further factor to every step of the backward pass: tanh′(z) = 1 − tanh²(z), which is at most 1 and usually well below it. That factor makes vanishing worse and never better. It cannot recover a shrinking gradient, and it is not the cause of the exploding case.

Question 1 of 2

Why does the gradient reaching an early timestep shrink toward zero in a long sequence?

Correct answer: repeated multiplication by the same U, once per step.

The first option is a real contributing factor and a good instinct — tanh′ is at most 1, so it makes vanishing worse — but it is not the mechanism, and it cannot explain the exploding case at all. The second is false in an instructive way: shared weights mean the parameter count does not grow with sequence length, which was session 23's whole selling point. The advantage and the defect are the same design decision. The fourth is true of sequence classification but is not why the gradient shrinks — a single final loss would backpropagate perfectly well if it were not multiplied down on the way.

The solution, named

SLP3's framing of the answer is not about gradients at all. It is about context management: architectures designed to "explicitly manage the task of maintaining relevant context over time, by enabling the network to learn to forget information that is no longer needed and to remember information required for decisions still to come."

Read against reason 1, this corresponds exactly. If the problem is one vector serving two purposes, the solution is to give the two purposes separate machinery: a place to hold context that is not where the current decision is computed, together with learned control over what enters and leaves it. That is the LSTM, and it is the subject of session 25.

The gradient explanation

The same design also addresses reason 2, as a consequence rather than a goal. The LSTM's cell state gives the gradient a path along which information is added from step to step rather than multiplied through a weight matrix, and a sum does not decay exponentially as a repeated product does. This is the standard explanation but not SLP3's framing, and should not be attributed to the book. Two reasons, two solutions, one architecture.

Question 2 of 2

In SLP3's own framing, what does an LSTM change about a plain RNN?

Correct answer: it gives the two jobs separate machinery, with learned control over what is remembered and forgotten.

That is reason 1's fix, and it is why the LSTM looks the way it does. The gradient benefit is real but secondary — if you remember the LSTM as "a trick to stop gradients vanishing," its actual design will look arbitrary next session. The third option describes a bidirectional RNN, which is a different idea and does not solve this problem; the fourth is a workaround people really do use, and it works by giving up the long-range dependency rather than by fixing anything.

The same pattern has appeared throughout this course: session 18's idf, session 19's cosine, session 21's analogy, and session 22's comparison of GloVe with Word2Vec. In each case the claim is checked against the arithmetic and what survives is kept.

The main points

Training an RNN means backpropagating one loss, computed at the end of the sequence, back through every earlier step. This is ordinary backpropagation applied to the unrolled network, which is all that "backpropagation through time" denotes. The process fails in two ways at once. The hidden state is required to serve the current decision and carry information for later ones, with no machinery separating the two; and the gradient is multiplied by the same matrix U once per step, so that ∂h_n/∂h₁ = U^(n−1) collapses to nothing when |U| < 1 and grows without bound when |U| > 1. Session 23's own example, with U = 0.5, was already a vanishing case: twenty steps back the gradient is 0.0000019, which is effectively no learning signal at all. The solution is to stop requiring one vector to serve both purposes, by adding an explicit context layer with learned control. That is the LSTM, and it is the subject of session 25.
