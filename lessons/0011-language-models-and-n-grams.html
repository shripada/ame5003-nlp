Language models and n-grams

A model that guesses the next word. It is the same idea ChatGPT runs on — and the simplest version of it is just looking at the last word or two.

From finding text to predicting text

Unit I was concerned with finding text. We cleaned it, tagged it, indexed it, and ranked it. Every technique assumed the text already existed and that the task was to locate the relevant part of it.

Unit II asks a different question: can a machine predict text? Given the words so far, what word comes next?

I want to eat Indian ____

Most readers supply food, or perhaps curry, and almost certainly not helicopter. No lookup is involved; the prediction comes from knowledge of how English sentences typically proceed. A language model is a system that does the same thing.

What a language model is

The definition is brief.

Definition

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence of words. Equivalently, it predicts the probability of the next word given the words that came before.

These appear to be two different tasks but are in fact one. If we can score how likely each next word is, we can score a whole sentence by proceeding through it word by word; and if we can score a whole sentence, we can compare sentences:

sentenceprobability
I want to eat Indian foodhigh
I want to eat Indian helicoptervery low
eat Indian I food want tonear zero

Note what the model does not do. It makes no judgement about whether the sentence is true, polite, or sensible. It reports only how likely those words are to appear in that order in the kind of text it was trained on.

Applications

Prediction proves useful in a wide range of applications:

  • Autocomplete and phone keyboards. The three suggested words above a phone keyboard are a language model's three highest-probability predictions.
  • Speech recognition. The microphone receives something between "recognise speech" and "wreck a nice beach". The acoustic signals are nearly identical, and the language model selects the far more probable sequence.
  • Spelling and grammar correction. "their are two" receives a low probability and "there are two" a high one.
  • Machine translation. Among several grammatical translations, the model selects the one that reads most like natural language.
  • Text generation. Predict a word, append it, and predict again.

The last of these requires further discussion, since it is where the course is heading.

The relation to large language models

It is worth stating this now rather than at the end of the unit. A large language model is a language model, under exactly the definition given above: it takes the words so far and produces a probability for every possible next word, then selects one, appends it, and repeats. This loop is how a page of text is produced one word at a time.

The question being answered is therefore identical. What differs is how the probabilities are computed:

Beginning with n-grams is not a matter of studying an obsolete method before the real one; it is studying the same problem in the one form whose every component can be inspected. Every idea introduced later — context, prediction, the difficulty posed by rare words, and the measurement of model quality — appears first here, in a version small enough to verify by hand.

Why the full context cannot be used

To predict the next word properly, we would want to use everything preceding it. Stated exactly, the quantity we want is:

P(food | I want to eat Indian)

This reads as "the probability of food given I want to eat Indian". It is manageable for five words, but a model must handle any history at all, and most sequences of eight or more words have never been written down anywhere. A model that learns from examples cannot learn from examples it does not have, and the longer the history required, the more certain it is that the history has never been observed.

We therefore make a trade. Instead of the whole history, we use only the last few words:

P(food | I want to eat Indian)   ← everything (impossible)
P(food | Indian)                 ← just the last word (easy)

Short histories recur constantly; Indian food appears frequently in any corpus. This trade, discarding the distant history and retaining a small window, is the idea underlying n-grams.

What an n-gram is

Definition

An n-gram is a run of n words next to each other in the text.

They are obtained by sliding a window of width n along the sentence one step at a time. For the sentence I want to eat Indian food:

nnamethe n-gramshow many
1unigram I · want · to · eat · Indian · food6
2bigram I want · want to · to eat · eat Indian · Indian food5
3trigram I want to · want to eat · to eat Indian · eat Indian food4

A sentence of T words yields T − n + 1 n-grams. The windows overlap, so every word except those at the ends belongs to several n-grams. This overlap is essential, since it is how the model observes each word in more than one context.

An n-gram language model predicts the next word using the previous n − 1 words as its context:

modelcontext it looks atquestion it asks
unigramnothing at allhow common is this word?
bigramthe previous 1 wordwhat usually follows Indian?
trigramthe previous 2 wordswhat usually follows eat Indian?

A unigram model has no context and therefore predicts common words, producing output such as "the of and to a". A bigram model at least preserves adjacent pairs, and a trigram model produces output noticeably closer to natural language. More context yields better predictions.

Why n cannot be large

The reason is that the number of possible n-grams grows extremely rapidly. With a vocabulary of 50,000 words:

modelpossible sequences
unigram50,000
bigram2,500,000,000
trigram125,000,000,000,000
4-gram6,250,000,000,000,000,000

Each increase in n multiplies the count by the size of the whole vocabulary. There is not enough text in existence to have observed most trigrams even once, let alone often enough to estimate a probability from. The counts therefore become sparser as n grows.

This is the central tension in n-gram models: more context gives better predictions, but more context leaves less data from which to estimate it. In practice, trigram to 5-gram models are typical. Later in this unit, neural models avoid the tension entirely, which is the principal reason they displaced n-grams.

Exercise

A bigram model is asked to continue the students opened their, predicting from their alone. What information has been discarded, and what kind of error does this make likely?

It has discarded the students opened, which is the part indicating that the answer is probably books, laptops, or notes. The model sees only their, which can be followed by almost any noun in English: hands, car, parents.

The output will therefore be grammatical but unrelated to the topic. This is the characteristic n-gram failure: locally well-formed and globally incoherent. The word determining the answer, opened, lay just outside the window.

Question 1 of 2

What does a language model do?

Correct answer: it gives a probability to a word sequence, so it can predict the next word.

Scoring a sequence and predicting the next word are the same capability viewed from two directions. Truth is not involved: a language model will assign a high probability to a confidently stated false sentence, because "likely" here means "resembles real language", not "is correct".

Question 2 of 2

Why do n-gram models not simply use a large n, such as 10?

Correct answer: longer sequences almost never repeat, so there is too little data.

The number of possible n-grams is the vocabulary size raised to the power n, which grows extremely quickly. Most 10-word sequences have never been written down even once, and a model that estimates from counts cannot estimate from a sequence it has never observed.

Summary

The main points

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence of words, which is equivalent to predicting the next word. This single capability underlies autocomplete, speech recognition, machine translation, and large language models alike. An n-gram model implements it in the simplest possible way, discarding the distant history and using only the previous n − 1 words, which trades accuracy for having sufficient data from which to estimate.

Session 12 makes this concrete by addressing where the probabilities come from. The answer is counting: count how often Indian food appears, compare that with how often Indian appears, and the result is a probability. This introduces the chain rule and the assumption that permits discarding the distant history.
