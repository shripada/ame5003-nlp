N-gram probabilities and the Markov assumption

Where the numbers come from. The exact answer is easy to write down and impossible to use — so we deliberately use the wrong one.

Where session 11 left off

Session 11 defined a language model as a system that assigns a probability to a sequence of words, and stated that an n-gram model uses only the last word or two. It did not say where the numbers themselves come from.

They come from counting. This session covers which quantities to count and one assumption we make deliberately, despite knowing it to be false.

A small corpus

Everything that follows is computed from these four sentences:

<s> I want to eat Indian food </s>
<s> I want to eat Chinese food </s>
<s> I want Indian food </s>
<s> she wants to eat Indian food </s>

The sentence boundary symbols

<s> and </s> mark the start and end of a sentence. They are counted as tokens, like any other word, and each serves a specific purpose:

  • <s> gives the first word something to condition on. Without it we cannot ask how likely I is at the start of a sentence, since there would be no preceding word.
  • </s> is how the model determines when to stop. This is the less obvious and more important of the two. A model generating text selects one word after another, and something must terminate the process. </s> is a token the model can predict, and predicting it means the sentence has ended.

The chain rule

We want the probability of a whole sentence. The rule for decomposing a joint probability into a chain of conditional probabilities is the chain rule of probability. Applied to a sentence it reads left to right: the probability of the sentence is the probability of the first word, multiplied by the probability of the second given the first, multiplied by the probability of the third given the first two, and so on.

P(I want to eat Indian food)=P(I|<s>)×P(want|I)×P(to|I want)×P(eat|I want to)×P(Indian|I want to eat)×P(food|I want to eat Indian)×P(</s>|I want to eat Indian food)

This decomposition is exact, involving no approximation. Any one of its terms can be estimated by counting: the number of times the full sequence appears, divided by the number of times the history appears.

P(food|I want to eat Indian)=count(I want to eat Indian food)count(I want to eat Indian)

In our corpus this evaluates to 1/1=1.0, which appears to be a strong result and is in fact the source of the difficulty.

Why the exact computation is impractical

That estimate rests on a denominator of one, and a single observation is not sufficient evidence for a probability estimate. The situation is worse for a history the corpus has never contained, such as we want to eat Indian, where the denominator is zero and the probability is not merely small but undefined.

This is the limitation identified in session 11, stated precisely. Long histories almost never recur, so the required counts are either absent or too small to be reliable. The chain rule is exactly correct and unusable in practice.

The Markov assumption

Since we cannot condition on the whole history, we approximate it by a short one. This approximation is the Markov assumption.

Definition

The probability of the next word depends only on the last few words, not on the entire history.

For a bigram model, "last few" means one:

P(food|I want to eat Indian)≈P(food|Indian)

It should be clear that this assumption is false. Language contains long-range dependencies; the subject at the beginning of a sentence governs the verb at the end. We are not claiming otherwise, but trading correctness for something we can measure. A short history is incorrect but countable, and a long history is correct but uncountable.

Substituting the assumption into the chain rule reduces every term to a single previous word:

P(I want to eat Indian food)≈P(I|<s>)×P(want|I)×P(to|want)×P(eat|to)×P(Indian|eat)×P(food|Indian)×P(</s>|food)

Estimating a bigram probability by counting

Each of these terms is now a count of a pair divided by a count of a single word:

P(w2|w1)=count(w1w2)count(w1)

This is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE). The term denotes the estimate that best fits the observed data, which for counting problems is simply the observed proportion. Nothing is adjusted or assumed: the corpus shows eat followed by Indian in 2 of 3 cases, so the estimate is 2/3.

The counts from our four sentences are given below. Note that count(eat)=3 and count(<s>)=4.

bigramcount(w1w2)count(w1)P(w2|w1)
<s> I 343/4 = 0.75
<s> she 141/4 = 0.25
want to 232/3 ≈ 0.67
want Indian 131/3 ≈ 0.33
eat Indian 232/3 ≈ 0.67
eat Chinese 131/3 ≈ 0.33
I want 333/3 = 1.0
to eat 333/3 = 1.0
Indian food 333/3 = 1.0
food </s> 444/4 = 1.0

Every bigram used in the calculations below appears in this table, so each can be verified by hand.

Consider the pairs sharing a first word. After eat, the model assigns Indian twice the probability of Chinese, and the two sum to 1, since they are the only words that ever follow eat in this corpus. The same holds for want. This is the model behaving as intended: a distribution over what comes next, estimated entirely from counts.

Interpreting a probability of 1.0

Several rows above are exactly 1.0. This is an artefact of a very small corpus rather than a claim about English. P(food|Indian)=1.0 holds only because these four sentences never place any other word after Indian.

In a real corpus, Indian would be followed by hundreds of different words and the largest probability might be a few per cent. The values here are small so that the arithmetic remains checkable by hand. It is the method that generalises, not these particular numbers.

Scoring a whole sentence

We multiply the bigram probabilities together:

P(I want to eat Indian food)≈34×33×23×33×23×33×44=13≈0.333

The model can now be used for its intended purpose, which is comparing sentences:

sentencebigram probability
I want to eat Indian food1/3 ≈ 0.333
I want to eat Chinese food1/6 ≈ 0.167
she wants to eat Indian food1/6 ≈ 0.167

The first sentence is rated twice as likely as the others, which is correct for this corpus: Indian follows eat more often than Chinese does, and sentences begin with I more often than with she. The model has no representation of what any of these words mean, and it ranks them correctly from counts alone.

What the Markov assumption changes

A modern large language model factorises a sentence left to right with the same chain rule — one word at a time, each conditioned on what came before. That part never went away.

What it discards is the Markov assumption. It conditions on thousands of previous words rather than one, because a neural network can process a long history without having counted it. The Markov assumption is therefore not a permanent feature of language modelling but the compromise required when counting is the only available tool. Much of the progress in this field has consisted of removing it.

Exercise

Using the table above, compute the bigram probability of I want Indian food. The required bigrams are <s> I, I want, want Indian, Indian food, and food </s>, the last of which is 4/4.

34×33×13×33×44=14=0.25

This is lower than I want to eat Indian food at 0.333, because want is followed by Indian in only 1 of 3 cases but by to in 2 of 3.

Note that this sentence is shorter and still scores lower. That occurs here only because so many probabilities in this small corpus are exactly 1.0, which costs the longer sentence nothing. In real data, every additional word introduces a further probability well below 1, so longer sentences score lower almost automatically. Raw probability is therefore a poor basis for comparing sentences of different lengths, a problem addressed in session 14.

Where the method fails

Consider one further sentence, which is perfectly ordinary English:

P(I want to eat Indian pizza)≈34×33×23×33×23×P(pizza|Indian)×⋯

The corpus never places pizza after Indian, so count(Indian pizza)=0 and the estimate is P(pizza|Indian)=0/3=0, which sends the whole product to zero:

P(I want to eat Indian pizza)=0

The result is not a small probability but exactly zero. A single unseen pair anywhere in the sentence reduces the entire product to zero, however ordinary the remaining six words are. The model is asserting that this sentence is impossible, when in fact four sentences of training text simply did not happen to contain it.

Real corpora exhibit this problem severely. Most bigrams that could occur never do, so most test sentences contain at least one unseen pair and score exactly zero. A model that assigns zero probability to most valid sentences is not usable.

Question 1 of 2

Why do we make the Markov assumption when it is clearly false?

Correct answer: long histories are too rare to count, and short ones are not.

The chain rule is exact but requires counts of long word sequences, which almost never recur; the denominator is often 1, or 0 and therefore undefined. Reducing the history to one or two words discards real information in exchange for counts large enough to be reliable. This is a trade made deliberately.

Question 2 of 2

In the corpus, eat occurs 3 times and eat Indian twice. What is P(Indian | eat)?

Correct answer: 2/3, because you divide the pair count by the word count.

The maximum likelihood estimate is count(w₁ w₂) / count(w₁). Of the 3 occurrences of eat, 2 were followed by Indian. The denominator is always the count of the history word alone, never the corpus size.

Summary

The main points

The chain rule gives the exact probability of a sentence but requires counts of long word sequences that almost never recur. We therefore adopt the Markov assumption, which we know to be false, and condition on only the last word or two, which can be counted. Each probability is then a single division, the count of the pair over the count of the first word, and multiplying these together scores a whole sentence.

The method assigns exactly zero to far too many acceptable sentences. Every unseen pair eliminates a sentence entirely, and unseen pairs are common in real data. Session 13 is devoted to this problem: how to move probability mass from what was observed to what was not.
