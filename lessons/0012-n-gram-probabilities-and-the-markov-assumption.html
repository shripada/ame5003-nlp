N-gram probabilities and the Markov assumption

Where the numbers come from. The exact answer is easy to write down and impossible to use — so we deliberately use the wrong one.

The question we left open

Last session defined a language model: something that gives a probability to a sequence of words. We said an n-gram model looks only at the last word or two. What we never said is where the actual numbers come from.

They come from counting. That is genuinely all. This hour is about which things to count, and about one assumption we make on purpose even though it is false.

A corpus small enough to check by hand

Everything below is computed from these four sentences:

<s> I want to eat Indian food </s>
<s> I want to eat Chinese food </s>
<s> I want Indian food </s>
<s> she wants to eat Indian food </s>

The two extra symbols

<s> and </s> mark the start and end of a sentence. They are not decoration — they are counted as tokens, like any word, and each one earns its place:

  • <s> gives the first word something to condition on. Without it you cannot ask "how likely is I at the start?", because there is no previous word to look at.
  • </s> is how the model knows when to stop. This one is less obvious and more important. A model that generates text picks a word, then another, then another — something has to make it end. </s> is a word it can predict, and predicting it means "the sentence is over".

The exact answer: the chain rule

We want the probability of a whole sentence. The rule for breaking a joint probability into a chain of conditional ones is standard probability — the chain rule. For a sentence it reads left to right: the probability of the sentence is the probability of the first word, times the probability of the second given the first, times the third given the first two, and so on.

P(I want to eat Indian food)
  = P(I | <s>)
  × P(want | I)
  × P(to | I want)
  × P(eat | I want to)
  × P(Indian | I want to eat)
  × P(food | I want to eat Indian)
  × P(</s> | I want to eat Indian food)

This is exact. No approximation anywhere. And to estimate any one of those terms you just count — the number of times the full sequence appears, divided by the number of times the history appears:

P(food | I want to eat Indian)  =  count(I want to eat Indian food)
                                   ─────────────────────────────────
                                   count(I want to eat Indian)

In our corpus that is 1 / 1 = 1.0. Which looks like a great result and is actually the whole problem.

Why the exact answer is useless

That estimate rests on a denominator of one. One observation is not evidence; it is a coincidence. And it gets worse: ask about a history the corpus has never seen — we want to eat Indian — and the denominator is zero. The probability is not small, it is undefined.

This is the wall from last session, in its exact form. Long histories almost never repeat, so the counts you need are either absent or too thin to trust. The chain rule is perfectly correct and completely unusable.

The fix: assume the far past does not matter

Since we cannot condition on the whole history, we approximate it with a short one. That step has a name:

The Markov assumption

The probability of the next word depends only on the last few words, not on the entire history.

For a bigram model, "last few" means one:

P(food | I want to eat Indian)  ≈  P(food | Indian)

Be clear about what just happened: this assumption is false. Language obviously has long-range dependencies — the subject at the start of a sentence controls the verb at the end. We are not claiming otherwise. We are trading correctness for something we can actually measure. A short history is wrong but countable; a long history is right but uncountable. Countable wins.

Put the assumption into the chain rule and every term shrinks to one previous word:

P(I want to eat Indian food)
  ≈  P(I | <s>) × P(want | I) × P(to | want) × P(eat | to)
     × P(Indian | eat) × P(food | Indian) × P(</s> | food)

Estimating a bigram probability by counting

Each of those terms is now a count of a pair divided by a count of a single word:

P(w₂ | w₁)  =  count(w₁ w₂)  /  count(w₁)

This is called the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE). The name sounds heavier than the idea: it means the estimate that best fits the data we saw, which for counting problems is simply the observed proportion. Nothing is being adjusted or assumed — the corpus said eat was followed by Indian in 2 of 3 cases, so the estimate is 2/3.

Here are the counts from our four sentences. Note count(eat) = 3 and count(<s>) = 4:

bigramcount(w₁ w₂)count(w₁)P(w₂ | w₁)
<s> I 343/4 = 0.75
<s> she 141/4 = 0.25
want to 232/3 ≈ 0.67
want Indian 131/3 ≈ 0.33
eat Indian 232/3 ≈ 0.67
eat Chinese 131/3 ≈ 0.33
I want 333/3 = 1.0
to eat 333/3 = 1.0
Indian food 333/3 = 1.0
food </s> 444/4 = 1.0

Every bigram used in the calculations below is in that table, so you can follow each one through by hand.

Look at the pairs that share a first word. After eat, the model gives Indian twice the probability of Chinese — and those two add to 1, because they are the only things that ever followed eat. Same for want. That is the model working: a distribution over what comes next, learned purely from counts.

Do not read 1.0 as certainty

Several rows above are exactly 1.0, and that is an artefact of a toy corpus, not a claim about English. P(food | Indian) = 1.0 only because these four sentences never put anything else after Indian.

In a real corpus, Indian would be followed by hundreds of different words and the largest probability might be a few percent. The numbers here are tiny so the arithmetic stays checkable by hand. Trust the method, not these particular values.

Scoring a whole sentence

Multiply the bigram probabilities straight through:

P(I want to eat Indian food)
  ≈ 3/4 × 3/3 × 2/3 × 3/3 × 2/3 × 3/3 × 4/4
  = 1/3  ≈ 0.333

And now the model can be used for its actual job — comparing sentences:

sentencebigram probability
I want to eat Indian food1/3 ≈ 0.333
I want to eat Chinese food1/6 ≈ 0.167
she wants to eat Indian food1/6 ≈ 0.167

The first is rated twice as likely as the others, and it should be: the corpus prefers Indian over Chinese after eat, and starts with I more often than she. The model has no idea what any of these words mean. It ranked them correctly anyway, from counts alone.

The chain rule survives; the assumption does not

A modern large language model factorises a sentence left to right with the same chain rule — one word at a time, each conditioned on what came before. That part never went away.

What it drops is the Markov assumption. It conditions on thousands of previous words instead of one, because a neural network can handle a long history without needing to have counted it. So the Markov assumption is not a permanent fact about language modelling. It is the compromise you must make when your only tool is counting — and most of the progress in this field has been about removing it.

Think about this before reading on

Using the table above, work out the bigram probability of I want Indian food. The bigrams you need are <s> I, I want, want Indian, Indian food, and food </s> (which is 4/4).

3/4 × 3/3 × 1/3 × 3/3 × 4/4 = 1/4 = 0.25

Lower than I want to eat Indian food (0.333), because want is followed by Indian in only 1 of 3 cases but by to in 2 of 3.

Worth noticing: this sentence is shorter and still scores lower — only because so many probabilities in this toy corpus are exactly 1.0, which costs the longer sentence nothing. In real data every extra word multiplies in another probability well below 1, so longer sentences score lower almost automatically. That makes raw probability a poor way to compare sentences of different lengths — a problem session 14 solves.

Then it breaks

Score one more sentence — a perfectly reasonable English sentence:

P(I want to eat Indian pizza)
  ≈ 3/4 × 3/3 × 2/3 × 3/3 × 2/3 × P(pizza | Indian) × …

  count(Indian pizza) = 0    →    P(pizza | Indian) = 0/3 = 0

  ⇒  P(I want to eat Indian pizza) = 0

Not "unlikely". Zero. One unseen pair anywhere in the sentence multiplies the whole thing to nothing, no matter how ordinary the other six words were. And the model is claiming something absurd — that this sentence is impossible, when all that happened is that four sentences of training text did not happen to contain it.

Any real corpus has this problem badly. Most bigrams that could occur never do, so most sentences you test will contain at least one unseen pair and score exactly zero. A model that says "impossible" about most valid sentences is not usable.

Question 1 of 2

Why do we make the Markov assumption when it is clearly false?

Correct answer: long histories are too rare to count, and short ones are not.

The chain rule is exact but needs counts of long word sequences, which almost never repeat — often the denominator is 1, or 0 and undefined. Cutting the history to one or two words gives up real information in exchange for counts big enough to trust. It is a trade, made knowingly.

Question 2 of 2

In the corpus, eat occurs 3 times and eat Indian twice. What is P(Indian | eat)?

Correct answer: 2/3, because you divide the pair count by the word count.

The maximum likelihood estimate is count(w₁ w₂) / count(w₁) — of the 3 times eat appeared, 2 were followed by Indian. The denominator is always the count of the history word alone, never the corpus size.

The one thing to remember

The whole lesson in two sentences

The chain rule gives the exact probability of a sentence but needs counts of long word sequences that almost never repeat, so we make the Markov assumption — knowingly false — and condition on just the last word or two, which we can count. Each probability is then a plain division, count of the pair over count of the first word, and multiplying them through scores a whole sentence.

Except that it scores far too many good sentences at exactly zero. Every unseen pair kills a sentence outright, and in real data unseen pairs are everywhere. Next session is entirely about that: how to take probability away from what we did see and hand it to what we did not.
